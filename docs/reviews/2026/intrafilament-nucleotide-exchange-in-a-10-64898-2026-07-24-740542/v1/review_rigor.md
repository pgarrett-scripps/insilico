# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This is a careful experimental study of MreB polymerization dynamics with novel findings about intrafilament nucleotide exchange. The core claims are well-supported by multiple complementary methods (TIRF, HS-AFM, biochemistry, modeling, in vivo). However, the headline claim about nucleotide exchange within filaments rests on FRAP recovery of a fluorescent ATP analog under conditions where the interpretation is not airtight, and the causal language about what 'tunes' filament stability conflates correlation with mechanism. The work merits publication but requires qualification of the strongest claims and clarification of the exchange mechanism.

## Strengths
- The authors use multiple independent methods (TIRF, HS-AFM, fluorescence anisotropy, liposome binding, ATPase assays, FRAP, in vivo imaging) to converge on the same conclusions, reducing the risk that any single technical artifact drives the result.
- The Monte Carlo model is grounded in experimentally measured kinetic parameters and accurately reproduces the observed polymerization and depolymerization dynamics, including the differential behavior of ATPase mutants, providing quantitative support for the proposed mechanism.
- The in vivo validation (CCCP experiment showing E136A stability when ATP is depleted) directly tests a key prediction of the model and strengthens the biological relevance of the in vitro findings.

## Weaknesses
- **Nucleotide exchange mechanism and evidence.** The central claim is that 'continuous nucleotide exchange within filaments tunes their stability' (Abstract). The primary evidence is FRAP recovery of ATP* fluorescence (Fig. 3F), which shows ~50% recovery in ~2 min for WT but not E136A. However, the authors acknowledge that TFLime-labeled filaments show no recovery because 'high-intensity laser illumination used for photobleaching induces irreversible photochemical trapping' (line 251). This raises a critical ambiguity: ATP* recovery could reflect either (i) nucleotide dissociation and rebinding from solution, or (ii) lateral diffusion of ATP*-labeled subunits within the filament, or (iii) exchange of ATP* between neighboring subunits without full dissociation. The authors do not experimentally distinguish these mechanisms. The claim that exchange 'tunes stability' is further weakened by the fact that the model predicts the same filament behaviors (polymerization, ADP-induced depolymerization, E136A stability) without requiring any intrafilament exchange at all — exchange is invoked post-hoc to explain the FRAP data, not independently predicted. A direct measurement of nucleotide off-rates from intact filaments (e.g., by stopped-flow kinetics or single-molecule tracking) would be needed to establish that nucleotides actually dissociate from filament subunits rather than merely diffusing laterally or exchanging with neighbors.
- **Causal language about 'tuning' stability.** The Abstract states that 'continuous nucleotide exchange within filaments tunes their stability on the membrane.' The Results show that (i) WT filaments exchange ATP* with solution (Fig. 3F), (ii) the rate of exchange is faster than the rate of Pi release (line 283), and (iii) in simulations, filaments loaded with ADP disassemble faster than those with ATP (Fig. 4I). However, 'tuning' implies active regulation — that exchange causally controls stability — whereas the data show only that the nucleotide state (ATP vs ADP) affects stability. Exchange is a passive consequence of the filament structure, not a regulatory mechanism. The model shows that even without exchange, the ATP/ADP ratio in the free pool would control filament fate (Fig. S10). The distinction matters: the title and abstract claim a novel regulatory mechanism, but the evidence supports only that nucleotide composition (which happens to be maintained by exchange) correlates with stability. Reframing as 'nucleotide exchange maintains ATP-bound state, which stabilizes filaments' would be more precise.
- **Generalization of the 'new class' claim.** The Abstract and Discussion claim that 'nucleotide cycling within MreB filaments defines a new class of biological polymer behavior' (Abstract) and 'a new class of self-organizing biological filament dynamics' (line 367). This is presented as a fundamental distinction from actin. However, the evidence is limited to B. subtilis MreB under in vitro conditions on a supported lipid bilayer. The authors note that Geobacillus MreB shows different Pi release kinetics (line 10 of Discussion), and ParM (another prokaryotic actin homolog) shows ADP-induced disassembly (line 313), suggesting heterogeneity among prokaryotic actins. The claim would be stronger if restricted to 'MreB' rather than 'prokaryotic actin homologs' or if the authors had tested MreB from another species. As written, the claim overgeneralizes from one organism.
- The claim that ATP hydrolysis is 'not required for polymerization' (section heading, line 222) is supported, but the phrasing risks confusion: hydrolysis is not required for the initial polymerization event, but the model shows it is required for the eventual depolymerization and turnover that stabilize filaments in vivo (Fig. S10). A qualifier such as 'not required for the initial polymerization event' would be clearer.
- The critical concentration is reported as 'negligible (0.003 µM)' (Table 1, line 476) based on a linear fit at low concentrations, but the plateau in Fig. 2E suggests the true Cc may be higher; the authors should clarify whether 0.003 µM is the Cc for ATP-MreB or an artifact of the 2D diffusion model.
- The claim that filaments 'remain stable through TIRF time courses (up to several hours), with no detectable depolymerization' (line 246) is not quantified; a figure showing filament length over hours would strengthen this claim and rule out slow, steady depolymerization.
- The model assumes 'identical dynamics at both filament ends' (Fig. 4D legend), but Fig. 2G shows occasional weak polarity; the impact of this assumption on the simulations is not discussed.

## Questions
- Does the ATP* FRAP recovery (Fig. 3F) represent nucleotide dissociation from the filament, or could it reflect lateral diffusion of ATP*-labeled subunits or exchange between adjacent subunits without full release into solution?
- In the model, what is the sensitivity of the predicted filament stability to the intrafilament exchange rate (k+,n and k-,n)? If exchange rates were 10-fold slower, would the polymerization and depolymerization kinetics still match the data?
- Why do WT and D158A filaments show such different nucleotide affinities (Fig. 3B) when both are expected to have the same nucleotide-binding pocket? Is this a direct effect of the mutation on binding, or an indirect effect on protein folding?
- The in vivo CCCP experiment (Fig. 4J) shows E136A remains localized while WT delocalizes, but does not directly measure ATP/ADP ratios in the cell; could the difference reflect other ATP-dependent processes (e.g., membrane potential, protein synthesis) rather than MreB nucleotide state?
