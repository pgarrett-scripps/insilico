# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents compelling evidence for intrafilament nucleotide exchange in MreB filaments, a novel finding supported by multiple complementary techniques. However, the design has a critical gap: the claim that nucleotide exchange (rather than end-capping or other mechanisms) drives filament stability is not directly tested, and alternative explanations for the observed ADP-induced depolymerization are not ruled out. The Monte-Carlo model is valuable but does not constitute independent experimental evidence for the proposed mechanism. The work merits publication with major revisions clarifying what the data actually demonstrate about the mechanism of stability regulation.

## Strengths
- The study employs multiple orthogonal techniques (TIRF, HS-AFM, fluorescence anisotropy, FRAP, in vivo imaging) that converge on the same phenomenon, substantially strengthening confidence in the core observations.
- The ATPase mutants (E136A, D158A) are well-chosen tools that reveal mechanistic distinctions: their differential behavior under ATP depletion and ADP exposure provides strong evidence that nucleotide state, not just hydrolysis, controls filament stability.
- The capping experiment (Fig. 4G–H) directly tests whether end-capping alone explains ADP-induced depolymerization, and the finding that E136A-capped WT filaments still fragment under ADP is a genuine advance over the prior hypothesis.

## Weaknesses
- **Nucleotide exchange vs. alternative mechanisms for stability regulation.** The central claim is that "intrafilament nucleotide exchange tunes filament stability" (Abstract, Main Text). The evidence for exchange itself is strong: ATP* FRAP recovery (Fig. 3F) and homogeneous ATP* incorporation along filament length (Fig. 3H) directly show nucleotide movement. However, the causal link between exchange and stability is inferred, not demonstrated. When WT filaments are exposed to ADP, they depolymerize rapidly (Fig. 4A–B). The authors attribute this to ADP-loading via exchange, but the design does not exclude an alternative: ADP could directly destabilize subunits at filament ends without requiring exchange throughout the filament. The capping experiment (Fig. 4G–H) shows that WT cores fragment even when capped, which argues against simple end-capping, but it does not prove that interior nucleotide exchange is the mechanism. A nucleotide-free interior with ADP-bound ends could produce the same fragmentation pattern. To distinguish these, one would need to show that preventing exchange (e.g., by a mutation that blocks nucleotide dissociation from interior subunits) prevents ADP-induced destabilization, or that the rate of depolymerization correlates with the rate of exchange. The model predicts this correlation but does not test it experimentally. As written, the paper demonstrates that nucleotide exchange occurs and that filament stability depends on nucleotide identity, but the causal role of exchange in stability is not isolated from the role of nucleotide state at filament ends.
- **Interpretation of the Monte-Carlo model as mechanistic evidence.** The model (Fig. 4D, S10) is presented as support for the nucleotide-exchange mechanism ("Simulating a buffer exchange to MreB-free and nucleotide-free buffer mirrors the distinct behaviors observed experimentally"). However, a model that reproduces observations does not prove the mechanism is correct—it shows the mechanism is *consistent* with the data. The model incorporates intrafilament exchange as a parameter and shows that including it produces the observed kinetics. But the same kinetics could arise from other parameter sets or mechanisms not explored. The model is valuable for hypothesis generation and for showing internal consistency, but it should not be presented as independent experimental validation of the exchange mechanism. The paper would be stronger if it acknowledged this limitation explicitly.
- **Incomplete characterization of the nucleotide-exchange rate and its functional significance.** Figure 3F shows FRAP recovery of ATP* in WT filaments, but the recovery kinetics are not quantified in detail (no half-time, mobile fraction, or diffusion coefficient reported for the filament interior). The initial slope in Fig. 3I is given for incorporation of pure ATP*, but the exchange rate in the presence of competing ATP (the physiological condition) is not directly measured. The model uses $k_{+,n} = 0.28 \mu M^{-1}.s^{-1}$ and $k_{-,n} = 0.0141 s^{-1}$, but these are not independently validated experimentally. Without quantitative kinetics, it is difficult to assess whether the exchange rate is fast enough to be physiologically relevant or whether it is a minor side reaction.
- **The in vivo experiment (Fig. 4J) does not directly test the nucleotide-exchange hypothesis.** The CCCP-induced delocalization of WT MreB but not E136A is consistent with the model (ATP depletion → loss of ATP-bound stabilization), but it does not distinguish between stabilization by ATP-bound subunits at filament ends versus stabilization by ATP-bound subunits throughout the filament via exchange. Both mechanisms predict the same in vivo outcome. A more direct test would be to measure ATP/ADP ratios in cells and correlate them with MreB localization, or to use a nucleotide-exchange-deficient mutant (if one could be designed) and show it behaves differently from WT in vivo.
- The claim that MreB filaments display "non-polar growth" (Abstract, Main Text) rests on the photobleaching experiment (Fig. 2G), which shows comparable elongation rates at both ends, but the authors acknowledge that "weak apparent polarity" is observed and attribute it to crowding and stochastic noise—a post-hoc explanation that weakens the conclusion that growth is truly symmetrical rather than weakly polar.
- The liposome co-sedimentation assay (Fig. 1E–F) shows that CL promotes MreB binding, but the assay does not distinguish between direct binding to CL and indirect effects (e.g., CL-induced changes in membrane fluidity or protein conformation); the QCM-D data (Fig. 1H) provide complementary evidence but are limited to one lipid composition.
- The critical concentration is estimated from TIRF elongation rates at low MreB concentrations (Fig. 2E), but the plateau in elongation rate above 0.06–0.09 µM is attributed to 2D diffusion limitation without direct measurement of diffusion coefficients or membrane-bound monomer concentrations to validate this interpretation.
- The HS-AFM elongation rate (14.4 ± 10.16 nm·s⁻¹, n=7) is described as a "first estimate" due to tracking difficulties, and the authors note that only a limited number of filaments could be tracked; this small sample size and acknowledged technical limitation reduce confidence in this quantitative result, though the TIRF data provide a more robust measure.
