# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents a well-executed study of MreB polymerization dynamics with two solid contributions: demonstrating that ATP hydrolysis is not required for polymerization (supported by ATPase-deficient mutants), and showing that filament stability is tuned by nucleotide exchange. The claimed novelty—intrafilament nucleotide cycling in biological polymers—survives a preprint search and is supported by FRAP recovery of ATP* and in vivo CCCP experiments. However, the FRAP evidence for exchange is consistent with but does not definitively prove intrafilament nucleotide exchange; lateral diffusion of labeled subunits or nucleotide rebinding are not fully excluded. The work merits publication but the central mechanistic claim requires more direct evidence.

## Strengths
- The use of ATPase-deficient mutants (E136A, D158A) with altered nucleotide binding properties provides strong evidence that ATP hydrolysis is mechanistically uncoupled from polymerization, and the in vivo CCCP experiment validating ATP-dependent filament localization is a valuable orthogonal test.
- Comprehensive experimental approach combining HS-AFM, TIRF microscopy, fluorescence anisotropy, and Monte Carlo modeling provides multiple lines of evidence for the proposed mechanism, and the authors honestly report limitations (e.g., HS-AFM tracking difficulties, n=7).
- The capping experiment (Fig. 4G-H) elegantly tests whether ADP-induced disassembly requires an ATP cap or operates through filament-interior mechanisms, and the finding that E136A caps stabilize WT cores argues against a simple end-capping model.

## Weaknesses
- **Intrafilament nucleotide exchange mechanism not definitively established.** The central novelty claim rests on FRAP recovery of ATP*-labeled filaments (Fig. 3F), interpreted as nucleotide exchange within subunits. However, the experimental design does not cleanly isolate this from alternative mechanisms: (a) lateral diffusion of ATP*-labeled subunits into the bleached region, (b) rebinding of ATP* from solution to the same subunits after dissociation, or (c) exchange of entire subunits. The authors acknowledge that ATP* has different photochemical properties than TFLime (lines 250–252) but do not present a direct measurement distinguishing intrafilament nucleotide exchange from these alternatives. The capping experiment (Fig. 4G-H) shows ADP-induced fragmentation but is consistent with nucleotide-dependent stability without proving the exchange mechanism. A direct measurement—such as tracking the fate of a single nucleotide within a filament using a non-exchangeable analog, or measuring nucleotide occupancy by mass spectrometry—would resolve this.
- Nucleotide binding affinity differences in ATPase mutants confound interpretation of polymerization data. E136A has higher affinity for both ATP and ADP; D158A has reduced ATP affinity and undetectable ADP binding (Fig. 3B). The polymerization of these mutants could result from their altered binding kinetics rather than proving hydrolysis is truly dispensable. The ADP-AlF₄⁻ result (Fig. S6A-C) more directly supports the claim, but the mutant data alone do not fully separate nucleotide-binding effects from hydrolysis effects.
- HS-AFM elongation rate estimate is based on only 7 filament segments due to high lateral mobility, limiting quantitative confidence in the 14.4 ± 10.16 nm/s rate and its comparison to TIRF-derived rates.
- Monte Carlo model parameters include inferred rates (nucleotide exchange kinetics from FRAP recovery) that rest on the interpretation of ATP* recovery as exchange; the model is consistent with the data but is not independent evidence for the proposed mechanism.
- Cardiolipin requirement claimed as 'major determinant' of MreB membrane association in vivo (line 133) based on its proportion in lipid extracts, but direct in vivo evidence of necessity is absent.
- CCCP-induced delocalization experiment (Fig. 4J) is consistent with ATP-dependent filament stability but does not exclude indirect effects of CCCP on MreB localization through other cellular pathways.
- Generalizability unclear: Geobacillus MreB shows markedly different Pi release kinetics (reference 10), raising questions about whether the nucleotide exchange mechanism is conserved across MreB orthologs.
- The claim of 'first report' of nucleotide cycling in biological polymers survives the preprint search, but ParM (another prokaryotic actin homolog) is mentioned as showing ADP-induced disassembly via nucleotide exchange at filament ends (line 313); the distinction between end-exchange and intrafilament exchange should be clarified.

## Questions
- Can the authors provide a direct measurement distinguishing intrafilament nucleotide exchange from lateral diffusion of labeled subunits or nucleotide rebinding (e.g., using a non-exchangeable nucleotide analog or mass spectrometry)?
- How do the altered nucleotide binding affinities of E136A and D158A (Fig. 3B) affect the interpretation that ATP hydrolysis is dispensable for polymerization, rather than that altered binding kinetics compensate?
- Is the nucleotide exchange mechanism conserved in Geobacillus MreB, given its different Pi release kinetics, and does this affect the generality of the proposed 'new class' of polymer behavior?
