# Clarity & Presentation Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript is well-written overall with clear experimental design and mostly transparent reporting. The central claims about intrafilament nucleotide exchange in MreB are supported by multiple complementary methods (TIRF, HS-AFM, biochemical assays, modeling, and in vivo imaging). Presentation is generally strong, though a few specific passages create unnecessary friction or ambiguity that should be resolved in revision.

## Strengths
- The paper uses consistent, well-defined terminology throughout (e.g., 'filament' explicitly defined as one doublet; nucleotide states clearly distinguished) and defines all non-standard abbreviations at first use.
- Figures are well-captioned with axes labeled in units, symbols defined, and every panel referenced in text; the narrative flow from motivation through methods to results to interpretation is clear and logical.
- The authors transparently report limitations (e.g., HS-AFM tracking difficulties acknowledged; FRAP recovery interpretation explained; stochastic fluctuations noted as source of apparent polarity) and distinguish what they can and cannot conclude.

## Weaknesses
- The central claim that 'nucleotide exchange within filaments tunes their stability' rests on the ADP-induced depolymerization experiment (Fig. 4A–C), but an alternative explanation is not excluded: rapid ADP loading could simply reflect a change in nucleotide affinity under the new buffer conditions rather than exchange occurring within intact filaments. The FRAP experiment with ATP* (Fig. 3F) shows recovery, but recovery of a fluorescent nucleotide analog does not prove that the same exchange mechanism operates under physiological ATP/ADP ratios or that it is the primary stability-tuning mechanism in vivo. The capping experiment (Fig. 4G–H) is suggestive but involves E136A (an ATPase mutant), not WT filaments, so it does not directly test whether intrafilament exchange in WT filaments is necessary for the observed ADP-induced destabilization. A direct measurement of nucleotide composition within filaments (e.g., by mass spectrometry or NMR) would definitively establish that exchange occurs and quantify its rate relative to hydrolysis.
- The critical concentration (Cc) is reported as 0.003 µM (Table 1, Fig. 2E), but the text states 'At 0.05 µM MreB, isolated short filaments appeared within one minute' (line 147–148), which is 17-fold above Cc; this makes it unclear whether the plateau in elongation rate (Fig. 2E) reflects a genuine saturation of membrane-binding sites or a concentration-dependent artifact of the experimental setup, and the authors do not discuss whether the Cc value is reliable given the membrane-templated polymerization mechanism.
- The statement 'nucleotide exchange within MreB filaments defines a new class of biological polymer behavior' (Abstract, line 8–9) is presented as a novel finding, but the manuscript does not explicitly compare the rate or mechanism of intrafilament exchange in MreB to any other polymer system (actin, tubulin, ParM) to justify the claim of novelty; the comparison to actin is qualitative ('no evidence of rapid nucleotide exchange') rather than quantitative.

## Questions
- Figure 3F (FRAP with ATP*): what is the half-time of recovery and the mobile fraction, and how do these compare to the rate of intrafilament nucleotide exchange predicted by the model (kex = 0.014 s⁻¹)?
- In the Monte Carlo model (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Information), what is the sensitivity of the predicted filament stability to the assumed rate of intrafilament nucleotide exchange (k+,n and k−,n)—i.e., if exchange were 10-fold slower or faster, would the model still fit the experimental data?
- The authors state that 'MreB, which was purified in a nucleotide-free state, is partially unfolded in the absence of nucleotide' (line 103–104) as an explanation for slow ATP binding kinetics; is there direct evidence (e.g., circular dichroism, size-exclusion chromatography) that nucleotide-free MreB is indeed partially unfolded?
