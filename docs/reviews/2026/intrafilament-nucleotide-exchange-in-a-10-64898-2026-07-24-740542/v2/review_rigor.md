# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This is a rigorous experimental study with strong technical execution and novel findings about nucleotide exchange within MreB filaments. The core claim—that MreB filaments undergo continuous intrafilament nucleotide exchange, unlike canonical actin—is well-supported by multiple independent lines of evidence (FRAP with ATP*, capping experiments, ADP-induced disassembly, ATPase mutants). However, the mechanistic interpretation of *why* this exchange occurs relies on conformational arguments that are not directly tested, and the generalizability of findings to other MreB species and in vivo conditions remains uncertain. The work merits publication with clarification of mechanism claims.

## Strengths
- The nucleotide exchange phenomenon is demonstrated through multiple orthogonal approaches (ATP* FRAP recovery, capping with E136A, ADP-induced disassembly kinetics, and ATPase mutant behavior), making the core observation robust.
- The experimental design isolates nucleotide exchange from depolymerization by comparing WT, E136A, and D158A mutants with different ATPase activities and nucleotide affinities, allowing mechanistic dissection.
- The Monte Carlo model integrates all measured kinetic parameters and successfully predicts the distinct behaviors of mutants under different conditions, providing quantitative support for the proposed mechanism.

## Weaknesses
- **Mechanism of nucleotide exchange is inferred, not demonstrated.** The authors propose that membrane anchoring reduces twist angle and opens the nucleotide-binding pocket to permit exchange (lines 268–283), but this is a conformational hypothesis unsupported by structural data or direct conformational measurements. The evidence shows that exchange *occurs* and that it depends on ATPase activity (E136A shows no recovery in Fig. 3F), but not *why* the pocket opens. The alternative—that the membrane-bound state simply has inherently higher nucleotide off-rates regardless of pocket geometry—is not excluded. This is SOFT overclaiming of mechanism: the claim 'membrane anchoring was not or only poorly considered in these studies' (line 269) is true, but the authors then assert a specific conformational model without testing it. Resolve by: (1) stating the mechanism as 'consistent with' rather than asserting it, or (2) measuring nucleotide dissociation rates from membrane-bound vs. soluble MreB directly, or (3) performing molecular dynamics on membrane-anchored MreB to test the twist-angle hypothesis.
- **Generalization to B. subtilis in vivo is supported only by one indirect experiment.** The CCCP delocalization experiment (Fig. 4J, lines 338–357) shows that E136A-MreB remains membrane-localized when ATP is depleted, consistent with the model. However, this is a single condition in a single strain, and the authors do not measure nucleotide composition in vivo, ATP/ADP ratios, or whether the predicted nucleotide exchange actually occurs in living cells. The claim 'in growing cells, high ATP levels maintain MreB filaments on the membrane through intrafilament nucleotide exchange' (lines 350–351) is supported by the CCCP result but not by direct evidence of exchange occurring in vivo. The in vivo section reads as extrapolation from in vitro findings rather than direct validation. Resolve by: measuring intracellular ATP/ADP ratios and correlating them with MreB stability, or using fluorescent nucleotide analogs in live cells to detect exchange.
- **Nucleotide binding kinetics are anomalously slow and poorly explained.** The ATP and ADP binding rates to soluble MreB are ~300-fold slower than actin (line 100), and the authors propose that MreB is 'partially unfolded in the absence of nucleotide' and requires conformational rearrangement (lines 101–103). This explanation is plausible but not tested: no evidence of unfolding is presented (no circular dichroism, thermal stability, or hydrodynamic measurements). The A260/A280 ratio (0.62) indicates little bound nucleotide but does not address protein folding. This matters because the slow binding kinetics are unusual and central to understanding MreB assembly dynamics. Resolve by: measuring the hydrodynamic radius or secondary structure of nucleotide-free MreB, or performing stopped-flow kinetics to test whether a conformational step limits binding.
- The claim that nucleotide exchange 'tunes filament stability' (abstract, line 42) is supported for the WT case but the D158A mutant shows that low nucleotide affinity (not exchange per se) drives rapid disassembly, leaving unclear whether exchange itself is the tuning mechanism or merely a consequence of the nucleotide-binding properties.
- Figure 3H shows homogeneous ATP* binding along WT and D158A filaments but not E136A; the authors interpret this as evidence that E136A cannot exchange nucleotides, but the figure also shows E136A filaments are not visible under standard labeling conditions (line 261), making the comparison indirect.
- The model assumes 'identical dynamics at both filament-ends' (Fig. 4D legend), but the photobleaching experiment (Fig. 2G) shows occasional asymmetry in elongation rates; the impact of this assumption on predictions is not discussed.
- The critical concentration derived from TIRF (0.003 µM, line 177) is much lower than the 1–2 µM used in most experiments; the authors do not discuss whether the plateau in elongation rate (Fig. 2E) reflects a real biological saturation or an artifact of the labeling strategy at higher concentrations.

## Questions
- What is the nucleotide dissociation rate from membrane-bound MreB subunits, and does it differ from soluble MreB?
- Can you measure the nucleotide composition of MreB filaments in living B. subtilis cells, and does it match the predicted ATP/ADP ratio from the model?
- Does the secondary structure or hydrodynamic radius of nucleotide-free MreB differ from ATP-bound MreB, supporting the proposed unfolding hypothesis?
