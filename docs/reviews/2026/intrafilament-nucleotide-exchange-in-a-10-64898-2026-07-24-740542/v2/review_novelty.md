# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents solid contributions to understanding MreB filament dynamics, particularly the role of nucleotide exchange in regulating filament stability. The multi-method approach (TIRF, HS-AFM, fluorescence anisotropy, ATPase assays, FRAP, in vivo imaging) provides converging evidence, and the capping experiments elegantly test the intrafilament mechanism. However, the novelty claims require recalibration: the claim of being the "first report of nucleotide cycling within a biological polymer" overstates the contribution relative to precedent in ParM filaments, where nucleotide exchange was previously proposed. The mechanistic claim that hydrolysis occurs within filaments rather than at ends relies on inference and model fitting rather than direct measurement. The FRAP evidence for nucleotide exchange (ATP* recovery) does not fully exclude alternative explanations involving filament end dynamics. These are refinements to framing and interpretation rather than fundamental flaws, and the work merits publication with clarification of novelty positioning and resolution of mechanistic ambiguities.

## Strengths
- Multi-method approach (TIRF, HS-AFM, fluorescence anisotropy, ATPase assays, FRAP, in vivo imaging) provides converging evidence for nucleotide exchange and its functional role in filament stability.
- The capping experiment (Fig. 4G–H) is elegant and directly tests whether ADP-induced disassembly depends on nucleotide exchange within the filament body rather than at ends, providing strong support for the intrafilament mechanism.
- Quantitative Monte-Carlo model integrates kinetic parameters and makes testable predictions (e.g., Fig. 4I on filament fate under different nucleotide conditions), with predictions validated against experimental observations.
- The in vivo validation (Fig. 4J) showing that E136A MreB remains membrane-bound during ATP depletion while WT delocalizes provides functional support for the nucleotide-dependent stability model.
- The finding that ATP hydrolysis is not required for polymerization (supported by E136A and D158A mutants polymerizing at WT rates) is well-demonstrated and mechanistically significant.

## Weaknesses
- The claim that this is the "first report of nucleotide cycling within a biological polymer" is overstated. The manuscript cites ParM filaments where free ADP increases disassembly rate "proposed to result from nucleotide exchange at filament ends" (line 313, ref. 29). While the authors distinguish their finding as intrafilament rather than end-localized, nucleotide exchange in actin-like filaments is not unprecedented; the novelty is the mechanism and rate of intrafilament exchange in MreB, not the phenomenon itself.
- The FRAP evidence for nucleotide exchange (Fig. 3F, ATP* recovery) does not exclude the alternative that ATP* recovery reflects preferential loss and rebinding of ATP*-labeled subunits at filament ends rather than exchange within intact subunits. The authors attribute lack of TFLime recovery (Fig. 3E) to photochemical trapping rather than nucleotide immobility, but this interpretation is plausible but not independently verified.
- The claim that ATP hydrolysis occurs within filaments (rather than at ends) is inferred from slow Pi release and filament stability, not directly measured. The model fits the data but does not prove the location of hydrolysis; direct measurement of Pi release from preformed filaments blocked from polymerizing would be needed.
- The relationship to concurrent work (de San Eustaquio-Campillo et al., 2026, PPR, cited as "in preparation" for G. stearothermophilus MreB mutants) should be clarified to establish priority and avoid duplication.
- The explanation for the plateau in elongation rate (Fig. 2E)—attributed to 2D diffusion on the membrane—is qualitative; lateral diffusion coefficients for membrane-bound MreB are not measured, leaving the explanation unverified.
- The model contains many fitted parameters (Table 1); sensitivity analysis or parameter uncertainty is not discussed, limiting confidence in quantitative predictions.
- The in vivo CCCP experiment (Fig. 4J) shows delocalization of WT MreB but does not characterize the timescale or distinguish between disassembly, depolymerization, and loss of membrane binding.
- The comparison of nucleotide exchange rate to Pi release rate (line 330) uses parameters with different units and is not made explicit in the text, making the claim difficult to verify.

## Questions
- Does ATP* recovery in the FRAP experiment (Fig. 3F) occur in E136A filaments, which do not exchange nucleotides (Fig. 3H)? If not, this would directly support the exchange mechanism and exclude end-dynamics alternatives.
- What is the lateral diffusion coefficient of membrane-bound MreB, and does it match the 2D-diffusion explanation for the plateau in elongation rate (Fig. 2E)?
- Can Pi release be measured from preformed filaments in the absence of polymerization (e.g., by blocking monomer addition with a high-affinity cap) to directly test whether hydrolysis occurs within filaments or at ends?
- How does the timescale of MreB delocalization upon CCCP treatment (Fig. 4J) compare to the predicted filament disassembly rate, and is delocalization reversible upon ATP restoration?
- What is the relationship and priority between this manuscript and the concurrent work by de San Eustaquio-Campillo et al. (2026, PPR) on MreB polymerization cycles?
