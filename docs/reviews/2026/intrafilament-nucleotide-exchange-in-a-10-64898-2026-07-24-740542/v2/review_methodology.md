# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The experimental design is sound and well-controlled for the core claims about intrafilament nucleotide exchange in MreB filaments. The authors use multiple orthogonal methods (TIRF, HS-AFM, FRAP, fluorescence anisotropy) to establish their main findings, include appropriate negative and positive controls, and test predictions with ATPase mutants. The Monte Carlo model provides quantitative support for the mechanistic interpretation. However, the design does not fully isolate the mechanism driving ADP-induced depolymerization, and some claims about nucleotide exchange rates rest on indirect evidence that could be strengthened.

## Strengths
- The use of ATPase-deficient mutants (E136A, D158A) as internal controls effectively isolates ATP hydrolysis from polymerization and tests predictions about nucleotide-dependent stability, with each mutant showing distinct behavior consistent with its nucleotide-binding properties.
- Multiple independent methods (TIRF microscopy, HS-AFM, FRAP with different fluorophores, fluorescence anisotropy) converge on the same conclusions about bidirectional growth and nucleotide exchange, reducing the risk that a single technical artifact drives the findings.
- The capping experiment (Fig. 4G-H) elegantly tests whether ADP-induced depolymerization requires nucleotide exchange within filaments by physically separating ATP-bound and ADP-responsive regions, and the result that only the WT section breaks supports the exchange mechanism over a simple ATP-cap model.

## Weaknesses
- The claim that 'nucleotide exchange within filaments tunes their stability' rests primarily on FRAP recovery of ATP* (Fig. 3F) and the observation that WT filaments depolymerize faster in ADP than in buffer alone (Fig. 4A-B). However, the design does not exclude an alternative: ADP could destabilize filaments by direct binding to subunits without requiring exchange—i.e., ADP-bound subunits may simply dissociate faster than ATP-bound ones, regardless of whether nucleotide cycling occurs. The ATP* FRAP recovery shows nucleotide can exchange, but does not prove that exchange is the mechanism by which ADP destabilizes filaments. To distinguish these, the authors would need to measure the nucleotide state of filaments during depolymerization (e.g., by directly quantifying ADP incorporation into filaments during the ADP-flush experiment) or show that blocking exchange (if possible) prevents ADP-induced destabilization. The current evidence is consistent with exchange but does not prove it is the operative mechanism.
- The quantification of intrafilament nucleotide exchange rates relies on ATP* FRAP recovery (Fig. 3F, 3I) and the kinetics of ATP* incorporation into preformed filaments (Fig. 3H-I). However, ATP* is a non-hydrolysable analogue with lower polymerization efficiency than ATP (Fig. S5), and the fluorophore may alter binding kinetics or accessibility. The authors show ATP* binds competitively (Fig. S2C), but do not directly measure whether ATP* exchange kinetics match those of native ATP. The Monte Carlo model uses exchange rates inferred from these ATP* experiments, so if ATP* kinetics differ substantially from ATP, the model's predictions about WT filament behavior under physiological nucleotide pools may not hold. Measuring exchange with native ATP (e.g., by pulse-chase with radioactive ATP) would strengthen this claim.
- The in vivo experiment (Fig. 4J) shows that E136A filaments remain stable when ATP is depleted by CCCP, consistent with the model, but the design does not rule out that E136A's stability results from altered membrane binding or protein-protein interactions rather than ATP hydrolysis per se. The mutant has higher ATP affinity (Fig. 3B), which could independently stabilize filaments on the membrane. A more direct test would be to measure whether E136A filaments remain stable in the presence of ADP (which should destabilize WT filaments) in vivo, isolating the effect of nucleotide state from other properties of the mutant.

## Questions
- Figure 3F (FRAP of ATP*-labeled filaments): what is the mobile fraction and half-recovery time, and how do these compare quantitatively to the ATP* incorporation rates measured in Fig. 3H-I to confirm they reflect the same exchange process?
- In the Monte Carlo model, how sensitive are the predictions in Fig. 4I (filament stability under different nucleotide pools) to the inferred exchange rate constant k_{+,n} and k_{-,n}? Does a 2-fold or 5-fold change in these rates alter the qualitative predictions?
- The ADP-flush experiment (Fig. 4A-B) shows rapid depolymerization, but does the filament length decrease linearly or accelerate over time, and does this match the model's prediction of a transition from k_{-,t} to k_{-,d} as ATP is depleted?
- In Fig. 3H, why are D158A filaments not visible under standard ATP* labeling (17:1 ATP:ATP*) but become visible when exposed to pure ATP*? Does this indicate that D158A has lower affinity for ATP* specifically, or that the low ATP affinity of D158A prevents filament formation under the mixed nucleotide condition?
