# Statistics & Data-Analysis Reviewer

## Summary
This is a well-executed biophysical study with sound quantitative methods overall. The core claims about MreB polymerization kinetics, bidirectional elongation, and nucleotide exchange are supported by appropriate experiments and statistical reporting. The Monte Carlo model is well-parameterized and its predictions align with observations. However, the study conflates two distinct mechanisms (intrafilament nucleotide exchange vs. end-capping) in its interpretation of filament stability, and some key kinetic parameters rest on limited sample sizes or transient tracking conditions that warrant explicit discussion of their reliability.

## Strengths
- Polymerization kinetics are quantified across multiple independent concentrations with clear reporting of n values, linear fits, and derived rate constants (k₊,ₜ, k₋,ₜ, Cₜ) that enable comparison to actin literature.
- The dual-color TIRF experiments (Fig. 2F–G) and photobleaching assay directly demonstrate bidirectional elongation with explicit n values (n=13 for photobleaching) and a principled explanation for apparent asymmetry from stochastic fluctuations.
- The Monte Carlo model transparently specifies all rate constants (Fig. 4D, Table 1), validates against polymerization kinetics (Fig. S8), and correctly predicts the distinct behaviors of WT and mutant filaments under buffer exchange and ADP exposure.

## Weaknesses
- **Mechanism of filament stability conflation**: The authors propose that intrafilament nucleotide exchange tunes filament stability, but the evidence does not cleanly separate this from end-capping by ATP/ADP-Pi-bound subunits. In Fig. 4, WT filaments disassemble slowly after buffer wash (−1.03 ± 0.54 nm·s⁻¹) and rapidly when ADP is added (−70.34 ± 29.89 nm·s⁻¹). The model predicts both behaviors by invoking nucleotide exchange, but an alternative explanation is that end-capping by ATP-bound subunits (present initially) stabilizes filaments, and exchange merely reflects the kinetic consequence of losing that cap. The capping experiment (Fig. 4G–H) attempts to address this by showing that E136A-capped WT filaments still fragment in ADP, but this does not isolate the contribution of intrafilament exchange to stability in the WT case. The authors state "filament ends are capped by one or several ATP or ADP-Pi-bound subunits" (line 331–332) but do not quantify cap size, lifetime, or the relative contribution of capping vs. exchange to the observed disassembly kinetics. This ambiguity undermines the claim that exchange is a primary tuning mechanism rather than a secondary consequence of nucleotide dynamics.
- HS-AFM elongation rate (14.4 ± 10.16 nm·s⁻¹, n=7) is derived from filament segments with "transient immobilization or reduced mobility, likely due to transient interactions with the AFM tip" (lines 160–162). This non-random sampling introduces bias: immobilized segments may elongate differently than freely diffusing ones, and the small n and high SD (70% of the mean) make this a weak anchor for the plateau interpretation in TIRF. The authors acknowledge tracking difficulties but do not quantify the bias or sensitivity of the conclusion to this estimate.
- FRAP experiments (Fig. 3E–F) use different interpretations for the same phenomenon: TFLime shows no recovery (attributed to photochemical trapping), while ATP* shows rapid recovery (attributed to nucleotide exchange). The claim that TFLime trapping is irreversible is not independently validated; if TFLime can exchange under some conditions, the absence of recovery in Fig. 3E does not prove monomers are immobile. The authors should either measure TFLime exchange kinetics independently or use ATP* alone to quantify monomer turnover.
- Nucleotide binding kinetics (Table 1, Fig. 1D) are measured on soluble monomers, but polymerization occurs on membranes where local concentration, lipid interactions, and conformational constraints differ. The relevance of the measured Kd values (ATP: 0.50 µM, ADP: 0.56 µM) to the filament state is assumed but not validated; a direct measurement of nucleotide affinity in membrane-bound filaments would strengthen the model.
- The critical concentration (Cₜ ≈ 0.003 µM) is derived from a linear fit at low MreB concentrations (0.01–0.05 µM, Fig. 2E) with no explicit confidence interval or bootstrap resampling reported; the fit quality and sensitivity to the choice of fitting range are not shown.
- ATPase activity (Pi release rate: 0.030 ± 0.001 Pi/min/MreB, Fig. 1G) is measured at a single MreB concentration (0.5 mg/mL liposomes) with n=2 independent experiments; this is insufficient to establish the rate as a robust parameter, especially given the high precision claimed (SD of 0.001).
- Statistical tests for comparisons between WT and mutant filament behaviors (e.g., Fig. 3D, 4B) are not explicitly named; the figure legends state "n = ..." but do not specify whether t-tests, Mann-Whitney U, or other tests were used, or whether assumptions (normality, equal variance) were checked.

## Questions
- In the model (Fig. 4D, Supplementary 'Modeling MreB dynamics'), how sensitive are the predictions in Fig. 4C and Fig. S10 to the choice of rate constants, particularly kₕᵧ𝒹 and k₋,𝒅? A one-way sensitivity analysis would clarify which parameters most strongly drive the predicted disassembly kinetics.
- Figure 3I shows D158A filaments incorporate ATP* ~2× faster than WT (slopes 28.8 vs 12.2 intensity/min). Is this difference statistically significant, and does it reflect a genuine difference in nucleotide exchange kinetics or a consequence of D158A's lower ATP affinity (Fig. 3B) leading to more frequent binding attempts?
- The authors state that MreB monomers are "partially unfolded in the absence of nucleotide" (line 107), explaining slow ATP/ADP binding kinetics. Is this claim supported by circular dichroism, hydrogen-deuterium exchange, or other structural data, or is it an inference from the kinetic data alone?
