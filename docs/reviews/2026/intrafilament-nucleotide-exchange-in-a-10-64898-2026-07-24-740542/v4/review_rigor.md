# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This is a technically sophisticated study of MreB polymerization dynamics with strong experimental work, but the central claim—that intrafilament nucleotide exchange defines a new class of biopolymer behavior—rests on evidence that does not fully isolate this mechanism from simpler alternatives. The FRAP experiments showing ATP* recovery are consistent with nucleotide exchange, but they do not exclude exchange occurring only at filament ends or through transient dissociation-rebinding cycles rather than true intrafilament cycling. The ADP-induced depolymerization phenotype is explained equally well by a model in which ADP simply fails to stabilize filaments, without requiring active exchange. These gaps do not invalidate the work, but they do overstate the evidence for the headline claim.

## Strengths
- The dual-color TIRF and photobleaching experiments demonstrating symmetric, bidirectional filament elongation are well-designed and clearly establish non-polar growth, a genuine advance over prior structural inference.
- The quantitative kinetic measurements (association/dissociation rates, critical concentration, elongation rates) are thorough and the comparison to actin parameters is informative for understanding MreB's membrane-templated assembly.
- The Monte Carlo model integrating polymerization, hydrolysis, Pi release, and nucleotide exchange is a valuable tool for interpreting the complex dynamics, and the authors transparently report its assumptions and predictions.

## Weaknesses
- **Nucleotide exchange mechanism not isolated from alternatives.** The core claim is that 'continuous nucleotide exchange within filaments tunes their stability' (abstract). The evidence is: (1) ATP* FRAP recovery in Fig. 3F showing fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, and (2) rapid ADP-induced depolymerization in Fig. 4A–B. However, FRAP recovery of a fluorescent nucleotide analogue is consistent with nucleotide exchange anywhere in the filament, at the ends, or via transient dissociation and rebinding of subunits without true intrafilament exchange. The manuscript does not experimentally distinguish between these mechanisms. The ADP-induced depolymerization phenotype (Fig. 4A–B) is equally explained by a simpler model: ADP binds poorly to MreB (Kd ~0.56 µM, Table 1) and fails to stabilize filaments, so filaments shed ADP-bound or nucleotide-free subunits. This does not require active exchange; it only requires that ADP-loaded filaments are unstable. The authors' model (Fig. 4D, S10) assumes intrafilament exchange occurs and uses it to explain the data, but the model is not validated against the alternative hypothesis that exchange is negligible and the phenotypes arise from nucleotide affinity and end-wise dissociation alone. To isolate true intrafilament exchange, one would need to show that nucleotide is released and rebound within the filament interior in a way that cannot occur at the ends—for example, by blocking end dissociation and measuring whether internal nucleotide still exchanges. The current data do not exclude end-only exchange.
- **Claim of 'new class of biopolymer behavior' not adequately scoped.** The abstract and main text state that 'nucleotide cycling within MreB filaments defines a new class of biological polymer behavior' and 'nucleotide cycling within intact filaments defines a new class of biopolymer dynamics.' This is presented as a fundamental discovery. However, the evidence is restricted to B. subtilis MreB on supported lipid bilayers at 0.05–1.2 µM protein, 0.2 mM ATP, 20% cardiolipin, and room temperature or 37°C. No other MreB species is tested; no other conditions are explored. The in vivo data (Fig. 4J) show only that E136A (ATPase-deficient) MreB remains localized when ATP is depleted by CCCP, consistent with the model, but do not directly demonstrate that nucleotide exchange occurs in living cells or that it is the dominant mechanism regulating MreB in vivo. The claim of a 'new class' is not hedged to the tested regime and overstates the generality of the finding. A more precise claim would be: 'MreB filaments on cardiolipin-containing membranes exhibit nucleotide exchange that may contribute to filament stability,' with explicit acknowledgment that the mechanism and its prevalence in vivo remain to be established.
- **Overclaiming on mechanism of ADP-induced depolymerization.** The manuscript states (line 310–311) that 'ADP-rich MreB filaments disassembly occurs through both rapid depolymerization at rates corresponding to k_{-,d} and filament instability leading to fragmentation.' The evidence is the capping experiment (Fig. 4G–H) in which E136A-capped WT filaments break in the WT section when exposed to ADP. However, this experiment does not prove that ADP causes filament instability or fragmentation as a mechanism; it shows that WT filaments depolymerize faster in the presence of ADP than E136A filaments do. The fragmentation observed could result from differential depolymerization rates at the two ends of the WT section, not from ADP-induced instability of the filament interior. The model (Fig. S10A, C) predicts rapid end-wise depolymerization (k_{-,d}) when ADP is present, which alone explains the data without invoking filament fragmentation as a separate mechanism. The language 'filament instability leading to fragmentation' suggests a mechanism not directly demonstrated.
- The claim that ATP hydrolysis is 'not required for polymerization' (section heading, line 223) is supported by the E136A and D158A mutant data, but the phrasing is imprecise: hydrolysis is not required for the initial polymerization reaction, but the manuscript shows it does occur within filaments and contributes to disassembly, so the functional role of hydrolysis is not 'dispensable' as the heading might imply—it is decoupled from initiation.
- The critical concentration estimate (Cc ≈ 0.003 µM, Table 1) is derived from a linear fit at low MreB concentrations (0.01–0.05 µM, Fig. 2E), but the fit window and error bounds are not shown, and the extrapolation to zero concentration is not validated; confidence in this value is unclear.
- The statement that 'nucleotide exchange with free ATP in solution stabilizes the filaments, while exchange with free ADP promotes their disassembly' (line 337–338) is a model prediction (Fig. 4I), not a direct experimental observation; it should be labeled as such.
- The in vivo CCCP experiment (Fig. 4J) measures MreB localization, not filament assembly or nucleotide state directly; the interpretation that E136A remains stable because ATP is not hydrolyzed is indirect and depends on the model.
- The manuscript does not report whether the pFAST tag or TFLime/TFCoral fluorophores affect MreB polymerization kinetics or nucleotide binding, beyond the note that ATP* is 'less efficient' (Fig. S5); potential artifacts from labeling are not fully addressed.

## Questions
- Can you provide the fitting window and residuals for the critical concentration estimate (Cc ≈ 0.003 µM) to assess the reliability of the extrapolation?
- In the FRAP experiment (Fig. 3F), what is the recovery time constant and mobile fraction, and have you tested whether blocking filament ends (e.g., with capping proteins or by anchoring filaments at both ends) abolishes ATP* recovery to distinguish end exchange from internal exchange?
- In the ADP-induced depolymerization experiment, can you measure the depolymerization rate separately at the two ends of a single filament to test whether fragmentation results from differential end rates rather than interior instability?
- Does the model prediction that intrafilament nucleotide exchange is 'significantly faster than the rate of Pi release' (line 335) depend on the assumed exchange rate constant (k_{+,n}, k_{-,n}), and how were these values determined or constrained from the data?
