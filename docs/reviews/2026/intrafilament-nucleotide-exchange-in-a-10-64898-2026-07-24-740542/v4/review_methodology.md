# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive experimental study of MreB polymerization dynamics with a novel finding—intrafilament nucleotide exchange—supported by multiple complementary techniques. The core experimental design is sound and the key claims are largely well-supported, but a critical gap exists in the causal interpretation of the ADP-induced depolymerization experiments, and the Monte Carlo model, while useful, is not independently validated against new data. The work merits publication with major revisions addressing the mechanism of ADP-driven filament instability and the scope of model predictions.

## Strengths
- The study employs multiple orthogonal methods (TIRF, HS-AFM, fluorescence anisotropy, FRAP, in vivo imaging) to establish the same phenomena from different angles, substantially strengthening confidence in the core findings.
- The use of ATPase-deficient mutants (E136A, D158A) as mechanistic probes is well-designed: their differential behavior under nucleotide exchange and depolymerization conditions isolates the role of hydrolysis from binding and exchange, and the predictions match observations across multiple conditions.
- The experimental design for demonstrating bidirectional, kinetically symmetric elongation (dual-color TIRF, photobleaching) is rigorous and directly addresses a key structural question about MreB filament polarity.

## Weaknesses
- **Mechanism of ADP-induced depolymerization is not established.** The paper shows that free ADP triggers rapid filament collapse (Fig. 4A–C, Movie 6), but the design does not isolate whether this occurs because: (1) ADP-bound subunits have intrinsically higher dissociation rates (a nucleotide-state effect); (2) ADP loading destabilizes the filament lattice through a conformational change that propagates; or (3) ADP competes with ATP at filament ends, removing a stabilizing cap. The capping experiment (Fig. 4G–H) shows that E136A caps protect WT cores from rapid disassembly, but this tests whether ATP-bound caps stabilize, not why ADP destabilizes. The model (Fig. 4D, Fig. S10) assigns ADP a higher dissociation rate (k_{-,d} = 60 s⁻¹ vs. k_{-,t} = 0.135 s⁻¹), but this parameter is not independently measured—it is inferred from the requirement that the model reproduce the observed disassembly kinetics. This is circular: the model is fit to the phenomenon it is meant to explain. To establish mechanism, the authors would need to measure ADP-subunit dissociation rates directly (e.g., by stopped-flow kinetics or single-molecule tracking) or show that ADP binding alone, without free nucleotide exchange, is sufficient to trigger disassembly by using a nucleotide-exchange-deficient mutant or a closed system. Without this, the claim that 'ADP promotes disassembly' conflates observation with mechanism.
- **The Monte Carlo model is not validated against independent data.** The model incorporates experimentally measured parameters (k_{+,t}, k_{-,t}, nucleotide binding affinities from anisotropy) but also infers others (k_{-,d}, k_{-,e}, k_{hyd}, k_{Pir}) by fitting to the same polymerization and disassembly time courses it is meant to explain (Fig. S8, S10). The model then generates predictions (e.g., Fig. 4I: the fate of filaments under different nucleotide conditions), but these are not tested against new, independent experiments. For instance, the model predicts that ATP stabilizes filaments while ADP promotes disassembly (Fig. 4I); this is consistent with the ADP-flush experiments, but those experiments were used to constrain the model. A genuine test would be to predict the outcome of a condition not used in fitting—e.g., a mixed ATP/ADP buffer at a specific ratio, or the kinetics of nucleotide exchange under varying ATP* concentrations—and then measure it. Without such validation, the model is a post-hoc narrative that fits the data but does not independently support the mechanistic claims.
- **The FRAP experiment (Fig. 3E) does not measure monomer turnover as claimed.** The authors state that 'monomers do not turnover within filaments' based on the absence of TFLime recovery after photobleaching. However, they then acknowledge that TFLime binding to pFAST is reversible under normal imaging but becomes irreversibly photochemically trapped by the high-intensity bleach laser. This means the FRAP result does not distinguish between (1) no nucleotide exchange (no new ATP* enters the bleached region) and (2) nucleotide exchange occurs but the fluorophore does not recover because the photochemical trap prevents re-binding. The ATP* FRAP (Fig. 3F) does show recovery and is the appropriate experiment for measuring nucleotide exchange, but the TFLime FRAP is not a valid test of monomer turnover and should not be presented as evidence against it. The text acknowledges this caveat but still uses the TFLime result to frame the finding; this conflates two different questions.
- The critical concentration (C_c ≈ 0.003 µM) is derived from a linear fit at low MreB concentrations (Fig. 2E), but the fit window and method are not specified—it is unclear whether the authors used all data points below the plateau or selected a subset, and whether error propagation was performed to estimate uncertainty in C_c.
- The claim that 'nucleotide exchange within MreB filaments defines a new class of biological polymer behavior' (abstract, main text) rests on the comparison to actin, but the paper does not measure whether nucleotide exchange occurs in other prokaryotic actin homologs (ParM, FtsA) or whether it is unique to MreB; the generality of the finding is therefore unknown.
- The in vivo CCCP experiment (Fig. 4J) shows that E136A filaments remain stable when ATP is depleted, consistent with the model, but the experiment is qualitative (visual delocalization scoring) and does not quantify filament dynamics, stability, or the ATP/ADP ratio during the treatment, limiting the strength of the in vivo validation.
- The liposome co-sedimentation assay (Fig. 1E–F) uses a single MreB concentration (2 µM) and does not measure binding affinity or kinetics, so it cannot distinguish between cardiolipin being a major determinant of binding versus one of several contributing factors.

## Questions
- What is the measured dissociation rate of ADP-bound MreB subunits from filament ends, and does it differ significantly from ATP-bound subunits under the same conditions?
- Were the model parameters (k_{-,d}, k_{-,e}, k_{hyd}, k_{Pir}) fit to the polymerization and disassembly data shown in Figures 2, 3, and 4, or were they independently measured or derived from the literature?
- Does nucleotide exchange occur in ParM or other prokaryotic actin homologs, and if so, at comparable rates to MreB?
- In the capping experiment (Fig. 4G–H), what is the distribution of E136A cap sizes, and does the occasional filament stability or complete depolymerization correlate with cap length?
