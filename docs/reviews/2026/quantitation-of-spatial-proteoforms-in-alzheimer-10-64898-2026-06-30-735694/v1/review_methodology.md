# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript employs a sound fractionation-based spatial proteomics strategy coupled with quantitative mass spectrometry and degradation kinetics (QUAD) to interrogate Alzheimer's disease pathogenesis. The core design—comparing AD and non-AD human hippocampi across subcellular fractions, then validating findings in a transgenic mouse model at multiple timepoints—is well-motivated and largely rigorous. However, several design choices limit the strength of causal inference: the human study is observational and cross-sectional (cannot distinguish driver from consequence), the mouse model lacks tau pathology (limiting generalizability to full AD), and key claims about protein mislocalization and aggregation rely partly on indirect evidence (e.g., inferring aggregation from hydrophobicity and insolubility enrichment rather than direct aggregation assays). The fractionation protocol is standard but not validated for this specific tissue/disease context, and the interpretation of reciprocal abundance patterns as evidence of "aberrant trafficking" is plausible but not uniquely supported by the design. Statistical analysis is generally appropriate, though some claims slightly overstate what the data show. Overall, the work makes a solid methodological contribution and identifies biologically plausible candidates, but causal mechanisms remain suggestive rather than proven.

## Strengths
- The spatial fractionation design is well-justified: 75% of proteins detected in multiple fractions, yet 78% of AD-associated changes are fraction-specific, directly supporting the hypothesis that subcellular localization matters—a non-trivial finding that justifies the added complexity.
- Human-mouse integration is thoughtful: the authors compare 12-month AD mice (most analogous to human disease stage) with human data and identify six proteins (BIN1, NSFL1C, HSPA12A, CACNA2D3, DNAJA4, PHYHIPL) altered at the earliest pre-pathological timepoint (2 months), suggesting early perturbations; this cross-species concordance is a genuine strength.
- Multiple orthogonal analytical methods (WGCNA, ANOVA, correlation, GO enrichment, immunoblot validation) converge on endosomal-lysosomal and protein-folding pathways, increasing confidence in those findings.
- Immunoblot validation of key findings (Tau, VPS35, ubiquitin) provides independent corroboration of LC-MS quantification, addressing reproducibility.
- The QUAD degradation assay is a rigorous, quantitative approach to measuring protein stability in vivo across fractions and timepoints, not merely snapshot abundance.
- Sample size and metadata are adequate: 13 AD + 14 non-AD human samples with neuropathological confirmation and Braak staging; 4 biological replicates per condition in mouse studies; Abeta ELISA confirms disease phenotype.

## Weaknesses
-  **HARD: Observational design cannot distinguish driver from consequence in human data.** The human study is cross-sectional post-mortem tissue; 78% of AD changes are fraction-specific, but the design cannot determine whether these changes cause pathology or result from it. The authors acknowledge this ('cannot distinguish alterations that drive pathogenesis from those that respond to it') but then claim findings 'reveal disease-relevant signals' and 'establish spatial proteoforms as essential units of pathogenic analysis'—language that implies causal insight not supported by the design. The mouse QUAD data partially address this by measuring degradation kinetics, but the mouse model lacks tau pathology, which correlates more strongly with dementia severity than Aβ alone (authors note this: 'Tau accumulation...correlates more strongly with dementia severity than Aβ does'). Thus, the human-mouse integration cannot fully resolve causality for the full AD phenotype.
-  **HARD: Fractionation protocol not validated for AD brain tissue.** The authors use a 'simple traditional fractionation method' (differential centrifugation with NP-40 lysis) but do not validate that this protocol reliably separates subcellular compartments in post-mortem AD hippocampi, where pathological aggregates and cellular disruption may alter sedimentation properties. GO enrichment of unique proteins per fraction (Figure 2D) provides indirect evidence of compartment separation, but no direct validation (e.g., marker enzyme assays, immunofluorescence co-localization, or electron microscopy) is shown. This is a potential confound: observed 'mislocalization' could partly reflect altered sedimentation of pathological material rather than true subcellular redistribution.
-  **HARD: Reciprocal abundance patterns (P3 up, S2 down) interpreted as 'aberrant trafficking' without ruling out aggregation/insolubility as the sole mechanism.** The authors observe 150 proteins with discord P3/S2 patterns and propose these reflect 'protein transport' dysfunction (e.g., retromer mislocalization, nuclear transport defects). However, the design does not isolate trafficking from aggregation: proteins enriched in P3 are more hydrophobic (Figure 9D, Figure S4A), enriched for ubiquitination (Figure S4B), and overlap with known insoluble AD proteins (Figure 7E). The authors speculate that 'non-nuclear proteins enriched in P3 and diminished in S2 are aggregating or becoming less soluble,' but this is post-hoc rationalization. A direct test (e.g., solubility assay, cross-linking to detect aggregates, or live-cell trafficking assay in neurons) would be needed to distinguish active mislocalization from passive aggregation/precipitation. Without this, the claim that 'retromer complex mislocalization' drives endosomal dysfunction is suggestive but not proven.
-  **HARD: VPS35 'mislocalization' claim rests on fractionation pattern, not direct localization evidence.** The authors highlight VPS35 as a key finding: 'upregulated in AD P3 and downregulated in AD S2' (Figure 7D, immunoblot shown), and state 'we demonstrated that the expression in total unfractionated hippocampi is unchanged, indicating that this alteration would not been observed in unfractionated LC-MS analysis.' However, the immunoblot (Figure 7D) shows S2, P3, and 'total' lanes; if total is unchanged but fractionated levels differ, this is consistent with redistribution, but it does not prove VPS35 is mislocalized to a pathological compartment. The P3 fraction is enriched in insoluble/aggregated material; VPS35 could be sequestered in aggregates rather than actively transported. Immunofluorescence or subcellular fractionation with solubility controls would directly test localization.
-  **SOFT: Mouse model lacks tau pathology, limiting mechanistic generalizability.** The authors acknowledge this ('Most importantly, it lacks tau pathology') and argue that the Aβ-only model reveals 'molecular mechanisms of Aβ that contribute to dementia.' However, tau correlates more strongly with cognitive decline than Aβ, and the human data show tau-related nuclear transport dysfunction (Ran GTPase binding proteins enriched in P3, NUP160/NUP205 increased in P3). The mouse QUAD data do not capture these tau-driven perturbations, so the human-mouse concordance may miss a major driver of human AD pathology. This limits the scope of conclusions about 'initiating features' of AD.
-  **SOFT: Interpretation of 'early perturbations' at 2 months relies on limited statistical power and single timepoint.** Six proteins overlap between human AD data and 2-month mouse data (BIN1, NSFL1C, HSPA12A, CACNA2D3, DNAJA4, PHYHIPL). The authors propose these are 'initiating features' of AD. However, (i) the 2-month mouse timepoint has few significant changes (Figure 9A shows sparse volcano plot for 2-month), (ii) no pre-pathological baseline (e.g., 1-month or younger) is included to confirm these are truly 'earliest,' and (iii) the overlap is small (6 proteins) and could reflect noise or shared biology rather than causal initiation. A dose-response or time-course in younger mice would strengthen this claim.
-  **SOFT: Tau correlation analysis (Figure 5C, 5D) uses post-hoc thresholding (r > 0.7) without multiple-comparison correction.** The authors identify 124 proteins correlating with Tau and report 15 as 'previously reported Tau regulators.' However, the correlation threshold (r > 0.7) is arbitrary, and no multiple-comparison correction (e.g., FDR) is stated. This inflates false-positive rate for candidate biomarkers. The immunoblot validation of Tau (Figure 5B) is good, but the correlation-based candidate list should be treated as exploratory, not confirmatory.
-  **SOFT: Asymptomatic AD (AsymAD) comprises 80% of non-AD controls, conflating two phenotypes.** The authors note 'the NA samples were comprised of two different classifications: 1) humans (N=11) with AD brain pathology...also known as asymptomatic AD (AsymAD) or 2) humans (N=3, i.e. "normal") with no AD brain pathology.' This means the 'non-AD' group is mostly AsymAD, not cognitively normal. The authors argue this is an advantage ('our findings likely reflect proteomic changes underlying AD dementia rather than mechanisms triggered by Aβ plaques or NFT'), but it also means the study compares symptomatic AD to pre-symptomatic AD, not AD to healthy controls. This narrows the scope of conclusions about 'AD pathogenesis' and may miss early, reversible changes. Sensitivity analysis comparing symptomatic AD to only the 3 normal controls would clarify.
-  **SOFT: WGCNA module assignment and GO enrichment use default parameters without sensitivity analysis.** The authors perform WGCNA with MetaNetwork but do not report soft-thresholding power, module detection parameters, or robustness to parameter variation. GO enrichment uses 5% FDR, but no sensitivity to FDR threshold is shown. This is standard practice but limits reproducibility and robustness assessment.
-  **SOFT: Hydrophobicity (GRAVY score) used as proxy for aggregation propensity, but not validated against direct aggregation assays.** Figure 9D and S4A show P3 proteins are more hydrophobic than S2 proteins, and the authors infer this indicates 'higher propensity to aggregation.' However, GRAVY score is a sequence-based prediction, not a direct measure of aggregation state. Biochemical validation (e.g., filter trap assay, thioflavin-T fluorescence, or sedimentation of aggregates) would strengthen this inference.

## Questions
- How were the four fractions (S2, P2, S3, P3) validated to represent distinct subcellular compartments in post-mortem AD hippocampi? Were marker enzymes (e.g., cytochrome c oxidase for mitochondria, acid phosphatase for lysosomes) or immunofluorescence used to confirm compartment identity, especially given that AD pathology may disrupt normal sedimentation?
- The authors state that VPS35 is 'upregulated in AD P3 and downregulated in AD S2' but unchanged in total hippocampi. Does the P3 fraction represent a pathological insoluble pool, or a distinct functional compartment? Can you provide solubility data (e.g., urea or detergent extraction) to distinguish aggregation from active mislocalization?
- For the six proteins identified at 2 months (BIN1, NSFL1C, HSPA12A, CACNA2D3, DNAJA4, PHYHIPL), were younger timepoints (e.g., 1 month or pre-symptomatic) examined to confirm these are the earliest perturbations, or is 2 months the earliest timepoint measured?
- The Tau correlation analysis (Figure 5D) uses r > 0.7 as a threshold for 'correlated proteins.' Was this threshold pre-specified, and was multiple-comparison correction (e.g., FDR) applied to the 124 correlations tested?
- The mouse model (APPswePS1delta9) lacks tau pathology. Given that the human data show tau-related nuclear transport dysfunction (Ran GTPase enrichment in P3), how do the authors reconcile the absence of tau pathology in mice with the claim that the human-mouse overlap reveals 'initiating features' of AD?
- Can the authors provide direct evidence (e.g., immunofluorescence, live-cell imaging, or solubility assays) that the observed reciprocal abundance patterns (e.g., VPS35 up in P3, down in S2) reflect active protein trafficking rather than passive aggregation or altered sedimentation due to pathological changes?
- The non-AD group is 80% asymptomatic AD (AsymAD). How do the findings change if the analysis is restricted to the 3 cognitively normal controls versus the 11 AsymAD samples? Is there a sensitivity analysis comparing symptomatic AD to only normal controls?
- For the QUAD analysis, the authors pool Day 0 samples within each genotype and timepoint (Methods: 'For each timepoint, the Day0 samples for each genotype with an equal number of male and female mice were pooled'). This removes within-genotype variance at Day 0. How does this pooling affect the Day7/Day0 ratio calculation and the power to detect genotype differences, especially at early timepoints where changes may be subtle?
- The authors claim that 78% of significant AD changes are 'fraction-specific' (Figure 4C), yet 89% of these proteins are quantified in more than one fraction. What is the statistical power to detect a change in a given fraction if a protein is present in multiple fractions? Could the fraction-specificity reflect lower abundance or higher noise in non-primary fractions rather than true biological compartmentalization?
- Figure 7E shows overlap between AD P3-upregulated proteins and previously reported insoluble AD proteins. How much of this overlap is expected by chance, given that P3 is enriched in insoluble material by design? A permutation test or comparison to a random protein set would clarify whether the overlap is significant.
