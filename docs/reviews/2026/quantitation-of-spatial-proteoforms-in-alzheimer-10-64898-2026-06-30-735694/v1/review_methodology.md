# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
This is a creative and technically ambitious design — subcellular fractionation coupled to TMT-LC-MS in human hippocampi, plus QUAD degradation measurements in an AD mouse model across three ages. The data are rich and the fractionation approach is genuinely underused in AD proteomics. However, the central interpretive claims — that discordant fraction patterns demonstrate mislocalization, and that fraction-specific changes demonstrate differential vulnerability of spatial proteoforms — are confounded by a biochemical artifact the design cannot separate from true spatial relocalization. The cross-species inference at the 2-month timepoint rests on an overlap of six proteins with no demonstrated significance beyond chance. The design supports the descriptive findings but not several of the mechanistic conclusions drawn from them.

## Strengths
- The paired human/mouse design is well conceived: post-mortem snapshots complemented by a degradation-kinetics approach that can address causality.
- The authors repeatedly validate key LC-MS findings with orthogonal immunoblotting (Tau, VPS35), which is exactly the corroboration a reviewer wants.
- The analysis is unusually candid about acknowledged limitations (e.g., the trypsin/Abeta ambiguity, the lack of tau pathology in the mouse model).

## Weaknesses
- HARD — The P3/S2 discordant pattern cannot distinguish mislocalization from insolubility. The claim that VPS35 and the retromer are mislocalized in AD rests on the observation that VPS35 is up in P3 and down in S2. But P3 is the NP-40-insoluble pellet. A protein that becomes insoluble or aggregate-associated in AD will shift from S2 to P3 during centrifugation regardless of its true subcellular location. The authors acknowledge this for insoluble protein accumulation but still claim mislocalization for the retromer and nuclear transport dysfunction for the Ran GTPase-binding proteins. The design cannot distinguish a protein moved to a different compartment from a protein that became insoluble and therefore pellets differently. The authors' own data support the latter: they show increased hydrophobicity and ubiquitination in P3, and 37 P3-upregulated proteins overlap with previously published insoluble-fraction datasets. To support the mislocalization claim, an orthogonal assay that does not depend on pelleting is needed — e.g., immunofluorescence showing VPS35 in a different compartment, or a soluble-fraction measurement that isolates transport from solubility.
- HARD — The 78% fraction-specific claim does not rule out a measurement artifact. The headline claim that subcellular localization is a primary determinant of disease vulnerability rests on the observation that 78% of significant changes are restricted to one fraction. But a protein quantified in multiple fractions might show significance in only one fraction because of fraction-specific variance, sensitivity, or peptide sampling depth — not because a specific proteoform is differentially vulnerable. The authors do not test whether the fraction-specific changes are consistent with a single underlying effect with fraction-specific noise. A straightforward check: for proteins significant in one fraction but quantified in others, is the effect size in the non-significant factions in the same direction but below significance, or genuinely absent? The claim as stated is not earned without ruling out the noise explanation.
- HARD — The QUAD Day0 pooling and the 2-month mechanism are both problematic. Day0 samples were pooled per genotype within each timepoint, so the Day7/Day0 ratio has no variance in the denominator. If AD and WT differ in baseline AHA incorporation (i.e., protein synthesis), the ratio is biased in a direction the design cannot detect. The authors do not report whether synthesis rates were comparable between genotypes. Separately, at 2 months the AD mice have minimal Abeta (the authors' own ELISA shows 5 times more at 5 times than at 2 times), so what drives the degradation change at 2 months? The claim that degradation changes are Abeta-driven is not supported at the earliest timepoint, and the authors do not offer an alternative mechanism.
- HARD — The six-protein overlap at 2 months could be chance. With thousands of proteins tested, six overlaps between the 2-month mouse data and the human data could occur by chance. No enrichment, permutation, or hypergeometric test is reported. The claim that these six implicate vesicle transport and proteostasis disruption as initiating features is not earned without showing the overlap exceeds chance expectation. The authors should report the expected number of overlaps given the protein counts in each dataset and the significance threshold used.
- SOFT — The AsymAD composition of the control group undermines a stated interpretation. Eleven of NA samples are AsymAD (pathology present, no dementia). The comparison is therefore dementia vs. no-dementia, both with plaques and tangles. The claim that findings reflect protein changes underlying AD dementia rather than mechanisms triggered by Abeta plaques or NFT is not earned — AsymAD brains have plaques and tangles too, just less severe. This is a severity gradient, not a clean contrast, and the interpretation should be scaled accordingly.
- SOFT — WGNCA cross-fraction comparisons compare different protein sets. Modules are constructed independently per fraction, so a protein folding module in S2 is not the same set of proteins as a protein folding module in P2. The claim that a pathway is regulated differently between fractions requires the same proteins in both modules; the authors show only partial overlap (14 of 86 proteins shared between two fractions, 4 in all three). The claim of differential regulation is therefore based on largely non-overlapped protein sets.
- SOFT — The Tau correlation threshold (r > 0.7) has no multiple-testing correction. With 27 samples and thousands of proteins, the expected number of correlations exceeding this threshold by chance is not addressed. The claim that Rab2a may be a negative regulator of Tau aggregation based on correlation in NA samples (which have low Tau) is a weak basis for a regulatory claim.
- SOFT — The human-mouse correlation assumes degradation determines steady-state abundance. The inference that faster degradation in AD mice corresponds to decreased abundance in AD humans holds only if synthesis is unchanged. Synthesis is unmeasured in the human samples (post-mortem, cannot be), so the direction-of-change assumption is an untested premise.

## Questions
- For proteins significant in one fraction but quantified in others, are the non-significant fraction effect sizes in the same direction but below significance, or genuinely absent? This would distinguish the measurement-artifact explanation from true proteoform-specific vulnerability.
- What is the baseline AHA incorporation (Day0) in AD vs. WT — is there evidence that synthesis rates are comparable between genotypes?
- What is the expected number of overlaps between the 2-month mouse and human datasets by chance, given the protein counts and thresholds used?
