# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This is a substantial and methodologically ambitious manuscript — fractionating human hippocampi into four subcellular compartments and pairing the analysis with a degradation-rate assay in a transgenic mouse model is a genuine contribution. The core data (fraction-specific changes, discordant S2/P3 patterns, degradation differences) appear real and worth reporting. The problem is concentrated where it matters most: the abstract and several Results sentences assert mechanisms — "mislocalization," "transport dysfunction," "initiating features" — that the design cannot establish, and the headline claim that subcellular localization is a "primary determinant of disease vulnerability" rests on a 78% figure that may be an artifact of detection incompleteness. The manuscript needs requalification of these claims and one additional analysis to exclude the most obvious confound.

## Weaknesses
- The abstract's claim that the 78% fraction-specificity 'demonstrating that subcellular localization is a primary determinant of disease vulnerability' is not licensed; the figure may reflect detection incompleteness (proteins near the detection limit in other fractions) rather than localization-dependent biology.
- The authors report that 75% of proteins were detected in more than one fraction, but never state whether the 78% fraction-specific changes are concentrated among proteins detected in only one fraction; if they are, the claim collapses.
- The claim that the six-protein overlap 'implicat[es] vesicle transport and proteostasis disruption as initiating features of AD' is causal language not supported by the evidence: the 2-month mice are transgenic with mutations present from conception, so 'pre-pathological' means only low Abeta accumulation, and the six-protein overlap is not tested against chance expectation.
- The discordant S2/P3 patterns do not license 'nuclear transport dysfunction': the fractionation protocol places nuclei in P3 by design, and the pattern is equally consistent with aggregation/insolubility (proteins migrating from S2 to P3 during centrifugation without any transport defect); the immunoblot confirms the abundance pattern, not the mechanism.
- The retromer 'mislocalization' claim is under-hedged, since the same aggregation alternative applies.
- The human-mouse correlation assumes degradation rate is the sole determinant of protein abundance, ignoring transcription and other inputs; this assumption is neither stated nor tested.
- The QUAD Day0 samples were pooled per genotype/timepoint, so the Day7/Day0 ratio variance reflects only Day7, yet the statistics treat both timepoints as contributing variance.
- The 5-month reversal in degradation patterns (AD S2 faster at 2mo, slower at 5mo, faster at 12mo) is unexplained and the narrative reads as post-hoc.
- The biological meaning of opposite module directions within one pathway ('protein folding pathway was upregulated in the blue and green modules in the S2 with AD but downregulated in the turquoise modules in the P2 and P3 fractions') is asserted, not explained.
- The claim that proteins correlating with Tau in NA samples 'may reflect Tau resilience' is speculative; no evidence links those correlations to resilience.
- 'These findings establish spatial proteoforms as essential units of pathogenic analysis' outruns evidence that is correlational and drawn from one tissue (hippocampus) and one AD model.
- The abstract's 'reveal disease-relevant signals invisible to bulk tissue approaches' is supported by the VPS35 example but asserted generally; the meta-analysis comparison used non-hippocampal tissue, so the comparison is not apples-to-apples.
