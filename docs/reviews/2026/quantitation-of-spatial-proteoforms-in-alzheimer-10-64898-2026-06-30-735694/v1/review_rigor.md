# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript presents a spatially resolved proteomics study of Alzheimer's disease using subcellular fractionation and mass spectrometry. The core claim—that 78% of AD-associated protein alterations are fraction-specific despite 75% of proteins being detected in multiple fractions—is well-supported by the ANOVA data presented. However, several load-bearing claims in the abstract and discussion are either over-stated relative to the evidence, imprecisely hedged, or conflate correlation with mechanism. The human data are observational and cannot establish causation; the mouse QUAD data provide degradation rates but not direct proof that observed abundance changes result from altered degradation rather than localization shifts. The work is rigorous in execution and novel in scope, but claims about what drives pathogenesis and the initiating role of specific proteins require stronger hedging or additional evidence.

## Strengths
- The core finding—that 78% of significant AD changes are fraction-specific—is directly supported by ANOVA results (Figure 4C, Supplementary Table 6) and represents a genuine advance over unfractionated analyses.
- Fractionation protocol is well-documented and reproducible; GO enrichment of unique proteins per fraction (Figure 2D) validates that fractions capture distinct subcellular compartments.
- The retromer complex (VPS35/VPS29) mislocalization finding is compelling: LC-MS shows reciprocal P3/S2 patterns, confirmed by immunoblot (Figure 7D), and notably absent in unfractionated meta-analysis—a concrete example of signal rescue.
- QUAD methodology is sound and appropriately applied; degradation trends across ages and genotypes are clearly presented with appropriate statistical tests (Figure 8E–H).
- Cross-dataset validation (human hippocampi vs. mouse cortex at 12 months, Figure 9E) identifies 155 correlated proteins enriched in vesicle transport and protein folding—a reasonable intersection approach.
- Tau analysis includes Braak score correlation (r=0.67, p<0.0002 in P3; Figure 5C), providing independent clinical validation of the P3 enrichment finding.
- Supplementary tables are comprehensive and appear complete; data availability statement is present (though specific repository/accession not visible in excerpt).

## Weaknesses
- {HARD} Abstract claims 'retromer complex mislocalization' and 'nuclear transport dysfunction' as established findings, but these are inferred from protein abundance patterns, not directly measured. The abstract states: 'Discordant abundance patterns between fractions revealed retromer complex mislocalization, nuclear transport dysfunction, and insoluble protein accumulation.' Mislocalization is inferred from reciprocal LC-MS ratios; NCT dysfunction is inferred from RAN GTPase binding protein enrichment (Figure 7B). Neither is a direct measurement of localization or transport function. This should be reworded to 'are consistent with' or 'suggest.'
- {HARD} The claim that 'six proteins altered at the earliest pre-pathological timepoint, implicating vesicle transport and proteostasis disruption as initiating features of AD' (abstract) uses 'implicating' and 'initiating' language that exceeds the evidence. The six proteins (BIN1, NSFL1C, HSPA12A, CACNA2D3, DNAJA4, PHYHIPL) are identified at 2 months in the APPswePS1delta9 mouse model, which already expresses human APP and PSEN1 mutations—not a pre-pathological state. The model shows Aβ42 elevation by 2 months (Figure S4C). The authors acknowledge this limitation in the discussion ('Although there are many biological mechanisms proposed to cause AD dementia…') but the abstract does not. The abstract should clarify that these are early changes in a transgenic model with pre-existing mutations, not necessarily initiating events in human disease.
- {HARD} Discussion states: 'This striking concordance between QUAD and human datasets suggests a subset of AD perturbations in human tissue involved in vesicle transport and protein folding are produced by alterations in protein degradation' (p. 28). This is a causal claim ('are produced by') based on correlational data. The logic is: if a protein degrades faster in AD mice, it should be less abundant in AD humans; if it correlates, degradation may explain the abundance change. But the design does not exclude alternative mechanisms (e.g., altered synthesis, sequestration, or localization without degradation). The word 'produced' should be replaced with 'consistent with' or 'may involve.'
- {SOFT} The claim that 'most AD perturbations were restricted to one fraction' (Results, p. 15) is stated as a general finding, but the quantitative support is 78% of 2,405 significant changes (= ~1,876 proteins) being fraction-specific. The denominator is 6,123 total proteins identified. The phrasing 'most AD perturbations' is accurate but could be misread as 'most proteins are perturbed'—a much stronger claim. Suggest: 'most significant AD-associated changes were restricted to one fraction' to avoid ambiguity.
- {SOFT} The discussion claims that 'P3 was unique due to its nuclear protein enrichment' (p. 26), but Figure 2D shows P3 is enriched in nuclear GO terms among the unique proteins in P3, not that P3 is uniquely enriched in nuclear proteins overall. The statement is defensible but could be clearer: 'P3 was distinguished by enrichment of nuclear-associated proteins among its uniquely identified proteins' would be more precise.
- {SOFT} The paper infers that proteins with opposite S2/P3 patterns 'could represent misfolding or aggregating proteins accumulating in P3' (p. 27), but this is speculative. The authors note that P3 proteins are more hydrophobic (Figure S4A) and that some overlap with published insoluble fractions (Figure 7E), which supports the hypothesis, but the mechanism is not directly tested. The hedging ('could represent', 'we speculate') is present but the inference chain is long. This is acceptable as a hypothesis but should not be presented as established.
- {SOFT} The claim that 'Endosomal pathway dysfunction is a preclinical AD hallmark' (Discussion, p. 24) is cited to references 77–78, but the authors then state 'Consistently, we observed an enrichment of vesicle trafficking and endo-lysosomal pathways (ELP) altered in AD' (p. 24). The word 'consistently' implies their finding confirms prior work, but they are observing the same pathways in a different tissue (hippocampus vs. prior studies' tissues) and with a different method (spatial fractionation). This is consistent in direction but not a replication; the phrasing is slightly misleading.
- {SOFT} The paper identifies 11 AD risk factors altered in the dataset (Figure 4E) and notes that 'All risk factors were altered in one fraction, except for Bin1 which was downregulated in both S2 and P2' (p. 17). However, no statistical test is reported for whether the distribution of risk factors across fractions differs from the genome-wide distribution. It is unclear whether risk factors are preferentially fraction-specific or simply follow the overall trend. This is a missed opportunity for a stronger claim if the data support it.
- {SOFT} The human cohort includes 11 asymptomatic AD (AsymAD) cases and only 3 cognitively normal controls with no pathology (Table S1, referenced in Results). The authors state: 'Because 80% of the NA group was AsymAD, our findings likely reflect proteomic changes underlying AD dementia rather than mechanisms triggered by Aβ plaques or NFT' (p. 24). This reasoning is inverted: if most controls have Aβ/Tau pathology, the comparison is AD dementia vs. asymptomatic pathology, not AD vs. normal. The authors cannot cleanly separate dementia-driving changes from pathology-independent effects with this cohort composition. The limitation should be stated more clearly near the main claim, not buried in the discussion.
- {HARD} Figure 4F shows APP peptides: Aβ peptides are upregulated in all fractions in AD, but the authors state 'Although LC-MS analysis cannot determine if an identified Aβ peptide was cleaved in vivo from APP or cleaved in vitro with trypsin, the most Aβ peptides were quantified in the P3 fraction' (p. 17). This is a significant caveat—the Aβ signal could be artifactual. The authors acknowledge it but do not quantify the risk (e.g., by comparing in vivo vs. in vitro cleavage patterns or using a protease inhibitor control). The interpretation of Aβ enrichment in P3 should be hedged further or experimentally validated.
- {SOFT} The QUAD analysis pools Day 0 samples within each genotype (Methods, p. 10: 'For each timepoint, the Day0 samples for each genotype with an equal number of male and female mice were pooled for each fraction'). This removes within-genotype variance at Day 0, which could inflate the Day 7/Day 0 ratio variance and affect statistical power. The authors do not discuss this design choice or its impact on the degradation estimates. A sensitivity analysis or justification would strengthen the QUAD section.

## Questions
- The abstract and Results use 'retromer complex mislocalization' as an established finding, but is this inferred solely from the reciprocal LC-MS abundance pattern (VPS35 up in P3, down in S2)? Have the authors considered that VPS35 could be sequestered or post-translationally modified rather than mislocalized? Could a direct imaging or cell fractionation validation be added?
- The six proteins identified at 2 months in the APPswePS1delta9 model (BIN1, NSFL1C, HSPA12A, CACNA2D3, DNAJA4, PHYHIPL) are called 'initiating features' in the abstract. But the model expresses mutant APP and PSEN1 from birth. How do the authors distinguish between changes driven by the transgenes themselves vs. early Aβ-driven pathology? Is there a wild-type littermate comparison at 2 months to show these proteins are not altered by the genetic background alone?
- In Figure 9E, the correlation between human and mouse datasets is based on the stipulation that faster degradation in AD mice should correspond to lower abundance in AD humans. But this assumes degradation is the only driver of abundance change. How do the authors exclude altered synthesis, sequestration, or transport? Is there a way to test whether degradation vs. localization explains the observed changes?
- The human cohort is 80% asymptomatic AD (pathology present, no dementia) vs. 20% cognitively normal with no pathology. How do the authors interpret findings when the 'control' group is mostly pathological? Can the authors stratify results by control type (AsymAD vs. truly normal) to show the effect is not driven by the pathological controls?
- Figure 7D shows VPS35 immunoblot confirming the LC-MS P3/S2 pattern, but the total (unfractionated) sample shows no difference between AD and NA. This is a key finding—why is it not highlighted more prominently? Does this suggest that spatial proteomics is necessary to detect VPS35 changes, or could the total sample have been underpowered?
- The P3 fraction is described as enriched in insoluble/aggregated proteins (based on Aβ, Tau, and hydrophobicity). But the fractionation protocol uses differential centrifugation at 700×g, 17.2K×g, and 0.5% NP-40 lysis—standard steps that may not fully separate soluble from insoluble material. How confident are the authors that P3 truly represents insoluble aggregates vs. a mixture of compartments?
- In the QUAD analysis, Day 0 samples are pooled within each genotype, removing individual variance. How does this affect the Day 7/Day 0 ratio estimates and statistical power? Have the authors performed a sensitivity analysis with unpooled Day 0 data?
- The enrichment of vesicle transport and protein folding pathways is observed in both WGCNA (Figure 3A) and ANOVA (Figure 4D). Are these the same proteins or different subsets? A Venn diagram or overlap analysis would clarify whether the two statistical methods identify consistent biology.
- The authors state that 'most AD proteins being detectable across multiple subcellular fractions, 78% of significant protein alterations are fraction specific' (p. 24). Is this 78% figure specific to the hippocampus, or do the authors expect it to generalize to other brain regions or tissues? What is the biological basis for this specificity?
- Figure 5C shows Tau correlates with Braak score in P3 (r=0.67) and S3 (r=0.46). Why is the correlation weaker in S3? Does this suggest P3 is a better biomarker, or is it a statistical artifact of smaller sample size or higher noise in S3?
