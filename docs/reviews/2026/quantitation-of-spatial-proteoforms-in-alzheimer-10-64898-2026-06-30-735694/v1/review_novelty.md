# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The central novelty claim — that subcellular fractionation of AD hippocampus with TMT-LC-MS reveals spatial proteoform alterations invisible to bulk analysis — has real merit but is overstated relative to prior work. Bai et al. (Neuron 2020, ref 3) already employed multilayer fractionation (soluble, membrane/organelle, insoluble) with TMT-LC-MS in AD brain, identifying molecular networks and fraction-specific changes. The present study's delta over that work is not clearly articulated, and the claim of "disease-relevant signals invisible to bulk tissue approaches" (Abstract, Discussion) is not wrong but is a difference of degree, not kind. The QUAD extension to subcellular fractions in an AD mouse model is incremental over both the authors' own prior method (McClatchy et al. 2020) and Fornasiero et al. (Nat Commun 2018), which measured protein lifetimes across subcellular fractions in mouse brain. The VPS35 mislocalisation finding and the six-protein early-stage discovery are specific and potentially novel, but their significance is inflated by being presented as though the overall spatial-fractionation approach itself were unprecedented.

## Weaknesses
- The central novelty claim is incompletely differentiated from Bai et al. (2020). The manuscript states that "conventional unfractionated analyses may obscure" AD proteome changes (Abstract) and that fractionation "reveals disease-relevant signals invisible to bulk tissue approaches" (Discussion). But Bai et al. already fractionated AD brains into 3 layers (soluble, membrane/organelle, insoluble), used TMT-LC-MS, and identified 5,305 proteins across multiple regions including hippocampus. Their fractions differ from the S2/P2/S3/P3 scheme here, but the core insight — that fractionation reveals layer-specific AD alterations — was established. The present study does not name Bai 2020 as the closest prior work, does not provide a head-to-head table comparing what each set of fractions captures, and does not state explicitly what biological dimension the current 4-fraction scheme accesses that Bai's 3-fraction scheme does not. Without this differentiation it is unclear whether the 78 % fraction-specificity statistic is a novel finding or a replication of an expected property in a different fractionation scheme.
- The QUAD degradation comparisons are incremental over established methods in the same system. The authors apply the QUAD method (published by this group in 2020) to two subcellular fractions of APPswePS1dE9 mouse cortex at three ages and report that AD alters degradation rates. Fornasiero et al. (Nat Commun 2018) already measured protein lifetimes across subcellular fractions (cytosol, membranes, mitochondria, synaptosomes, nuclei) in mouse brain using a different labelling strategy (SILAC), finding that subcellular localisation is a major determinant of protein turnover. The present study's contribution is to repeat that analysis in an AD model. This is useful — degradation dynamics may differ in disease — but the manuscript frames it as though subcellular degradation analysis itself were novel ("we quantitated protein degradation in different biological fractions," Introduction). The delta (AD-specific vs. WT-specific changes in degradation across fractions) is real but should be presented as an incremental extension, not as an innovation.
- The six-protein early-stage overlap (2-month mouse + human) is a thin discovery. The authors highlight six proteins (BIN1, NSFL1C, HSPA12A, CACNA2D3, DNAJA4, PHYHIPL) that are altered in both the human AD analysis and the 2-month pre-pathological mouse timepoint. BIN1 is an established AD risk factor; its alteration in an APP-overexpressing model at 2 months could reflect a direct response to APP overexpression rather than an early pathogenic mechanism. The remaining five proteins are mostly novel but their individual statistical support is not described — the authors report only that they "overlap" between datasets, not the direction of change, the effect sizes, or whether the overlap exceeds chance expectation given the number of proteins quantified in both datasets. A Fisher's exact test or permutation-based enrichment would establish whether six proteins from a set of hundreds is surprising or within the expected false-positive count.
- The manuscript's core statistic "78% of significant changes were fraction-specific" would be more informative if reported alongside the same statistic for the unfractionated control (i.e., what percentage of proteins that change in *any* fraction would have been detected as changed if the fractions were pooled computationally?) — this would quantify the gain of fractionation directly.
- The comparison to "seven proteomic TMT datasets on human unfractionated AD non-hippocampal brain tissue" (Results, Supplementary Table 7) that confirmed 748/2405 (31%) of the present changes does not tell the reader how many of the remaining 69% are missed solely because of brain-region differences versus fractionation depth; a direct comparison using the same brain region (hippocampus) fractionated vs. unfractionated would be more informative.
- The manuscript uses "spatial proteoforms" in a way that conflates true splice-isoform-specific localisation with the trivial case of a protein detected in multiple fractions because of contamination or incomplete fractionation — a distinction not addressed in the text.
- The WGCNA and ANOVA analyses on the human data are described as parallel but their results are not cross-referenced; reporting the overlap of significant proteins/modules between the two methods would strengthen the claim that the findings are robust.
- The claim that "80% of the NA group was AsymAD" and therefore the findings "likely reflect proteomic changes underlying AD dementia rather than mechanisms triggered by Aβ plaques or NFT" (Discussion) is a reasonable inference but is stated as a conclusion when it is a selection-bias argument; the study has no AD-dementia vs. AsymAD contrast to demonstrate this.
- The QUAD analysis pools male and female mice (one of each per replicate) and does not examine sex as a variable; given known sex differences in AD progression, this is a limitation that should be acknowledged.
