# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
This is a competent, well-executed proteomics study with solid experimental design and useful specific findings (retromer mislocalization, nuclear transport dysfunction, early protein alterations in mouse model). However, the central novelty claim—that "spatial proteoforms" represent a new conceptual framework for understanding AD—is overstated. Subcellular fractionation, the application to brain proteomics, and even the QUAD method are all established by the authors' own prior work. The genuine contribution is demonstrating that compartment-specific analysis reveals disease-relevant signals (particularly VPS35 mislocalization and Tau-Braak correlation in specific fractions) that bulk analysis obscures. This is a real and useful finding, but it is an incremental methodological insight rather than a conceptual breakthrough.

## Strengths
- Solid experimental design combining human post-mortem tissue (27 samples, 13 AD/14 non-AD) with longitudinal mouse QUAD data across three timepoints (2, 5, 12 months) provides complementary perspectives on disease mechanisms.
- Comprehensive quantification of 6,123 proteins identified in human samples with 75% detected in multiple fractions; rigorous TMT labeling and LC-MS methodology with appropriate controls (Aβ42 ELISA validation, immunoblot confirmation of key findings like VPS35 and Tau).
- Retromer complex (VPS35/VPS29) mislocalization finding is well-supported by both LC-MS and immunoblot data and represents a specific, testable observation not obvious from unfractionated analysis; Tau correlation with Braak score in P3/S3 fractions is statistically robust (r=0.67, p<0.0002).
- Cross-dataset integration identifying six proteins altered at 2-month pre-pathological timepoint (BIN1, NSFL1C, HSPA12A, CACNA2D3, DNAJA4, PHYHIPL) with functional links to vesicle transport and proteostasis provides useful synthesis.
- Appropriate statistical rigor with multiple complementary analyses (WGCNA, ANOVA, correlation, GO enrichment) with FDR correction and transparent reporting of p-values and effect sizes.

## Weaknesses
- Overstated novelty of "spatial proteoforms" framework: the manuscript claims to introduce "a spatial proteomic framework for AD" and frames spatial proteoforms as "essential units of pathogenic analysis," but the concept that proteins localize to multiple subcellular compartments is well-established (authors cite Thul et al. 2017 noting >50% of proteins in multiple compartments), and the authors' own 2012 work (McClatchy et al., J Proteome Res 11:2467-2479) used identical fractionation methodology on brain tissue.
- Unclear novelty over prior insoluble fraction studies: the manuscript acknowledges that "numerous studies have analyzed one specific AD sub-proteome (i.e. insoluble fraction)" (Bai et al. 2013, Hales et al. 2016), and the P3 fraction enriches for insoluble/aggregated proteins; the key distinction of analyzing "all fractions" is methodologically sensible but not conceptually unprecedented.
- VPS35 finding not clearly novel: while VPS35 mislocalization is interesting, the authors note that retromer dysfunction "has been observed to be dysregulated in AD" with prior work suggesting it is "a key player in ELP dysfunction" (Small & Gandy 2006, Young et al. 2018); the novelty is demonstrating spatial mislocalization rather than identifying retromer as an AD target.
- "Earliest pre-pathological timepoint" claim needs qualification: the manuscript states that six proteins altered at 2-month mouse timepoint represent "initiating, rather than consequential, features of AD progression," but 2 months in APPswePS1delta9 mice is not pre-pathological—Aβ42 is already detectable (~5-fold increase by 2 months per Figure S4C); the claim that these proteins represent "initiating" features is speculative without temporal data showing they precede Aβ accumulation.
- Framing of "78% fraction-specific" finding as major discovery is ambiguous: the authors acknowledge that some fraction-specific changes may reflect "transcriptional changes for proteins targeted to only one compartment, though this is unlikely given that most altered proteins were quantified in more than one fraction," suggesting the 78% figure is not as clean a signal as presented.
- Limited mechanistic explanation for discord patterns: the P3/S2 discord proteins (150 shared proteins with opposite trends) are attributed to three mechanisms (nuclear transport dysfunction, insoluble protein accumulation, mitochondrial mislocalization), but evidence is correlative; for example, nuclear proteins enriched in P3 are interpreted as evidence of NCT dysfunction, but could simply reflect Tau-associated pathology rather than active NCT dysfunction.
- Asymptomatic AD (AsymAD) confound not fully addressed: 80% of the non-AD control group (11/14) had AD brain pathology; the authors infer that "findings likely reflect proteomic changes underlying AD dementia rather than mechanisms triggered by Aβ plaques or NFT," but this is an inference, not a demonstration; a comparison of symptomatic AD vs. AsymAD vs. normal controls would strengthen claims.
- Cross-dataset correlation modest: the human-mouse overlap (155 proteins, 27 in P3 and 128 in S2) represents only ~8% of the 1,905 unique proteins altered in human data, limiting confidence in generalizability of mouse findings to human disease.
- APP/Aβ peptide interpretation unclear: the authors note that "LC-MS analysis cannot determine if an identified Aβ peptide was cleaved in vivo from APP or cleaved in vitro with trypsin," and observe Aβ peptides upregulated in all fractions but only APP protein altered in P3; the distinction between in vivo and in vitro cleavage is a significant caveat deserving more discussion.
- Magnitude of protein stability changes in QUAD modest: while authors observe that AD S2 proteins degrade faster at 2 months and slower at 12 months (Figure 8F), median effect sizes are modest (log2 ratios ~0.3–0.5 in many cases); biological significance relative to noise is not clearly established.

## Questions
- How does this work differ fundamentally from prior subcellular fractionation proteomics studies (including your own 2012 work)? Is the novelty primarily the application to AD, the specific fractionation scheme, or the interpretation through a "spatial proteoforms" lens? This should be stated explicitly in the Introduction.
- You show VPS35 is increased in P3 and decreased in S2 in AD, confirmed by immunoblot on fractionated tissue. However, total (unfractionated) hippocampus shows no change (Figure 7D, right panel). Does this mean VPS35 is being redistributed between compartments without net change, or is there a technical artifact in the fractionation? If redistribution, what drives it—altered trafficking, aggregation, or degradation?
- You state that six proteins altered at 2 months represent "initiating features" of AD. However, Aβ42 is already elevated 5-fold at 2 months (Figure S4C). How do you distinguish between proteins altered in response to early Aβ accumulation versus proteins that initiate pathology? Would analysis at an earlier timepoint (e.g., 1 month or pre-symptomatic transgenic mice) clarify this?
- Given that 80% of your non-AD group has AD pathology, how do the proteomic changes in symptomatic AD (n=13) compare to AsymAD (n=11)? Does the spatial proteomics reveal why some individuals with Aβ/Tau pathology remain cognitively normal?
- You assign fractions based on GO enrichment (P3=nucleus, P2=mitochondria, S2=lysosomes/vesicles, S3=ER). Have you validated this with marker proteins (e.g., LAMP1 for lysosomes, TOM20 for mitochondria, KDEL for ER)? This would strengthen confidence in compartment assignment.
- You report 2,405 significant changes across all fractions (1,905 unique proteins). With 27 samples and 4 fractions, the per-fraction sample size is ~6–7 per group. How does this affect power to detect true fraction-specific effects? A power analysis or permutation test would address this.
- You pool Day0 samples by genotype within each timepoint (Methods, p. 7), meaning variation in degradation rates comes only from Day7 samples. This reduces the ability to detect genotype-specific degradation variance. Was this a design choice for statistical power, and how does it affect interpretation of the AD vs. WT degradation differences?
