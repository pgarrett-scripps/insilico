# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript develops a mathematically rigorous stochastic framework for SIR epidemics with regime-switching interventions and derives exact recursive formulas for joint distributions of extinction time and infection burden. The theoretical contributions are sound and well-presented. However, the empirical application contains a significant overclaim in the abstract and introduction regarding what the Luxembourg case study demonstrates. The authors specify rather than estimate switching mechanisms, yet the abstract and opening sections present the numerical results as if they evaluate 'the impact of epidemic control' in a causal sense. This framing misrepresents the conditional, model-based nature of the comparisons. The paper is otherwise technically solid but requires reframing of its empirical claims.

## Strengths
- The recursive formulation (Theorems 2–4) is mathematically sound and well-proven, reducing computational complexity from O(|S_T|³) to O(P³N²) by exploiting monotonicity of the susceptible compartment.
- The framework genuinely extends prior Markov-modulated work by allowing phase-transition intensities to depend explicitly on epidemic state (s,i), enabling state-dependent policy escalation—a feature not present in Almaraz & Gómez-Corral (2018) and clearly distinguished in Section 1.
- Joint analysis of extinction time and infection burden via mixed moments (Theorem 4) and covariances is a useful contribution; reporting Corr(T, C_I) across scenarios (Section 3.6) adds value beyond marginal summaries.
- The mathematical exposition is clear, proofs are complete (Appendix A), and the level-wise recursion is elegantly derived and explained.
- Baseline calibration to Luxembourg mpox data is methodologically sound: Poisson observation model is justified (negative-binomial size parameter hits upper bound κ_max = 20000), Pearson dispersion = 0.79 supports parsimony, and rounding of continuous MLEs to integer initial conditions is transparently documented.

## Weaknesses
-  **HARD: Abstract overclaims causal evaluation.** The abstract states 'The results show how switching mechanisms affect both the total number of infected individuals and the extinction time' (line 11–12 of abstract). This language ('show how…affect') implies causal or empirical evaluation of intervention effects. However, the authors immediately qualify this in the next sentence: 'Since the switching mechanisms are specified rather than estimated from the intervention history, the results are conditional model-based comparisons rather than estimates of the historical effects of interventions in Luxembourg.' This qualification contradicts the headline claim. The abstract should lead with the conditional nature, not bury it. As written, a reader skimming the abstract will infer causal findings about mpox control in Luxembourg, which is false.
-  **HARD: Introduction misrepresents the empirical scope.** The introduction (Section 1, final paragraph) states: 'To illustrate the method, we use weekly mpox incidence data from Luxembourg to calibrate a baseline one-phase SIR model and then evaluate alternative intervention mechanisms conditionally on the fitted parameters.' This is accurate. However, the preceding sentence claims the framework 'permits full distributional comparisons of intervention mechanisms, including cases in which marginal means are similar but uncertainty or dependence differs.' While true in principle, the empirical section does not compare mechanisms estimated from data; it compares hypothetical scenarios with specified switching rates. The framing suggests the method can empirically evaluate competing interventions, but the case study only demonstrates conditional scenario comparison. This is a genuine limitation that should be foregrounded, not implied to be overcome.
-  **SOFT: Specification of scenario parameters lacks justification.** Section 3.5 states: 'The intervention-specific values used in Scenarios 1–4 are illustrative and are chosen to separate the effects of intervention intensity, intervention timing, direct immunity acquisition, and state-dependent escalation.' The choices are reasonable for pedagogical separation, but the paper does not justify why these particular parameter values (e.g., ψ = 0.05 for vaccination, λ_sd coefficients in Eq. 19) are plausible for Luxembourg or mpox. The authors acknowledge this ('should therefore be interpreted as conditional model comparisons, not as estimates of the historical effects'), but the lack of sensitivity analysis or range exploration weakens the illustrative power. A brief sensitivity check (e.g., varying ψ or the state-dependent threshold) would strengthen the case study.
-  **SOFT: Partial weeks in data not fully addressed.** Section 3.1 notes: 'Since the observation window starts on 16/06/2022 and ends on 13/09/2022, the first and last aggregated weeks are partial. These weeks were retained in the calibration.' The authors mention this as a 'minor limitation' in Section 4 but do not quantify its impact. Dropping or downweighting partial weeks, or re-running the baseline fit without them, would clarify whether this edge effect materially affects the fitted parameters (b̂, Ŝ(0), Î(0)) used in all downstream scenarios.
-  **SOFT: Numerical inversion method not validated.** The Abate–Whitt Euler inversion (Section 3) is applied with standard parameters (A ≈ 18.4, 15 initial terms, 11 Euler averaging terms) but no validation is provided. The authors note 'Numerical values that were non-finite or negative only because of inversion error were set to zero before plotting,' which is a post-hoc correction. A brief check (e.g., comparing inversion results to known closed-form densities for a simple scenario, or reporting relative error bounds) would increase confidence in the plotted densities.
-  **SOFT: Claim of 'exact' results requires qualification.** The paper repeatedly states results are 'exact up to numerical linear-system solution' (e.g., Section 2, final paragraph; Section 3). This is technically correct but may mislead readers unfamiliar with numerical linear algebra. Condition numbers of the matrices A_{s,i}(z) are not reported, and no error analysis is provided. For large N or P, ill-conditioning could accumulate. A brief note on numerical stability or a condition-number check for the Luxembourg case (N=63, P≤3) would be helpful.
-  **SOFT: State-dependent escalation function (Eq. 19) appears ad hoc.** The function λ^sd_{1,2}(s,i) = 0.01 + 0.40 i²/(i² + 6²) is presented as illustrative but its form is not motivated. Why a quadratic numerator and denominator? Why the constant 0.01 and coefficient 0.40? The authors state 'The coefficients and threshold in the state-dependent escalation function (19) are chosen so that the transition rate is low when the number of infectious individuals is small and increases smoothly as i increases,' but this describes the qualitative behavior, not the specific parameterization. Sensitivity to these choices is not explored.

## Questions
- In the abstract, can the authors reorder the sentences so that the conditional nature of the comparisons ('specified rather than estimated') appears in the first sentence, not as a qualifier after the headline claim?
- Section 3.1 fixes γ = 1/3 per week based on a 'pragmatic calibration assumption' rather than estimation from data. How sensitive are the scenario results (Figures 3–11) to this choice? A brief one-way sensitivity analysis (e.g., γ ∈ [1/4, 1/2]) would clarify robustness.
- For the state-dependent escalation function (Eq. 19), can the authors justify the specific form (quadratic numerator/denominator, constants 0.01 and 0.40) or provide a sensitivity analysis showing how the results in Scenario 4 change if the function is replaced by, e.g., a linear or logistic form?
- Can the authors quantify the impact of retaining partial weeks in the baseline calibration? Re-fitting the one-phase model excluding the first and last weeks would show whether edge effects materially change b̂, Ŝ(0), Î(0).
- The Abate–Whitt inversion is applied with fixed parameters (A ≈ 18.4, 15 terms, 11 Euler weights). For the Luxembourg case, can the authors report the relative error or validate the inversion against a simple closed-form benchmark (e.g., exponential or gamma density)?
- In Section 2.4, the authors note that computing the full set of coefficient transforms h^(n)_{s,i,p}(z) has cost O(P³N² + P²N³). For the Luxembourg case (N=63, P=3), can they report actual wall-clock times for computing all scenarios, to help readers assess computational feasibility for larger populations?
- The paper claims the framework allows 'policy escalation to react to the number of infectious individuals' (Introduction). However, the state-dependent mechanism in Scenario 4 is the only empirical example. Can the authors discuss or illustrate how the framework would handle other reactive policies, e.g., escalation triggered by cumulative infections or time-since-peak?
