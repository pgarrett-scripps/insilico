# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript develops a mathematically rigorous finite-population stochastic SIR framework with state-dependent Markovian regime switching and derives exact recursive formulas for joint distributional analysis of extinction time and infection burden. The theoretical contribution is sound and well-presented. However, the empirical validation is limited: the baseline model is calibrated to observed data, but all intervention scenarios are specified rather than estimated from the historical intervention record. This design choice is acknowledged but creates a fundamental limitation—the scenarios are conditional model comparisons, not causal evaluations of interventions. The lack of sensitivity analysis on switching parameters, the absence of robustness checks across different baseline calibrations, and the reliance on a single small outbreak (N=63, 14 weeks, 55 total cases) limit the empirical support for the framework's practical utility. The design does not rule out the possibility that conclusions are sensitive to unexamined parameter choices or that the framework's distributional insights generalize beyond this illustrative case.

## Strengths
- The theoretical framework is mathematically sound: Theorem 2 establishes existence and uniqueness of the joint Laplace–Stieltjes transform–probability generating function via a well-defined recursive scheme (Eqs. 6–7), with proofs provided in Appendix A.
- The recursive formulation is computationally efficient: by exploiting monotonicity of the susceptible compartment, the method reduces O(|S_T|³) global system complexity to O(P³N² + P²N³) level-wise solves, a substantial practical advantage clearly explained in Section 2.4.
- The framework unifies several distributional quantities (extinction-time moments, infection-count distributions, mixed moments, conditional densities) within a single recursive scheme, enabling joint analysis of burden and duration rather than marginal summaries alone.
- State-dependent phase transitions are well-motivated: allowing λ_{p,p'}(s,i) to depend on current epidemic state (S,I) is a genuine extension beyond prior Markov-modulated work and permits representation of policy escalation responsive to infectious population.
- The mpox case study is transparent about its limitations: the authors explicitly state (Abstract, Section 3) that scenarios are 'conditional model-based comparisons rather than estimates of the historical effects of interventions in Luxembourg,' avoiding overclaiming.

## Weaknesses
-  HARD: The empirical validation does not test the framework's ability to support causal or comparative claims about interventions. All four scenarios (Scenarios 1–4) specify switching intensities and intervention effects rather than estimating them from the historical intervention record. The design therefore cannot distinguish whether observed differences in outcome distributions arise from the intervention mechanisms specified or from unexamined parameter choices. Specifically, the authors state (Section 3, p. 13): 'the switching intensities and intervention-specific effects are specified rather than estimated from the historical policy process.' This means no comparator intervention scenario is fitted to data; all are hypothetical. The claim that 'state-dependent escalation reduces the infection count by increasing the probability of strict control when the infectious population becomes large' (Section 3.5, p. 24) rests entirely on the specified functional form λ^sd_{1,2}(s,i) = 0.01 + 0.40 i²/(i² + 62), not on evidence that this form is optimal or even plausible given Luxembourg's actual policy.
-  HARD: No sensitivity analysis on switching parameters. The scenarios use fixed switching rates (q = 0.35 for early, q = 0.05 for delayed escalation; ψ = 0.05 for vaccination) without exploring how results change under reasonable perturbations. For instance, if q = 0.35 were instead 0.25 or 0.50, would the ranking of scenarios or the magnitude of differences in mean C_I and T remain stable? The design does not rule out the possibility that conclusions are artifacts of the particular parameter choices made.
-  HARD: Single baseline calibration. The one-phase SIR model is fitted once to the Luxembourg data (Section 3.1) with fixed γ = 1/3 per week (a pragmatic assumption, not estimated). All subsequent scenarios condition on this single calibration. The design does not test robustness to alternative baseline fits (e.g., γ estimated from data, or γ ∈ [0.2, 0.5]) or to different observation models. The authors note (p. 15) that the negative-binomial fit reached its upper bound κ_max = 20000, suggesting negligible overdispersion, but this does not rule out model misspecification or sensitivity to the fixed γ assumption.
-  HARD: No positive control or validation of the recursive method against independent benchmarks. The recursive formulas (Theorems 2–4) are mathematically proven, but the numerical implementation is not validated against an alternative method (e.g., Monte Carlo simulation, matrix exponentiation, or a published benchmark case). The authors provide basic consistency checks (Φ_{s,i,p}(0,1) = 1, Σ_n h^{(n)}_{s,i,p}(0) = 1) but these are necessary conditions, not sufficient validation of correctness. For a finite-population Markov chain with N = 63 and P = 3, a stochastic simulation or alternative solver could serve as a positive control.
-  SOFT: Limited scope of empirical illustration. The mpox outbreak in Luxembourg is small (N = 63, i_0 = 1, 14 weeks, 55 total cases) and represents a single, recent, well-controlled outbreak. The design does not test whether the framework's insights (e.g., strong positive correlation between T and C_I, or the differential effects of timing vs. intensity) generalize to larger, longer, or more severe outbreaks. The authors acknowledge this (Section 4, p. 27) but do not provide even a second illustrative case or synthetic scenario to probe robustness.
-  SOFT: No comparison to deterministic or simpler stochastic baselines. The paper does not show whether the distributional insights from the regime-switching framework differ meaningfully from those of a simpler model (e.g., a one-phase stochastic SIR, or a deterministic SIR with uncertainty propagated via perturbation). For instance, would a standard SIR with parameter uncertainty yield similar correlations Corr(T, C_I) ≈ 0.85–0.93 (Section 3.6)? The design does not isolate the added value of the regime-switching structure.
-  SOFT: Partial weeks in data. The authors note (Section 3.1, p. 15) that the first and last aggregated weeks are partial, retained to preserve the observation window. This may introduce edge effects in the baseline calibration, though the authors acknowledge it as 'a minor limitation.' No robustness check (e.g., excluding partial weeks, or re-fitting with full weeks only) is provided.
-  SOFT: Numerical inversion of Laplace–Stieltjes transforms. The extinction-time densities (Figures 4, 6, 8, 11) are obtained via the Abate–Whitt method (Section 3, p. 13). The authors set negative or non-finite values to zero 'before plotting' due to inversion error. No error bounds or convergence diagnostics are reported. For a public review, transparency about numerical accuracy would strengthen confidence in the displayed densities.

## Questions
- Can the authors provide a Monte Carlo simulation or alternative numerical solver (e.g., matrix exponentiation) as a positive control to validate the recursive implementation for a representative case (e.g., N = 63, P = 3, one scenario)?
- How sensitive are the scenario comparisons (Scenarios 1–4) to the specified switching rates and intervention effects? For instance, if q ∈ {0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.35} or ψ ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.10}, how do the rankings and magnitudes of differences in E[C_I] and E[T] change?
- How robust are the baseline calibration and subsequent scenario results to the fixed assumption γ = 1/3 per week? What if γ were estimated from the data or allowed to vary in a plausible range [0.2, 0.5]?
- Can the authors provide a second empirical illustration (e.g., a different outbreak, a larger population, or a synthetic scenario) to test whether the key findings (e.g., strong T–C_I correlation, differential effects of timing vs. intensity) generalize beyond the Luxembourg mpox case?
- Do the extinction-time densities (Figures 4, 6, 8, 11) obtained via Abate–Whitt inversion have quantified error bounds or convergence diagnostics? How many grid points and Euler terms were used, and how sensitive are the displayed curves to these choices?
- How would the scenario comparisons change if a simpler stochastic baseline (e.g., one-phase SIR without regime switching, or deterministic SIR with parameter uncertainty) were used as a comparator? Does the regime-switching structure materially alter the distributional insights?
- The state-dependent escalation function (Eq. 19, Section 3.5) is chosen to be 'low when the number of infectious individuals is small and increases smoothly as i increases.' What is the justification for this particular functional form, and how sensitive are the results to alternative forms (e.g., linear, exponential, threshold-based)?
