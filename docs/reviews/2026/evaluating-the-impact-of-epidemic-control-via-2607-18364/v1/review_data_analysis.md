# Statistics & Data-Analysis Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript develops a finite-population stochastic SIR model with Markovian regime switching and derives exact recursive formulas for joint distributions of extinction time and infection burden. The mathematical framework is sound and the recursive method is computationally efficient. The empirical application to Luxembourg mpox data is methodologically transparent but deliberately limited in scope: a one-phase baseline is calibrated via maximum likelihood, then alternative intervention scenarios are evaluated conditionally on fitted parameters rather than estimated from data. The statistical choices in calibration are reasonable and clearly disclosed, but the scenario analysis is illustrative rather than inferential, which is appropriately acknowledged. The main statistical limitation is that competing switching scenarios are not fitted to data, preventing empirical comparison of mechanisms; this is a design choice, not a flaw, but it constrains the practical impact of the work.

## Strengths
- The baseline calibration uses a principled maximum-likelihood approach with explicit observation models (Poisson and negative-binomial) and reports parameter estimates with approximate 95% Wald confidence intervals (bb=0.99 [0.80,1.21], bS(0)=61.88 [55.54,120.87], bI(0)=0.28 [0.06,1.12]), making the fitted model transparent and reproducible.
- The choice of Poisson over negative-binomial observation model is justified by diagnostic evidence: the negative-binomial size parameter reached its upper bound (κ̂=20000), indicating negligible overdispersion, and the Pearson dispersion statistic for the Poisson fit was D̂P=0.79, providing no evidence of residual overdispersion (Section 3.1).
- The rounding of continuous MLEs to integer initial conditions (S(0)=62, I(0)=1) is explicitly disclosed and correctly interpreted as a convention for the finite-state Markov chain rather than a separate parameter estimate, avoiding confusion about the source of these values.
- The recursive method for computing joint transforms and moments is computationally efficient (O(P³N²+P²N³) vs O(|ST|³) for dense global solve), and the paper provides explicit checks on implementation (Φs,i,p(0,1)=1, Σh(n)s,i,p(0)=1, Σ P(CI=c)=1) that can be verified by readers.
- The scenario analysis appropriately separates effects: Scenario 1 isolates transmission reduction, Scenario 2 compares intervention timing, Scenario 3 adds vaccination, and Scenario 4 demonstrates state-dependent escalation. The cross-scenario synthesis reports mixed moments (covariance between T and CI) showing strong positive correlation (0.85–0.93), which adds value beyond marginal distributions.
- The paper transparently discloses that switching intensities and intervention effects are specified rather than estimated, and explicitly states 'the results are conditional model-based comparisons rather than estimates of the historical effects of interventions in Luxembourg' (Abstract and Section 3), preventing misinterpretation.

## Weaknesses
- The empirical application does not fit the regime-switching model to data; instead, a one-phase baseline is calibrated and then alternative mechanisms are evaluated conditionally on fitted parameters. This design choice is disclosed but limits the inferential scope: competing intervention scenarios cannot be empirically ranked, and the practical utility of the framework for real policy evaluation remains undemonstrated (Section 3 and Discussion).
- The fixed infectious period (γ=1/3 per week, corresponding to 3 weeks) is stated as 'a pragmatic calibration assumption rather than as a direct estimate' (Section 3.1), but no sensitivity analysis is provided to show how results change if this assumption is relaxed. Given that the reported confidence interval for bI(0) is wide [0.06,1.12], sensitivity to the recovery rate would be informative.
- The numerical inversion of the Laplace–Stieltjes transform for extinction-time densities uses the Abate–Whitt method with standard parameters (A≈18.4, 15 initial terms, 11 Euler averaging terms). No validation of inversion accuracy is provided (e.g., comparison to analytical results for simple cases, or sensitivity to the number of terms). The statement that 'numerical values that were non-finite or negative only because of inversion error were set to zero before plotting' (Section 3) indicates post-hoc correction of numerical artifacts, which could mask convergence issues.
- The mpox data consist of only K=14 weekly observations (16 June to 13 September 2022), which is a small sample for calibration. The fitted model 'provides a reasonable parsimonious representation of the overall epidemic curve' but 'main discrepancies concern local week-to-week fluctuations' (Section 3.1 and Figure 2). No formal goodness-of-fit test (e.g., likelihood-ratio test against a saturated model, or simulation-based posterior predictive check) is reported; the assessment is visual only.
- The paper acknowledges that 'the first and last aggregated weeks in the mpox incidence series are partial weeks' and that they 'were retained to preserve the complete observation window' but notes this 'may introduce a small edge effect in the baseline calibration' (Discussion). No analysis of this edge effect is provided; it is unclear whether excluding these weeks would materially change the fitted parameters or subsequent scenario results.
- The integer rounding of continuous MLEs (S(0)=62 from 61.88, I(0)=1 from 0.28) is necessary for the finite-state model but introduces a small discontinuity. The impact of this rounding on the reported distributional quantities is not quantified; it is unclear whether results are robust to alternative rounding rules (e.g., rounding I(0) to 0 and checking if the absorbing state is reached immediately).
- The scenario parameters (transmission reductions of 25% and 50%, switching rates q=0.35 and q=0.05, vaccination rate ψ=0.05, state-dependent escalation coefficients in Eq. 19) are described as 'illustrative' and 'chosen to separate effects' (Section 3.1). No justification is provided for these specific values, and no sensitivity analysis explores how results change across a range of plausible intervention intensities. The practical relevance of the scenarios to real policy decisions is therefore unclear.

## Questions
- How sensitive are the reported distributional quantities (means, standard deviations, correlations of T and CI) to the fixed infectious period γ=1/3? A one-way sensitivity analysis varying γ over a plausible range (e.g., 1/4 to 1/2 per week) would strengthen confidence in the baseline calibration.
- The Abate–Whitt inversion of the Laplace–Stieltjes transform is applied to compute extinction-time densities (Figures 4, 6, 8, 11). Can the authors provide validation of this inversion for a simple case (e.g., a one-phase SIR model with known analytical solution) to demonstrate accuracy? Alternatively, how sensitive are the reported density shapes to the number of inversion terms?
- The mpox data consist of only 14 weekly observations. Did the authors consider fitting a richer model (e.g., SEIR with latency, or a two-phase model with an early intervention phase) to the same data to assess whether the one-phase baseline is adequate? A likelihood-ratio test or information criterion comparison would be informative.
- For the integer rounding of initial conditions (S(0)=62 from 61.88, I(0)=1 from 0.28), how do results change if I(0) is rounded to 0 and the process is initialized in the absorbing state? This would clarify whether the lower bound of one infectious individual materially affects the scenario comparisons.
- The state-dependent escalation function (Eq. 19) is λsd₁,₂(s,i)=0.01+0.40i²/(i²+62). How were the coefficients 0.01, 0.40, and 62 chosen? A sensitivity analysis varying these parameters would show whether the qualitative findings (e.g., state-dependent escalation reduces CI while leaving mean T nearly unchanged) are robust.
- In Scenario 4, the state-dependent escalation rate produces a mean CI of 28.50 (vs. 33.52 for constant delayed rate) and a mean T of 14.44 weeks (vs. 14.39 weeks). Given the small sample size and the width of the confidence intervals, is this difference in CI practically meaningful? A formal hypothesis test or equivalence test would clarify.
- The paper states that 'the switching intensities and intervention effects are specified rather than estimated from the intervention history' (Abstract). Could the authors outline a path forward for fitting the full regime-switching model to richer data (e.g., daily case counts, hospitalization data, documented intervention dates)? This would help readers understand the practical applicability of the framework.
