# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript makes several load-bearing claims about bacterial survival strategy and drug resistance mechanisms that are not adequately supported by the evidence presented. The core claim—that Mtb 'favours mutations that protect them from a drug while making the drug ineffective' and strikes a 'balance between fitness and drug resistance'—is presented as a discovered universal strategy, but the evidence is entirely correlational and observational. The bioinformatics predictions (conservation, stability, binding affinity) are computational inferences, not experimental validation of mechanism. The ranking of mutations into 'lethal/moderate/mild/neutral' categories conflates multiple independent predictions without establishing that this categorization reflects actual bacterial fitness costs or evolutionary selection. The abstract and title claim to 'uncover' and 'probe into' mechanisms and strategies, but the study is a retrospective analysis of published mutation frequencies paired with in silico predictions—it cannot establish causation, mechanism, or that the observed mutation pattern reflects active bacterial strategy rather than sampling bias, reporting bias, or neutral drift. Critical hedging is absent from headline claims.

## Strengths
- The dataset is substantial: 821 non-synonymous mutations from 31,073 isolates across 149 studies spanning 30 years, providing a valuable global compilation of mutation prevalence data.
- The geographic stratification by WHO region is useful and reveals that mutation patterns vary regionally, with higher burden in Western Pacific and South-East Asia.
- The bioinformatics pipeline is comprehensive, employing multiple tools (ConSurf, SIFT, PROVEAN, PolyPhen-2, I-MUTANT 3.0, mCSM, AutoDock) for cross-validation of predictions on conservation, function, stability, and binding affinity.
- The observation that frequently occurring mutations (S315T in katG, S450L in rpoB, M306V in embB) are located in non-conserved regions is interesting and well-documented with frequency data.
- The atlas of mutations (Supplementary Table S3) is a useful reference resource for the field.

## Weaknesses
- 
**HARD: Title and abstract claim to 'uncover' and 'probe into' mechanisms and survival strategies, but the study is observational and correlational.** The title states 'Uncovering the root of drug resistance and bacterial survival strategies' and the abstract claims 'we uncover a universal strategy of drug resistance.' However, the evidence is: (1) retrospective mutation frequency data from published studies, (2) computational predictions of protein properties, and (3) correlational observation that frequent mutations tend to be in non-conserved regions with mild effects. None of this establishes mechanism or proves that bacteria actively 'favour' or 'prefer' mutations—these are inferences from frequency patterns that could equally reflect sampling bias, publication bias, or neutral drift. The causal language ('bacteria favour mutations,' 'bacteria may evolve,' 'bacteria strike a balance') is not licensed by observational data without experimental manipulation or mechanistic validation.
- 
**HARD: The core hypothesis—that Mtb balances fitness and drug resistance—is not tested; only correlational patterns are observed.** The authors state: 'we hypothesise that bacteria favour mutations that protect them from a drug while making the drug ineffective' (p. 5). The evidence offered is: (1) frequent mutations are in non-conserved regions (Fig. 9a: 66.5% in highly conserved, but frequent ones in variable regions), (2) frequent mutations cause 'mild' effects on stability and function (Table 1, Fig. 11), and (3) all mutations reduce drug binding (Fig. 12). But this pattern does not prove bacteria 'favour' or 'prefer' these mutations. The study shows correlation (frequent mutations happen to have mild effects), not causation or selection. No fitness assay, no experimental evolution, no in vivo competition, no genetic manipulation is presented. The authors acknowledge compensatory mutations exist (p. 28) but do not measure them. The claim that this pattern reflects an 'evolutionary strategy' or 'guided evolutionary pathway' (p. 28) is speculative inference from frequency data.
- 
**HARD: The ranking of mutations into lethal/moderate/mild/neutral categories is presented as a validated classification, but it is an ad hoc consensus of multiple independent computational predictions without empirical validation.** Table 2 and the text (p. 25) state: 'Out of 821 variations, 340 were identified as lethal, 284 as moderate, 185 as mild and 12 as neutral.' The method (p. 25) states: 'Functionally deleterious mutations that compromise residue conservation with reduced structural stability and binding affinity were categorised lethal.' However, this categorization is based on: (1) SIFT/PROVEAN/PolyPhen-2 predictions of functional impact (sequence-based or structure-based inference, not measurement), (2) I-MUTANT 3.0 and mCSM predictions of stability change (machine learning on training sets, not experimental validation), and (3) AutoDock binding energy calculations (docking is a prediction, not a measurement of actual binding). No experimental data (growth assays, fitness measurements, binding kinetics, structural validation) is provided to show that mutations predicted as 'lethal' actually reduce bacterial fitness or that 'mild' mutations preserve it. The authors then use this unvalidated ranking to argue that Mtb 'prefers mild mutations' (p. 28), but the ranking itself is not empirically grounded.
- 
**HARD: The claim that mutations in non-conserved regions indicate a bacterial strategy is not supported; it could reflect ascertainment bias.** The authors state (p. 19): 'It was interesting to observe that mutations occurring more frequently were not present in the highly conserved regions in the first-line drug targets but in the highly variable regions' and conclude (p. 27): 'This means Mtb usually escape from drug action by mutating the variable region compared to conserved.' However, this pattern could arise from: (1) mutations in highly conserved regions are deleterious and are selected against (true), (2) mutations in highly conserved regions are less likely to be detected or reported (reporting bias), or (3) mutations in highly conserved regions are less likely to confer resistance and thus are not associated with resistant isolates in the dataset (ascertainment bias—the dataset is enriched for resistant isolates, not a random sample of all mutations). The authors do not distinguish between these explanations. The claim that this pattern reflects a 'strategy' or 'preference' is not justified.
- 
**HARD: The abstract claims 'we found that the most prevalent mutations occur in non-conserved areas in the drug binding region indicating a choice of a less dramatic level of change in target protein function and stability,' but this is an interpretation, not an observation.** The observation is that frequent mutations are in non-conserved regions and have mild effects on stability. The interpretation that this indicates a 'choice' or 'strategy' is not supported by the data. Bacteria do not 'choose' mutations; mutations arise randomly and are selected if they confer resistance. The authors cannot distinguish between: (1) bacteria selecting for mild mutations (active strategy), (2) mild mutations being more likely to confer resistance without lethal fitness cost (passive selection), or (3) the observed pattern being an artifact of how the dataset was constructed (enriched for resistant isolates with viable mutations). The language 'indicating a choice' is overclaimed.
- 
**HARD: The claim of a 'universal strategy' is not supported by the data.** The abstract and discussion (p. 27) state: 'we uncovered a universal strategy of drug resistance and bacterial survival: Mtb favours less harmful mutations in the drug binding region without compromising conservancy while destabilising the drugs, thus striking a balance between fitness and drug resistance.' However: (1) the pattern is observed in a dataset of published mutations from resistant isolates, not a random or representative sample of all mutations in Mtb; (2) the pattern differs across drugs (katG and rpoB show one highly frequent mutation, pncA shows scattered mutations, embB shows multiple moderate-frequency mutations—p. 27), so there is no single 'universal' strategy; (3) the interpretation that this reflects an active 'balance' or 'strategy' is not mechanistically validated. The claim of universality is not justified.
- 
**SOFT: The study conflates 'frequent mutations' with 'favoured mutations' without accounting for alternative explanations.** Frequent mutations in the dataset could reflect: (1) higher mutation rate at certain sites (hotspots), (2) higher fitness of certain mutations, (3) higher transmissibility of strains with certain mutations, (4) publication bias (studies more likely to report common mutations), or (5) sampling bias (isolates from high-burden regions more likely to have certain mutations). The authors observe that frequent mutations are in non-conserved regions and have mild effects, but do not test whether this is because bacteria 'favour' them or because other mutations are lethal and thus absent from the resistant isolate population. The causal language ('bacteria favour,' 'bacteria prefer') should be replaced with correlational language ('frequent mutations tend to,' 'are associated with').
- 
**SOFT: The claim that the study reveals 'how bacteria may evolve drug resistance mechanisms' (abstract) is overstated given that the study is observational and correlational.** The abstract states: 'we quantify the impact of mutations on both bacterial function and drug binding affinity to get to the root of drug resistance revealing how bacteria may evolve an arsenal of mutations towards an optimal survival strategy.' The study quantifies computational predictions of impact, not actual impact. The phrase 'how bacteria may evolve' suggests mechanistic insight, but the study only shows that frequent mutations have certain predicted properties. It does not show how bacteria evolve these mutations (mutation rate, selection pressure, genetic drift, compensatory mutations, etc.). The word 'may' provides some hedging, but the overall framing overstates the mechanistic insight.
- 
**SOFT: The bioinformatics predictions are not validated against experimental data.** The study uses multiple computational tools to predict functional impact, stability change, and binding affinity, but does not validate these predictions against experimental measurements (e.g., enzyme kinetics, protein stability assays, binding assays, growth curves, fitness measurements). The authors note (p. 27) that 'Analysing the large amount of data (821 drug resistant mutations) by wet-lab experiments would be difficult, time-consuming, and expensive,' which is true, but this means the study is hypothesis-generating, not hypothesis-testing. The predictions should be presented as such, not as established facts. For example, the statement 'All mutations reduced drug binding affinity' (abstract) is based on AutoDock docking scores, not experimental binding measurements.
- 
**SOFT: The claim that 'most prevalent mutations occur in non-conserved areas' is not precisely stated.** The data (Fig. 9a) show that 66.5% of all 821 mutations are in highly conserved regions, but the most prevalent mutations (by frequency) are in variable regions. The statement should clarify that this refers to the most frequent mutations, not most mutations overall. The current phrasing could mislead readers into thinking that most mutations are in non-conserved regions, which is false.
- 
**SOFT: The geographic analysis is descriptive but does not explain regional differences.** The authors note (p. 11) that mutation frequency varies by WHO region and speculate that 'Prolonged exposure to drugs with probable variable commitment to treatment regimen, weather conditions and poor performance of some sequence-based diagnostic testing could be partly responsible for the differences.' However, these are speculations without data. The authors also note (p. 11) that 'the available data were not granular enough to ascertain the probable reason for the differences in mutation count in different regions,' which is honest but limits the conclusions that can be drawn.
- 
**SOFT: The claim that the study provides 'a strong foundation for designing efficacious drugs and therapies' (abstract) is not supported by the evidence.** The study identifies frequent mutations and their predicted properties, but does not propose new drugs, inhibitors, or therapeutic strategies. The authors state (p. 28) that 'When designing new drugs, our methodology can help in predicting the impact of mutations on their respective drug targets for developing better drugs for TB treatment,' but this is a potential application, not a demonstrated outcome. The abstract should be more cautious about the practical implications.

## Questions
- 
1. Can the authors provide experimental validation (e.g., fitness assays, binding kinetics, enzyme activity measurements) for at least a subset of the mutations ranked as 'lethal,' 'moderate,' 'mild,' or 'neutral'? Without this, the ranking is an unvalidated computational inference.
- 
2. How do the authors distinguish between mutations that are 'favoured' by bacteria (active selection) versus mutations that happen to be frequent because they are the only viable mutations (passive selection)? What evidence supports the interpretation of active strategy rather than passive outcome?
- 
3. The dataset is enriched for resistant isolates from published studies. How might this ascertainment bias affect the observed pattern that frequent mutations are in non-conserved regions? Could mutations in conserved regions be underrepresented because they are lethal and thus absent from resistant isolates, rather than because bacteria 'avoid' them?
- 
4. The authors claim a 'universal strategy,' but the mutation patterns differ across the four drugs (katG and rpoB show one dominant mutation, pncA shows scattered mutations, embB shows multiple moderate-frequency mutations). How do these differences support a universal strategy? Should the claim be qualified to describe drug-specific strategies?
- 
5. The study relies on computational predictions of protein stability and binding affinity. How sensitive are the conclusions to the choice of prediction tools? Have the authors tested whether different tools or different parameter settings would change the ranking of mutations into lethal/moderate/mild/neutral categories?
- 
6. The authors mention compensatory mutations (p. 28) as a mechanism that might restore fitness in strains with deleterious mutations, but do not measure them. How would including compensatory mutations change the interpretation of which mutations are truly 'lethal' or 'mild'?
- 
7. The abstract states 'All mutations reduced drug binding affinity' (based on AutoDock docking scores). Is this statement accurate? Table 1 shows binding energy values, but are all of them more negative (weaker binding) than wild-type? Please provide the wild-type binding energies for all four drugs for comparison.
- 
8. The claim that Mtb 'strikes a balance between fitness and drug resistance' implies an optimization process. What evidence supports this interpretation over simpler explanations (e.g., mutations that are both viable and resistance-conferring are more likely to be observed)?
- 
9. How do the authors account for publication bias? Studies are more likely to report common mutations or mutations with clear phenotypes. Could the observed frequency distribution reflect publication bias rather than true prevalence in the bacterial population?
- 
10. The study is based on mutations reported over 30 years (1988–2018). Have mutation patterns changed over time? If so, does this suggest evolution of the 'strategy' or just changes in surveillance and reporting?
