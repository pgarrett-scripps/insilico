# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

SCORE: 3
CONFIDENCE: 4

**Overall take.** This is a genuinely useful methods paper with a strong empirical core: the systematic comparison of proteases and labeling strategies is well-designed, and the TMT charge-compensation finding for acidic acylations is a real and mechanistically plausible contribution. The central problem is that the abstract and several headline claims outrun the evidence presented — most seriously, the "dark epigenome" framing and the quantitative claims about NAM treatment rest on analyses that are not fully shown or are confounded by the authors' own missed-cleavage data. With requalification of the abstract and a few added analyses, this would be a solid minor-revision paper; as written, the overclaims are substantial enough to require revision.

**Strengths.** The systematic 10-condition comparison with n=4 replicates per condition is a genuine methodological contribution that the field will use. The TMT charge-compensation mechanism for succinylation/glutarylation detection is well-reasoned and supported by the fragmentation data shown. The RIPUP dual-protease workflow is practical, fast, and clearly described with cost analysis.

**Weaknesses (load-bearing).**

**1. The "dark epigenome" claim is over-strong relative to the evidence.** The abstract states that TMT "reveals 58 succinylation and 31 glutarylation sites – a 'dark epigenome' largely undetected by propionylation-based methods." The evidence is a single cell line (HEK293T), a single TMT condition (Arg-C Ultra and r-Chymotrypsin at one enzyme ratio), and no direct comparison showing these sites are *undetectable* by propionylation — only that they were *not detected* in the propionylated conditions tested. The authors never show that the same peptides, when propionylated, fail to ionize or fragment; they infer this from the charge-compensation mechanism. The alternative explanation — that the propionylated conditions simply had different peptide coverage in those regions — is not excluded. The claim should be requalified to "detected here with TMT but not in the propionylated conditions tested" rather than "largely undetected by propionylation-based methods." This is a SOFT-to-HARD issue: the mechanism is plausible, but the "largely undetected" generalization exceeds the single-cell-line, single-condition evidence.

**2. The NAM quantitative analysis is presented as establishing dose-dependent PTM changes, but the authors' own missed-cleavage data undermine the interpretation.** The authors report 112 significant peptidoform changes with 88 increasing with NAM dose, and present this as evidence of RIPUP's quantitative capacity. However, they also report 259 peptidoforms detected *exclusively* in NAM-treated samples and acknowledge "NAM induced dose-dependent missed cleavage redistribution." This means the denominator of detectable peptidoforms shifts with treatment, and the "increases" could reflect altered protease accessibility rather than true abundance changes. The authors explicitly reject the standard peptide-family-ratio approach because of this, but then proceed with per-peptidoform intensity normalization that does not correct for the same problem. The alternative explanation — that NAM-induced PTM changes alter Arg-C Ultra cleavage efficiency, producing new peptidoforms that are not more abundant but merely newly detectable — is not excluded. The claim "RIPUP quantified 112 statistically significant peptidoforms" is statistically valid but the biological interpretation ("predominantly increasing with NAM dose") is over-strong. This is HARD as worded in the abstract.

**3. The claim that TMT "improves PTM coverage and quantitative accuracy" (abstract) is not established for quantitative accuracy.** The paper demonstrates improved *identification* of certain PTM classes with TMT, but provides no quantitative accuracy data — no spike-in, no known-ratio experiment, no comparison of TMT-quantified ratios against a ground truth. The NAM experiment was done with unlabeled peptides, not TMT. The quantitative accuracy claim is unsupported by any evidence in the manuscript. This is HARD: the word "quantitative" in the abstract's first sentence is not backed by any quantitative validation experiment.

**Sweep.**

- The "first to" claim is absent, which is good, but the novelty framing ("revealing a 'dark epigenome'") implies discovery status for succinylation/glutarylation that is not supported — these modifications are known; the contribution is methodological detection, not discovery of new PTM classes.
- The claim that "TMT's tertiary amine provides charge compensation that rescues the ionization of negatively charged acylations" is presented as established mechanism but is inferred from detection patterns, not directly measured (e.g., no comparison of ionization efficiency of the same succinylated peptide with and without TMT).
- The missed-cleavage motif analysis (SI Figures S6–S7) is presented as a novel finding ("to our knowledge, this is the first report") but the analysis is descriptive and the sample size for motif enrichment is not stated; the claim should be hedged.
- The rat hippocampal data are presented as proof-of-concept with n=5, but the PTM counts are reported without any assessment of whether the 3-hour workflow preserved labile modifications (e.g., phosphorylation) — the authors do not compare against a slower, gentler workflow.
- The "informative histone peptides" (IHP) definition is reasonable but the comparison of IHP proportions across conditions is confounded by the different total peptide counts; the proportion metric rewards conditions with fewer total identifications.
- The claim that "TMT-labeling yielded ~2.3-fold more fully cleaved peptides with Arg-C Ultra than the established 'Trypsin + Prop' approach" is presented without noting that the Trypsin search was run with R-only cleavage specificity, which artificially deflates the Trypsin count by excluding K-cleaved peptides — the authors acknowledge this in the text but the headline number is still misleading.
- The succinylation site counts (58 and 31) are reported without FDR or localization confidence metrics at the site level; the authors report PSM-level FDR but not site-level localization probability, which is the standard for PTM site claims.
- The "dose-response concordance" r-values (0.803 and 0.791) are presented as evidence of quantitative reliability, but these are correlations between two contrasts from the same experiment, not independent validations — they would be high even if the method were systematically biased.

**Questions.**

1. Can the authors show, for a single succinylated peptide detected with TMT, the same peptide's behavior when propionylated — either its absence from the propionylated dataset or its failure to ionize — to directly support the charge-compensation mechanism rather than inferring it?
2. For the NAM experiment, can the authors report the same significant peptidoform list restricted to peptidoforms detected in all three dose groups (no imputation), and state how many of the 112 significant changes survive this restriction?
3. What is the site-level localization confidence (e.g., Ascore or equivalent) for the 58 succinylation and 31 glutarylation sites, and how many sites have flanking ions on both sides of the modified residue?
4. The abstract claims "quantitative accuracy" — is there any validation experiment (spike-in, known-ratio, or comparison against an orthogonal method) that supports this term, or should it be replaced with "quantitative capacity"?
