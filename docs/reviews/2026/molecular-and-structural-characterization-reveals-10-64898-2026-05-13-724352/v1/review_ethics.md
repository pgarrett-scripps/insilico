# Ethics & Compliance Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript is a careful, well-resourced characterization of EVs from WT and PSEN1-mutant cerebrocortical organoids, and the purification pipeline and multi-modal analysis are genuinely useful. My remit is ethics and compliance, and there the paper has one hard gap: the human-derived iPSC lines are described but no ethics approval or donor-consent statement is given anywhere. Scientifically, the two most load-bearing claims — EV-mediated transfer of pathology in co-culture, and the presence of amyloid fibrils in AD EVs — are each supported by evidence that admits a plausible alternative explanation the design does not exclude. These are fixable with additional controls and quantification, hence major revision rather than rejection.

## Strengths
- The EV purification workflow (TFF + size-exclusion) is clearly described and validated with appropriate negative fractions (permeate, HMW protein) and a calnexin control.
- The authors explicitly hedge their own interpretive claims (e.g., fibrils "may result from co-purification"), which is honest reporting.
- Funding is fully disclosed, and a detailed competing-interest statement is provided for the two senior authors.

## Weaknesses
- The co-culture experiment does not isolate EV-mediated transfer from soluble-factor transfer. The central claim of Figure 7 — that co-culturing AD with WT organoids transfers endosomal material and increases WT Aβ signal — rests on Aco-650 signal appearing in WT organoids and increased Aβ puncta. But the two organoids share the same medium, and Aco-650 is a membrane dye that can leak from the stained AD organoid into the medium and be taken up by the WT organoid; likewise, soluble Aβ secreted by the AD organoid can diffuse to the WT organoid. The "several rinses" do not exclude this. The same result would be produced by dye and Aβ diffusion alone, with no EV involvement. What would settle it: a control where a WT organoid is cultured in conditioned medium from an Aco-650-stained AD organoid without physical co-culture, or a dye-only spike control, alongside the current co-culture condition.
- The morphological claim of multilamellar vesicles and luminal fibrils in AD EVs is descriptive, not quantitative, and the fibril identity is unverified. The evidence is cryo-TEM on 24 WT vs 65 AD EVs (Figure 4H), and the MLV difference is stated only as "not as common" in WT — no counting statistics are reported. The fibrillar material is not identified by immunogold or tomography, and the authors themselves concede it could be co-purifying contaminant rather than EV cargo. The claim that AD EVs contain amyloid fibrils therefore outruns the evidence, which shows fibrillar material in some micrographs of an EV-enriched fraction. What would settle it: MLV frequency quantified across all micrographs in both conditions, and immunogold labeling (e.g., against Aβ or tau) or cryo-ET to identify the fibrils.
- The ThT signal is interpreted as amyloid content without excluding non-amyloid binders. The ~25-fold ThT signal in EV isolates (Figure 6A) is attributed to amyloid, but the authors themselves cite that ThT binds RNA and other biological polymers, and the EV isolates are not shown to be free of such material. WT and AD EVs give equally high signal, so this is not disease-specific. What would settle it: a competition control with a known amyloid ligand, or loss of signal after proteinase treatment.
- No ethics approval or donor-consent statement for the human-derived iPSC lines. The lines are described as "derived from a male donor" and obtained from the New York Stem Cell Foundation, but the manuscript contains no statement of ethics approval for the original derivation or of donor consent for use. Silence is not compliance here; the approval and consent statement must be stated, even if the original derivation is covered by the cited Paquet et al. (2016) protocol. (HARD)
- The competing-interest statement covers only J.W.K. and S.A.L.; there is no explicit sentence that the remaining authors declare no competing interests. (SOFT — request an explicit statement covering all authors.)
- No clinical-trial, animal-research, or dual-use concerns arise from this work.

## Questions
- What ethics approval and donor-consent arrangements cover the derivation and use of the hiPSC lines, and can the authors cite the approval or protocol identifier?
- Can the authors state explicitly that all co-authors not named in the competing-interest section declare no competing interests?
