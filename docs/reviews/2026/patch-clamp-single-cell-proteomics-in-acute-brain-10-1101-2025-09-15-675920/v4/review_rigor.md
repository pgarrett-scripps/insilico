## Strengths

1. The authors explicitly include and analyze failed retrievals (torn, no-gigaseal) rather than discarding them, which is a genuine methodological contribution for a technique where sample loss is common.
2. The framework distinguishing quantitative yield (protein count) from qualitative biological integrity (synaptic enrichment) is a useful conceptual advance that others in the field will likely adopt.
3. The authors are candid about limitations throughout, including the small sample size and the compartmental bias of soma retrieval.

## Weaknesses

### Load-bearing

**1. The central quantitative claim — capacitance correlates with protein identifications — rests on n=3.** The regression reported (F = 1577, p < 0.05, adjusted R² = 0.998) is computed on three data points. With n=3, the p-value is driven entirely by the spread of the points, and any single outlier would overturn the result. The authors do not report confidence intervals on the slope or intercept, and the adjusted R² of 0.998 is suspiciously high for biological data — it suggests the three points are nearly collinear, which is exactly what you would expect from a small sample rather than a robust relationship. The claim “providing a means of linking soma size to proteome recovery” is therefore over-strong. What would settle it: report the same regression with the torn neurons included (which would test whether the relationship holds across retrieval quality), or add bootstrap confidence intervals on the slope. If the relationship only holds for gigaseal-preserved retrievals, that is a different and more interesting claim — but it needs to be stated as such.

**2. The claim that spike integrity during retrieval is “associated with broader synaptic enrichment” is based on three individual neurons, not a comparison.** The authors compare neuron #4 (stable spiking, broad SynGO enrichment), neuron #7 (reduced amplitude, fewer unique BP terms), and neuron #6 (single spike, no synaptic signaling enrichment). This is a case series, not a test of association. The authors do not quantify the relationship between any measure of spike integrity (amplitude, number of spikes, threshold) and any measure of synaptic enrichment (number of enriched terms, enrichment score). The claim in the abstract — “Preservation of neuronal spiking during relocation tended to be associated with broader synaptic enrichment” — is hedged with “tended,” which is appropriate, but the body text goes further: “the physiological condition of soma retrieval (reflected in the integrity of neuronal spiking during relocation), may be associated with the recovery of synaptic proteins.” That is a hypothesis, not a finding. What would settle it: a scatterplot of spike amplitude or spike count against the number of enriched SynGO terms across all neurons where spiking was assessed, with the correlation reported. Without that, the claim is illustrative, not evidential.

**3. The PCA clustering is presented as evidence that “comprehensive analysis can distinguish between high- and low-context retrievals,” but the clustering is not validated against any independent measure.** The authors note that torn neurons cluster apart, and that neuron #6 (gigaseal but poor spiking) clusters with no-gigaseal neurons. This is consistent with their framework, but PCA will always produce clusters — the question is whether those clusters correspond to retrieval quality or to something else, such as total protein count (which is confounded with retrieval quality). The authors do not report the loadings of the principal components, so it is unclear whether the separation is driven by the presence/absence of specific synaptic proteins or simply by overall proteome size. What would settle it: report the top contributing proteins to PC1 and PC2, and show whether the clustering persists after normalizing for total protein identifications.

### Sweep

- The claim that “retrieval loss decouples proteomic measurements from electrophysiology recordings” is supported by the absence of correlation between in situ capacitance/RM and protein identifications (n=6), but the authors do not report the correlation coefficient or confidence interval — only “p > 0.05” — which is insufficient to distinguish “no relationship” from “underpowered to detect one.”
- The statement that “25–50% of the soma could be lost over the course of collection” is presented as an estimate, but the basis for this range is not given; the capacitance changes in Figure 3C are the only evidence, and the authors do not show how those changes translate to a percentage of soma volume.
- The claim that “ion channel, GPCR, and transporter recovery did not always scale with electrophysiological success” is a negative finding that is not quantified — the authors do not report the number of channels/GPCRs/transporters per neuron in a table, so the reader cannot assess the variability they describe.
- The use of “shotgun” to describe indiscriminate collection is potentially confusing, since “shotgun” in proteomics usually refers to bottom-up proteomics; the authors should clarify that they mean indiscriminate sample inclusion, not a shotgun proteomics method.
- The SynGO analysis is performed per neuron, but the authors do not report how many proteins were mapped to SynGO terms for each neuron — without this, it is unclear whether the absence of enrichment in neuron #6 reflects biological loss or simply fewer mapped proteins.
- The statement that “neuron #1 yielded an unusually large proteome and was located near the slice surface” is presented as a possible explanation, but the authors do not test whether proximity to the slice surface correlates with protein yield across all neurons — this is a testable hypothesis that would strengthen the paper.

## Questions

1. For the capacitance–protein identification regression (Figure 3D), what are the 95% confidence intervals on the slope and intercept, and does the relationship hold if the torn neurons (#11, #12) are included in the analysis?
2. Can you report the top 20 protein loadings for PC1 and PC2 in Figure 6A, and show whether the clustering persists after normalizing each neuron’s proteome to total protein count?
3. What is the quantitative relationship between spike integrity (e.g., number of spikes, mean amplitude) and the number of enriched SynGO terms across all neurons where spiking was assessed — is there a correlation, and what is its magnitude?
4. How was the “25–50% soma loss” estimate derived from the capacitance measurements in Figure 3C — what conversion factor was used, and what assumptions does it rest on?

## Overall

This is a useful proof-of-concept that introduces a sensible framework for interpreting patch-SCP outcomes, and the decision to analyze all retrievals rather than only successful ones is a genuine methodological contribution. However, the headline claims — that capacitance predicts proteome yield and that spike integrity is associated with synaptic enrichment — rest on n=3 and n=3 respectively, which is too thin to support the strength of the wording in the abstract. The paper would be stronger if the authors either expanded the dataset or systematically requalified the claims as hypotheses. The framework itself is sound and worth publishing; the specific quantitative claims need either more data or more hedging.
