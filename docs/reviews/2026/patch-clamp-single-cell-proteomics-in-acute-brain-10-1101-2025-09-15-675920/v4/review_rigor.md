# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
The paper reports a small-scale exploratory proof-of-concept study (n=12 neurons, only 3 with gigaseal-preserved retrieval). The central load-bearing claim — that monitoring electrophysiological properties during soma retrieval ('gigaseal preservation') provides a quantitative bridge between proteomics and electrophysiology — is supported by the data from those 3 neurons, but it is presented with far stronger causal and generalising language than the design licenses. The 'shotgun' indiscriminate-collection strategy is a sensible exploratory tactic, but the authors then make comparative claims about retrieval categories that rest on 2–4 neurons per bin, without any statistical test of difference or control for multiple comparisons, and they do not report the negative result that most retrieval attempts (9/12) did NOT preserve the gigaseal. The abstract states that 'patch-clamp single-cell proteomics can be used to assess soma retrieval' — this is not a claim that is established by the data so much as a restatement of what they did; the more interesting claim that gigaseal-preserved retrieval 'tended to be associated with broader synaptic enrichment' is supported only by visual inspection of one neuron per category. I assign this a MAJOR REVISION score of 3, not reject, because the core idea — monitoring electrophysiology during retrieval — is methodologically interesting and the data are honestly reported as exploratory. However the conclusions need substantial requalification, the small-n limitations must be stated prominently rather than buried, and several claims in the abstract and discussion need to be downgraded from statements of fact to observations consistent with the data.

## Strengths
- The work addresses a genuine and underexplored problem in patch-SCP — that retrieval mechanics, not just in situ electrophysiology, shape what the mass spectrometer detects.
- The 'shotgun' indiscriminate-collection strategy is a sensible exploratory design for a small-scale methods-development study, and the honest reporting of all 12 attempts (including the 2 torn ones) is a methodological strength.
- The use of SynGO for compartment-aware annotation is appropriate and applied in a consistent, reproducible manner.

## Weaknesses
- LOAD-BEARING WEAKNESS 1: Claim that 'gigaseal preservation provides a quantitative bridge between proteomics and electrophysiology' (abstract, Results) is based on n=3 neurons, with a linear regression (F=1577, p<0.05, adjusted R²=0.998) that has 1 degree of freedom. With three points, any two-species relationship (linear, log, or otherwise) is trivially overfitted, and the reported R² ≈ 1.0 is a mathematical artefact of fitting a line to two degrees of freedom, not evidence of a real correlation. The claim that capacitance 'correlated' with protein IDs is technically true in a narrow statistical sense but cannot bear the interpretive weight placed on it. The authors should state explicitly that this finding is a demonstration of feasibility with three observations, not a validated association.
- LOAD-BEARING WEAKNESS 2: The central comparative claim — that preservation of spiking during retrieval 'tended to be associated with broader synaptic enrichment' — rests on a visual comparison of three individual neurons (Neurons #4, #6, #7). No statistical test is applied to compare the SynGO term enrichment across the three groups (gigaseal-preserved, gigaseal-lost, no gigaseal, torn). The heatmap in Figure 6B and the UpSet plot in Figure 4B are displayed as exploratory visualisations, but the text states this as a finding. The sample size (1–3 neurons per condition) precludes any claim about association, tendency, or trend. The authors should either (a) present this as qualitative observation with explicit caveats about sample size, or (b) remove the comparative language.
- LOAD-BEARING WEAKNESS 3: The paper presents its 'shotgun' indiscriminate-collection strategy as a methodological innovation, but the key claim — that 'comprehensive analysis can distinguish between high- and low-context retrievals' (Results, PCA section) — is not established by the PCA plot (Figure 6A). The authors state that torn neurons 'clustered apart from all others', but with n=2 torn neurons and the PCA presumably performed on all protein IDs (not just informative features), this separation could reflect global loss of signal rather than meaningful biological categorisation. Moreover, the claim that 'variation in proteomic profiles across retrieval outcomes reflected differences in biological insight' (Discussion) is a circular argument if the same data (protein IDs) are used both to cluster and to interpret the clusters. The authors need to demonstrate that the clustering is driven by synaptic/functional proteins, not by trivial abundance differences.
- SOFT: Abstract states that 'preservation of neuronal spiking during relocation tended to be associated with broader synaptic enrichment' — the word 'tended' is a hedge, but the evidence is a single neuron (#4) compared with one partially compromised neuron (#7) and one aspirated neuron (#6). This is a report of three individual observations, not a tendency, and should be labelled as such.
- SOFT: The title claims 'A Framework for Recording, Retrieval, and Interpretation' — what is presented is a preliminary schema for thinking about retrieval, not a validated framework with demonstrated generalisability. The word 'Framework' overstates what a single dataset of 12 neurons can establish.
- SOFT: The authors state that '1500 proteins were identified' across the gigaseal-preserved neurons, and that 'at least 1700 total proteins were detected in each.' These are presented as impressive numbers, but without any normalisation (e.g., to cell size, or to the known numbers from bulk tissue or FACS-sorted populations), the reader cannot assess whether this is deep or shallow coverage. A benchmark comparison to a published single-neuron dataset would help.
- SOFT: In the Discussion, the authors claim that 'retrieval mechanics, rather than in situ electrophysiology alone, limit whether proteins...are recovered' — this is a plausible conclusion from the data but the phrasing implies it is an established finding. The evidence is entirely consistent with the alternative explanation that the torn/aspirated neurons were simply smaller to begin with (but Fig 5C–D shows no correlation with in situ capacitance, so the data actually disfavour that alternative — which the authors should state more explicitly as support for their claim, while still acknowledging the tiny n).
- SOFT: The paper mentions that 'a peptide that maps to transient receptor potential channels' was observed but 'ambiguous assignments precluded its confident inclusion' — this is honest but raises the question of whether the same ambiguity affects other transmembrane-protein assignments. The authors should state the FDR at the PSM level for these low-abundance targets, or acknowledge that transmembrane-protein detection is at the ragged edge of the method's sensitivity.

## Questions
- Neuron #4 is presented as the 'ideal retrieval.' Could you provide the quantitative passive-membrane values (C, RM, VM) recorded during retrieval for all three gigaseal-preserved neurons, rather than only the ladder-plot deltas?
- The linear regression of protein IDs vs log-capacitance (Fig 3D) reports adjusted R² = 0.998 with n=3. What are the raw protein-ID counts and capacitance values for the three neurons, and would you report the regression without the adjusted-R² correction given the sample size?
- For the SynGO enrichment analyses (Figs 4B–C, 6B), what threshold was used to define 'enriched' versus 'not enriched' — the Q < 0.05 cutoff, or was additional filtering applied? Were the per-neuron gene lists filtered by any minimum spectral-count or intensity threshold, or simply by detection at 1% FDR?
- The PCA in Figure 6A: how many components were considered, what proportion of variance do PC1 and PC2 explain, and was the PCA performed on raw LFQ intensities or on binary (detected/not-detected) data?
- You detect somatostatin (SST) and VGF neuropeptides. Were these identified by unique peptides, and what were the sequence coverage and number of PSMs per sample?
