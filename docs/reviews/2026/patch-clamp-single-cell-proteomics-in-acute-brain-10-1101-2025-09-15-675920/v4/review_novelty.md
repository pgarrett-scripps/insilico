# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript presents a framework for integrating patch-clamp electrophysiology with single-cell proteomics in acute brain slices, with contributions including a conceptual framework linking electrophysiological measurements to proteome recovery, demonstration that gigaseal preservation enables soma integrity monitoring, evidence that capacitance correlates with protein identifications, and a "shotgun" collection strategy analyzing all retrieval outcomes. However, three critical issues prevent acceptance: (1) a concurrent preprint (Johnson et al. 2026, PPR) directly addresses aspiration patch proteomics in intact brain tissue with overlapping scope and is not acknowledged or differentiated; (2) the central quantitative claim (capacitance-protein correlation, R² = 0.998) is based on n=3 neurons, which is statistically inadequate and misleading; and (3) novelty positioning relative to existing patch-SCP literature (Choi et al. 2022, Lee et al. 2024, Ghatak et al. 2024) is unclear, particularly regarding whether gigaseal preservation during retrieval is genuinely novel or an application of prior techniques to acute slices. The work is methodologically sound and the framework is useful, but novelty claims require sharpening and concurrent literature must be engaged.

## Strengths
- Explicit framework for retrieval quality: The manuscript articulates a clear conceptual model (Figure 1) distinguishing three patch-clamp outcomes (gigaseal preserved, lost, never formed) and their interpretive implications—this systematization is useful for the field, even if not entirely novel.
- Quantitative bridge between electrophysiology and proteomics: The correlation between soma capacitance (measured during retrieval) and protein identifications (Figure 3D: R² = 0.998, n=3) provides a concrete, measurable link between a passive electrophysiological property and proteome yield.
- Comprehensive inclusion strategy: The decision to analyze all retrieval outcomes (including torn/failed samples) rather than pre-filtering by success is pragmatic and generates useful negative controls, with PCA and SynGO analysis (Figures 6A–B) demonstrating that proteome composition varies within outcome categories.
- Synaptic protein enrichment analysis: Use of SynGO to assess biological process recovery (Figure 4B–C) and cellular component enrichment (Figure S3) adds qualitative depth beyond raw protein counts, showing that spike integrity during retrieval correlates with synaptic signaling enrichment.
- Reproducibility and transparency: Raw MS data deposited to ProteomeXchange (PXD068359), custom scripts on GitHub, and videos of soma retrieval on Zenodo support reproducibility.

## Weaknesses
- Concurrent preprint directly overlaps scope: Johnson et al. (2026, PPR, "Proteome-Driven Phenotyping of Identified Single Neurons in Intact Brain Tissue by Aspiration Patch Proteomics") addresses patch-based proteomics in intact brain tissue with similar goals, but the manuscript does not acknowledge or differentiate itself from this concurrent work, making the priority claim unclear.
- Sample size severely limits statistical claims: The core quantitative finding (Figure 3D: capacitance vs. protein identifications) is based on n=3 neurons with gigaseal preservation; an R² = 0.998 with n=3 is expected by chance and does not support generalization, making the statement about establishing a "quantitative bridge" misleading.
- Gigaseal preservation during retrieval novelty unclear: The manuscript claims to "maintain the gigaseal during soma retrieval" as a key innovation, but Choi et al. (2022) explicitly performed "Patch-Clamp Proteomics of Single Neurons in Tissue" and the manuscript does not clearly state what is new about this approach versus prior work or whether this is the first application to acute brain slices specifically.
- "Shotgun" strategy framing is imprecise: The manuscript claims a "shotgun patch-SCP strategy" for analyzing all patched neurons regardless of outcome, but this is simply analyzing all samples without pre-filtering—a reasonable analytical choice, not a novel methodological strategy, and the term "shotgun" borrowed from mass spectrometry is metaphorical and potentially confusing.
- Incomplete recovery of membrane proteins not adequately contextualized: The manuscript states it "did not consistently recover complete channel assemblies from individual somas and failed to detect several GPCR families of high biological interest (e.g., opioid, adrenergic, serotonergic, and CRF1 receptors)" but does not quantify whether this represents a regression or parity with existing methods like Choi et al. (2022) or Lee et al. (2024).
- Framework novelty is overstated: The "framework" (Figure 1) is largely a conceptual organization of known patch-clamp principles applied to SCP; the three outcome categories are logical but not surprising, and the contribution is in applying this logic to interpret variability, which is useful but incremental.
- Lack of head-to-head comparison with prior patch-SCP work: The manuscript cites Lee et al. (2024) and Ghatak et al. (2024) as prior patch-SCP studies but does not compare proteome depth, ion channel recovery, or synaptic enrichment quantitatively across studies.
- Generalizability claims unsupported: The manuscript states the framework is "best suited for benchmarking retrieval integrity in relatively large neurons" (Limitations) but then claims it "offers guidance for the design and interpretation of future patch-SCP studies in semi-intact brain circuits" (Conclusions), representing a scope mismatch.
- Spike integrity as a proxy for retrieval quality is correlative, not causal: Figure 4 shows that neurons with preserved spiking during retrieval tend to have richer synaptic enrichment, but the mechanism is not established; neuron #1 (no gigaseal) yielded an unusually large proteome and was near the slice surface, suggesting location may confound the relationship.
- Torn neurons as negative controls are weak: The manuscript includes only two torn neurons (#11, #12) as negative controls (Figure 5B), which is insufficient to establish a robust negative control set, and the claim that torn neurons "consistently showed the poorest recovery" (Figure 7) is based on n=2.

## Questions
- How does this manuscript differ from Johnson et al. (2026, PPR) on aspiration patch proteomics in intact brain tissue? Are the soma retrieval and aspiration approaches complementary, or do they address the same problem? When was each work completed?
- Given n=3 for the capacitance-protein correlation (Figure 3D), how confident are the authors that this relationship generalizes? Would the authors be willing to reframe this as a preliminary observation requiring validation in a larger cohort?
- What specifically is new about maintaining the gigaseal during soma retrieval compared to Choi et al. (2022)? Is this the first application to acute brain slices, or is the novelty in the analysis of retrieval outcomes rather than the technique itself?
- How does the recovery of ion channel subunits (Figure 7) and GPCRs (Figure S4) in this work compare quantitatively to Lee et al. (2024) or Ghatak et al. (2024)? Are the gaps in GPCR detection (opioid, adrenergic, serotonergic, CRF1) consistent with prior studies or a regression?
- Neuron #1 (no gigaseal) yielded an unusually large proteome and was near the slice surface (Video S4). How many of the 12 neurons were near the surface versus embedded deeper? Could slice location confound the relationship between electrophysiological outcome and proteome recovery?
- The Limitations section states the framework is "best suited for benchmarking retrieval integrity in relatively large neurons," yet the Conclusions claim it "offers guidance for future patch-SCP studies in semi-intact brain circuits." Should the conclusions be narrowed to match the demonstrated scope?
- Is the correlation between spike integrity and synaptic enrichment (Figure 4) causal (spiking integrity enables better synaptic recovery) or correlative (both reflect careful retrieval)? How would you distinguish these?
