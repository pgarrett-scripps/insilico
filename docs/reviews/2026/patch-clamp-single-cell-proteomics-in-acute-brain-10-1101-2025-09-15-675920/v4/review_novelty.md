# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript introduces a conceptual framework for interpreting patch-clamp single-cell proteomics (patch-SCP) data that explicitly accounts for variability in soma retrieval quality — something prior work in this space has acknowledged but not systematically addressed. The claim that maintaining the gigaseal during retrieval provides a real-time electrophysiological readout that correlates with proteomic yield (capacitance → protein identifications) is new, as is the “shotgun” strategy of collecting all patched somas regardless of outcome. However, the core evidence rests on only 3 neurons for the gigaseal-preservation analysis and 12 total, which limits the generality of the conclusions and means some framing claims outrun the current data. The novelty is real and appropriately incremental, but several claims need sharper differentiation from the nearest prior work to avoid overstatement.

## Strengths
- The conceptual framework (Figure 1) that classifies patch-SCP outcomes by retrieval integrity rather than simply filtering for “successful” recordings is a useful organizational contribution that could help standardize reporting in this small but growing field.
- The empirical demonstration that capacitance (measured during gigaseal-preserved retrieval) correlates with protein identifications — and that in situ recordings alone do not — is a clear, testable finding that addresses a genuine ambiguity in the literature.
- The authors are transparent about including “failure” cases (torn, aspirated, no-gigaseal neurons) and use them as internal benchmarks rather than discarding them, which is methodologically honest and sets a good precedent.

## Weaknesses
- The central novelty claim — that monitoring gigaseal preservation during retrieval provides information not available from in situ recordings — rests on n = 3 neurons (Figures 3–4). The correlation between log-transformed capacitance and protein identifications (Figure 3D) is reported with F = 1577, p < 0.05, adjusted R² = 0.998, which is a nearly perfect fit for three points. With three data points, any bivariate linear fit is necessarily high or perfect regardless of biological reality. The authors acknowledge the sample size is small, but the interpretive framework derived from these data (e.g., that spike integrity maps to synaptic enrichment) is presented as a general principle. At present, it is an observation on three cells. The claim that gigaseal preservation is a *practical indicator* (as stated in the Conclusions) — rather than a plausible but untested hypothesis — requires replication in a larger, independent cohort.
- The claimed differentiation from prior patch-SCP work is less sharp than it appears. The Choi, Polter & Nemes (2022) *Analytical Chemistry* paper already demonstrated patch-clamp proteomics from single neurons in acute brain slices, including detection of membrane and synaptic proteins. The Lee et al. (2024) *Acta Physiologica* paper applied it to locus coeruleus with sex comparisons. Both used cytoplasmic aspiration rather than whole-soma retrieval, which is a real methodological difference. But the authors do not explicitly state what new *biological or technical insight* whole-soma retrieval enables that cytoplasmic aspiration could not, other than higher protein counts. The Ghatak et al. (2024) *Advanced Science* paper used whole-soma retrieval from iPSC neurons, which is a different system, but already demonstrated the principle of somal collection for proteomics. The manuscript would benefit from a head-to-head comparison — even if qualitative — of what the present framework adds versus what was already achievable with cytoplasmic aspiration in slices.
- The “shotgun” indiscriminate-collection strategy is presented as novel, but its value depends on whether it produces interpretable data for the majority of collected cells. As shown in Figure 6, neurons #11 and #12 (torn) clustered separately and had low utility, and several other neurons (e.g., #6, #10) lacked synaptic enrichment despite high protein counts. The authors use this to argue that comprehensive analysis reveals compromised retrievals, but this is circular: a “shotgun” strategy that includes all outcomes can only reveal which outcomes are compromised if one already has a standard for what “good” looks like — and that standard is defined by the gigaseal-preserved neurons (n = 3). Without an independent validation set, the classification of “high-context” vs. “low-context” retrievals risks being post-hoc rationalization.
- The manuscript repeatedly uses “tended to be associated with” language for correlations drawn from n = 3, which is statistically and rhetorically too strong for the evidence.
- The claim that “retrieval mechanics, rather than in situ electrophysiology alone, limit whether proteins associated with excitability and synaptic function are recovered” is stated as a conclusion but is largely a restatement of the study design — it is not surprising that mechanical retrieval loss degrades recovery; what is missing is a quantitative threshold or guideline for when a retrieval is “good enough” for a given biological question.
- The ion channel, GPCR and transporter detection lists (Figures 7, S4, S5) are presented as a demonstration of capability, but the sparse and variable recovery (4–27 ion channel subunits per cell) is not benchmarked against the expected expression of these proteins in mPFC L2/3 pyramidal neurons, so a reader cannot assess whether the coverage is good or poor.
- The framework in Figure 1 is descriptive and useful, but novelty of a *framework* is limited — frameworks become contributions when they enable predictions or decisions that were not possible before, and the manuscript does not demonstrate such a use case (e.g., a prospective rule for excluding low-quality retrievals).
- A concurrent preprint from Johnson et al. (2026, “Proteome-Driven Phenotyping of Identified Single Neurons in Intact Brain Tissue by Aspiration Patch Proteomics”) appears to address a closely related question (patch-proteomics in intact tissue) and is not discussed or compared. While it posted later, its existence underscores the fast-moving nature of the field and the need to clearly stake out what the present work uniquely contributes.
- The claim that “patch-SCP can be used to assess soma retrieval” (from the Abstract) is a modest operational claim and uncontroversial; it does not require novelty scrutiny. The larger framing claims are where the weaknesses above apply.

## Questions
- Can the authors provide the raw data points and correlation statistics (e.g., bootstrapped confidence intervals) for the capacitance–protein-identifications relationship in Figure 3D, given that R² = 0.998 with three points is a mathematical necessity for any tight scatter and is not meaningful?
- What is the minimal detectable fold-change or expression level threshold for the transmembrane proteins reported in Figure 7, and how does this compare to the expected copy number of GABRA1, SCN2A, GRM3 etc. in a single mPFC L2/3 pyramidal soma?
- Would the authors be willing to prospectively define an inclusion criterion for “high-context retrieval” based on the current data (e.g., capacitance > X pF, spiking amplitude > Y mV during relocation) and test it on an independent set of neurons?
