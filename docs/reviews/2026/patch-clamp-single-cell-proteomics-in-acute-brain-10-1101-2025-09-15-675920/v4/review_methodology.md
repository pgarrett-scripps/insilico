# Methodology Reviewer

SCORE: 2  
CONFIDENCE: 4  

**Overall assessment**  
This is a proof-of-concept study with a genuinely useful interpretive framework, but the central quantitative claim — that capacitance measured during gigaseal-preserved retrieval predicts protein identifications — rests on n=3 with a fitted regression that cannot support the reported statistics. The design does not include the controls needed to distinguish retrieval quality from soma size, and the categorical comparisons across patch outcomes are confounded by sample size and by the absence of any pre-specified inclusion criteria. The framework is worth publishing as a methods proposal, but the evidence presented does not yet earn the correlational and comparative conclusions drawn from it.

**Strengths**  
1. The indiscriminate collection strategy is a thoughtful departure from the field’s common all-or-nothing filtering, and it provides a useful internal benchmark for retrieval quality.  
2. The explicit mapping of patch-clamp outcome categories to interpretability levels (Figure 1) is a clear conceptual contribution that will help future studies design their own inclusion criteria.  
3. The inclusion of torn/aspirated neurons as negative controls is a good design choice, even if the sample size limits its power.

**Weaknesses (load-bearing)**  

**1. The capacitance–protein-identification correlation is not established by the data.** The claim that log-transformed capacitance correlates with protein identifications (F = 1577, p < 0.05, adjusted R² = 0.998, n = 3) is presented as a quantitative bridge between electrophysiology and proteomics. With three points, the regression is trivially sensitive to any single outlier, the p-value is not meaningful at this sample size, and the adjusted R² is inflated by the small n. Moreover, the design does not separate soma size from retrieval quality: the three neurons differ in both capacitance and in the integrity of their active properties (neuron #6 was partially aspirated, neuron #7 showed reduced spike amplitude). The correlation could equally be driven by retrieval damage rather than by size. To support the claim, the authors would need either a larger sample of gigaseal-preserved retrievals with a range of capacitances and uniformly intact spiking, or a within-neuron control (e.g., capacitance measured before and after retrieval, with protein yield compared across matched sizes). As it stands, the result is suggestive but not load-bearing.

**2. The association between spike preservation and synaptic enrichment is confounded by retrieval mechanics.** The claim that preservation of spiking during relocation is associated with broader synaptic enrichment (Figure 4) rests on a comparison of three neurons, one of which (#6) was partially aspirated, one (#7) showed reduced spike amplitude, and one (#4) was described as an ideal retrieval. The design does not control for the degree of mechanical manipulation, the amount of suction applied, or the depth of the neuron in the slice — all of which could independently affect both spike integrity and the recovery of synaptic material. The authors acknowledge this is exploratory, but the conclusion as worded (“preservation of active properties during retrieval is associated with recovery of synaptic proteins”) implies a causal or at least predictive relationship that the design cannot isolate. A larger cohort with matched retrieval difficulty, or a blinded scoring of retrieval quality, would be needed to distinguish the effect of spike preservation from the effect of gentler handling.

**3. The claim that in situ recordings do not predict proteome recovery is underpowered and conflates categories.** Figure 5C–D reports no correlation between capacitance/RM measured in situ and protein identifications (n = 6), and the authors use this to argue that retrieval loss decouples proteomics from electrophysiology. But the six neurons span three different outcome categories (gigaseal lost, torn, no gigaseal), and the torn neurons are the ones driving the low end of the protein count. Pooling across categories conflates retrieval failure with the absence of a predictive relationship. A fair test would restrict the analysis to neurons that were successfully retrieved with a gigaseal, or would compare within-category correlations. As reported, the result does not rule out the possibility that in situ capacitance is predictive when retrieval is successful.

**Sweep (one sentence each)**  
- The PCA clustering (Figure 6A) is presented as evidence of retrieval-related separation, but with n=12 and no permutation testing or cross-validation, the clustering could be driven by noise or by protein-count differences rather than by biological content.  
- The SynGO enrichment analyses are performed per neuron with no multiple-testing correction across neurons or across the two ontologies, which inflates the number of reported significant terms.  
- The ion channel/GPCR/transporter detection heatmaps (Figures 7, S4, S5) are binary presence/absence calls from a single DIA run per neuron; no technical replicates or spike-in standards are reported, so detection variability cannot be distinguished from biological variability.  
- The claim that “torn samples still showed impressive protein recovery” is based on two neurons (#11, #12) with 4–27 ion channel subunits, which is not obviously impressive relative to the 1,400–2,300 proteins detected in other categories; the framing overstates the negative-control value.  
- The manuscript does not report whether the three gigaseal-preserved neurons were recorded on the same day, by the same operator, or with the same batch of internal solution — batch and operator effects are not addressed.  
- The framework’s “no gigaseal” category includes neurons that were never characterized electrophysiologically, yet these are pooled with characterized neurons in the PCA and SynGO analyses; this mixing weakens the interpretability of the clustering.  
- The authors do not state a pre-specified analysis plan or any inclusion/exclusion criteria for the proteomic data, which is a soft limitation given the exploratory framing.  
- The correlation between capacitance and protein identifications is reported with a log-transform of capacitance but not of protein counts; the choice of transform is not justified and could affect the fit.

**Questions**  
1. For the capacitance–protein identification correlation (Figure 3D), can you report the raw data points and the fit with and without neuron #6, which was partially aspirated?  
2. Were the three gigaseal-preserved neurons recorded on the same day and by the same operator, and was the internal solution from the same batch?  
3. For the SynGO analyses, what multiple-testing correction was applied across the 53 biological processes and 24 cellular components tested per neuron?  
4. Can you report the number of proteins detected in each neuron alongside the PCA loadings, to show that the clustering is not driven primarily by total protein count?
