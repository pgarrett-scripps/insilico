# Methodology Reviewer

SCORE: 2  
CONFIDENCE: 4  

**Overall take.** This is a proof-of-concept study with a sensible core idea — using gigaseal preservation during soma retrieval as a real-time indicator of proteomic recovery — but the design as implemented cannot support the central claims about the relationship between retrieval integrity and proteome content. The sample size (n=3 for the gigaseal-preserved group, n=12 total) is far too small for the correlational and clustering analyses presented, and the key comparisons lack the controls needed to distinguish retrieval quality from neuron-to-neuron biological variation. The framework is worth publishing as a methodological proposal, but the empirical claims need either substantially more data or a much more modest framing.

**Strengths.** The indiscriminate collection strategy — including torn and no-gigaseal neurons as internal comparators — is a genuinely useful design choice for benchmarking retrieval quality. The use of capacitance as a size proxy during retrieval is a clever, physiologically grounded metric that could plausibly link soma size to yield. The authors are candid about limitations, including the failure to detect many GPCRs and the compartmental bias of somatic sampling.

**Weaknesses (load-bearing).**

1. **The central claim — that gigaseal preservation during retrieval predicts proteomic quality — rests on n=3, with no control for biological variability.** The correlation between log-transformed capacitance and protein identifications (Figure 3D) is computed from three neurons. With n=3, a single outlier drives the regression; the reported adjusted R² = 0.998 is statistically meaningless at this sample size. The claim that "soma size plays a more direct role in protein recovery than RM" is not earned by three points. The authors need either (a) many more gigaseal-preserved retrievals, or (b) an explicit statement that this is a single illustrative case, not a tested relationship. The same problem afflicts the comparison of spiking integrity across neurons #4, #6, and #7 — three cases cannot establish that "preservation of active properties is associated with recovery of synaptic proteins."

2. **The PCA and SynGO clustering (Figure 6) are presented as evidence that retrieval outcomes shape proteome composition, but the design cannot separate retrieval quality from neuron identity.** Neurons #6 and #10 cluster with no-gigaseal neurons, and torn neurons separate cleanly — but these are different cells, potentially different sizes, depths, and local environments. There is no matched control: no pair of neurons from the same animal, same layer, same depth, same size, one retrieved with gigaseal and one without. The claim that "retrieval loss decouples proteomic measurements from electrophysiology" requires showing that the same neuron type retrieved differently produces different proteomes — not that different neurons cluster differently. The authors need a design where retrieval outcome is varied while neuron identity is held as constant as possible, or they must explicitly hedge that the clustering may reflect biological heterogeneity.

3. **The "torn neuron as negative control" logic is circular for the qualitative claims.** Torn neurons (#11, #12) show fewer proteins and cluster separately — but this is presented as evidence that retrieval quality matters, when it is simply the definition of a poor retrieval. The more interesting claim — that neuron #6, with a gigaseal but compromised spiking, resembles no-gigaseal neurons in lacking synaptic enrichment — is again a single case. The design does not rule out that neuron #6 was simply a smaller or different neuron whose proteome would have looked the same even with perfect retrieval.

**Sweep.**

- The comparison of ion channel recovery across retrieval categories (Figure 7) is descriptive only; no statistical test accounts for the fact that more proteins overall (a size effect) will mechanically yield more channel subunits — the authors should normalize channel counts to total identifications or use a proportional test.
- The claim that "in situ recordings are not a prerequisite for achieving a large number of protein identifications" is supported by only one no-gigaseal neuron (#1) with high yield; the other two no-gigaseal neurons (#5, #8) are not shown to be comparable in soma size, so this could be a size effect rather than a retrieval effect.
- The framework's distinction between "quantitative" (yield) and "qualitative" (synaptic content) axes is useful, but the manuscript never defines a priori thresholds for what counts as "compromised" retrieval — the categories (gigaseal preserved/lost/never formed/torn) are assigned post hoc, and the reader cannot tell whether the assignment was blinded to the proteomic results.
- The passive property measurements during retrieval (Figure 3C) are presented as showing "physiological changes can occur," but with n=3 and no statistical comparison, this is anecdotal; the authors should state whether any of the changes (e.g., in RM or VM) were consistent across neurons or within noise.
- The claim that "25–50% of the soma could be lost over the course of collection" is an estimate with no measurement basis shown — the authors should either show how this was derived (e.g., capacitance change) or remove the quantitative range.
- The absence of any replication across animals or slices is not addressed; all 12 neurons could come from one or two animals, and batch effects (slice preparation day, recording session) are not reported or controlled in the analysis.
- The SynGO enrichment analysis uses a Q-value threshold of 0.05 but does not report the number of proteins tested per neuron or the multiple-testing correction method beyond the Q-value; this is partly data_analysis's lane, but the design should specify whether enrichment was computed on detected-only proteins or on the full background.

**Questions.**

1. How many animals and how many slices were used for the 12 neurons, and were the gigaseal-preserved neurons (#4, #6, #7) from the same or different animals/slices? If they are from different animals, the capacitance–yield correlation could be confounded by inter-animal variation.
2. For the PCA in Figure 6, was the analysis performed on raw intensities, normalized intensities, or binary detection? The choice materially affects whether the clustering reflects abundance differences or presence/absence patterns.
3. Were the retrieval outcome categories (gigaseal preserved/lost/never formed/torn) assigned before or after the proteomic data were examined? If after, was the assignment done blind to the MS results?
4. Can the authors report the soma size (capacitance) for the no-gigaseal neurons (#1, #5, #8) and the torn neurons (#11, #12)? If the torn neurons were also the smallest, the "torn = poor proteome" result could be a size effect, not a retrieval effect.
