# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents a methodological framework for interpreting patch-clamp single-cell proteomics (patch-SCP) in acute brain slices, with emphasis on soma retrieval quality. The core design strength lies in the deliberate inclusion of all retrieval outcomes (gigaseal-preserved, gigaseal-lost, no gigaseal, torn) to benchmark retrieval integrity against proteomic recovery. However, the study is fundamentally limited by a very small sample size (n=3 for gigaseal-preserved; n=12 total), which prevents robust statistical inference and generalization. Key findings—that capacitance correlates with protein identifications and that retrieval integrity associates with synaptic enrichment—rest on minimal replication and lack orthogonal validation. The framework is conceptually sound and the 'shotgun' strategy is pragmatic for exploratory work, but the design does not yet support strong causal or comparative claims about what retrieval metrics predict proteome quality across neuronal populations.

## Strengths
- The 'shotgun' inclusion strategy (all retrieval outcomes analyzed) is a principled approach to benchmarking retrieval quality and avoiding selection bias that would hide variability—a genuine methodological contribution for exploratory patch-SCP work.
- Gigaseal preservation during retrieval is a novel technical achievement (n=3) that enables real-time monitoring of soma properties during extraction, providing a quantitative bridge between electrophysiology and proteomics that prior patch-SCP work has not exploited.
- The framework (Figure 1) clearly maps patch-clamp outcomes to interpretive contexts, explicitly acknowledging compartmental sampling limits and the decoupling of in situ recordings from retrieved material—a valuable conceptual contribution.
- Comprehensive GO analysis using SynGO (synaptic-specific ontology) and detection of ion channels, GPCRs, and transmembrane proteins across all samples demonstrates technical sensitivity and biological relevance.
- Transparent reporting of limitations (compartmental bias, incomplete channel recovery, spatial fidelity of voltage clamp) and honest discussion of what the small dataset can and cannot conclude.
- Videos of soma retrieval (Zenodo) and public deposition of raw MS data (ProteomeXchange) support reproducibility and transparency.

## Weaknesses
-  **HARD: Minimal replication for the central quantitative claim.** The correlation between log-transformed capacitance and protein identifications (Figure 3D) is based on n=3 neurons with gigaseal preserved. The authors report F=1577, p<0.05, R²=0.998, but with only 3 data points, this is not a robust statistical inference; the high R² is expected by chance with so few points, and no confidence interval or bootstrap resampling is provided. The claim 'capacitance...plays a more direct role in protein recovery' (Results) cannot be generalized from 3 neurons.
- **HARD: No independent replication of the key finding that spike integrity associates with synaptic enrichment.** The observation that neuron #4 (best spiking) shows the greatest synaptic GO enrichment while neuron #6 (single spike) lacks synaptic signaling enrichment (Figure 4C) is presented as a pattern, but with only 3 gigaseal-preserved neurons, this is a post-hoc observation on a single dataset, not a replicated effect. The authors do not state this was a pre-registered hypothesis.
- **HARD: Confound between soma size and retrieval quality not fully disentangled.** Neuron #4 is described as 'large' and 'ideal retrieval' with stable spiking; neuron #6 is 'smallest' and shows poor spiking and low synaptic enrichment. The design does not isolate whether poor synaptic enrichment in #6 is due to (a) smaller soma size reducing material recovered, (b) mechanical damage during retrieval, or (c) biological differences in synaptic content. The authors acknowledge size influences recovery but do not control for it when interpreting spike integrity as a predictor of synaptic enrichment.
- **HARD: No positive control demonstrating that the assay can detect synaptic proteins under optimal conditions.** The authors do not show a 'gold standard' retrieval (e.g., enzymatic dissociation of a soma, or a known positive control tissue) to establish the theoretical maximum synaptic protein recovery. Without this, claims about 'poor synaptic representation' in torn neurons (Figure 5) cannot be anchored to an absolute reference.
- **SOFT: Lack of orthogonal validation of the capacitance–protein correlation.** The correlation in Figure 3D relies solely on protein identification count from DIA-MS. An independent method (e.g., quantitative immunofluorescence of the retrieved soma, or a second MS platform) would strengthen the claim that capacitance predicts actual protein yield rather than just MS detection efficiency.
- **SOFT: No dose-response or sensitivity analysis for retrieval pressure.** The authors mention applying 'light negative pressure (−50 to −140 mmHg)' but do not systematically vary this to determine whether retrieval success or proteome quality is sensitive to pressure magnitude. A dose-response would strengthen claims about retrieval optimization.
- **SOFT: Torn neurons (n=2, #11 and #12) serve as internal negative controls, but no positive control (e.g., a neuron retrieved by enzymatic dissociation or a known high-quality sample) is included.** The framework would be more convincing if the extreme cases were bracketed by a known high-quality reference.
- **SOFT: PCA clustering (Figure 6A) shows separation of torn neurons and neuron #6, but the biological or technical basis for the clustering is not formally tested.** The authors state neuron #6 'grouped more closely with neurons lacking gigaseals,' but no statistical test (e.g., silhouette analysis, permutation test) validates that this clustering is robust or meaningful.
- **SOFT: No pre-registration or stated analysis plan.** The authors describe an exploratory study, which is appropriate for small n, but the absence of a pre-registered hypothesis or analysis plan increases the risk of post-hoc interpretation (e.g., the observation that neuron #4 shows the best synaptic enrichment may be selected because it is the 'best' example).
- **SOFT: Generalization to other neuronal types not addressed.** All 12 neurons are Layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons from rat mPFC. The authors acknowledge this limitation but do not discuss whether the framework (e.g., capacitance–protein correlation, spike integrity–synaptic enrichment association) would hold for smaller neurons, interneurons, or other brain regions.

## Questions
- The correlation in Figure 3D (capacitance vs. protein identifications) is based on n=3. How confident are the authors that this relationship holds across a larger sample? Would a replication study with n≥10 gigaseal-preserved neurons be feasible?
- In Figure 4C, neuron #6 (smallest, single spike) lacks significant synaptic enrichment, while neurons #4 and #7 (larger, better spiking) show enrichment. Is the lack of synaptic enrichment in #6 a consequence of smaller soma size (less material) or mechanical damage during retrieval? Can the authors disentangle these by comparing neurons of similar size but different retrieval integrity?
- The authors state that 'torn samples still showed impressive protein recovery' (Results). Does this mean torn neurons recovered similar numbers of proteins as intact neurons, or fewer? If similar, how do the authors reconcile this with the claim that retrieval integrity predicts synaptic enrichment?
- What is the theoretical maximum number of proteins expected from a single pyramidal soma, and how does the observed recovery (1,400–2,300 proteins) compare to this? Is there an orthogonal method (e.g., immunofluorescence, enzymatic dissociation) that could validate the protein yield?
- The authors mention that 'several neurons (#6 and #10) lacked significant enrichment for synaptic signaling despite being characterized by electrophysiology' (Results). Were these neurons excluded from downstream analyses, or were they retained? If retained, how does their inclusion affect the overall conclusions?
- In the 'Limitations' section, the authors note that 'variability cannot be completely mitigated' due to 3D organization of slices. Given this, what is the minimum sample size needed to make robust claims about the relationship between retrieval metrics and proteome quality?
- The authors use DIA-MS for all analyses. Did they consider or perform any DDA (data-dependent acquisition) analyses as a comparison, or is DIA the only platform used? If only DIA, how does this choice affect the detection of low-abundance transmembrane proteins?
- Figure 7 shows variable ion channel recovery across neurons. For example, neuron #1 (no gigaseal) recovered 27 ion channel subunits, while neuron #4 (gigaseal-preserved, 'ideal') recovered fewer. How do the authors explain this discrepancy, and does it undermine the claim that gigaseal preservation predicts better recovery of functionally relevant proteins?
