# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript presents a framework for interpreting patch-clamp single-cell proteomics (patch-SCP) in acute brain slices, with emphasis on soma retrieval quality. The work is methodologically sound and addresses a genuine technical bottleneck in the field. However, the abstract and several key claims overstate the evidence base. The study is explicitly exploratory (n=3 for gigaseal-preserved neurons, n=12 total), yet the abstract and title present findings as if they establish generalizable principles. The correlation between capacitance and protein yield (Figure 3D) is based on only 3 neurons and is presented without confidence intervals or discussion of the limited sample size. Claims about what patch-SCP "can be used to assess" and what the framework "demonstrates" are not fully licensed by the data. The work makes a valuable methodological contribution but requires substantial hedging and scope clarification to align claims with evidence.

## Strengths
- The framework explicitly acknowledges compartmental bias and retrieval variability—a genuine and underexplored technical limitation in patch-SCP.
- The decision to analyze all retrieval outcomes (including failed attempts) rather than cherry-picking successful cases is methodologically sound and provides useful negative controls.
- The use of SynGO for synaptic-specific enrichment analysis is appropriate and reveals that protein count alone does not predict biological content (e.g., neuron #6 had many proteins but poor synaptic enrichment).
- Electrophysiological monitoring during retrieval (gigaseal preservation) is a novel technical contribution that provides real-time readouts of soma integrity.
- The manuscript is transparent about limitations, including compartmental bias, incomplete channel recovery, and the small sample size—these are discussed in the Limitations section.
- Videos of soma retrieval are made publicly available, supporting reproducibility.

## Weaknesses
- {HARD} Abstract claims 'we demonstrate that patch-SCP can be used to assess soma retrieval' and 'provide a framework for interpreting how electrophysiological context and soma retrieval quality shape single-neuron proteomic measurements.' These are presented as established findings, but the evidence is from n=3 gigaseal-preserved neurons and n=12 total. The abstract does not disclose the exploratory, small-scale nature of the dataset. The claim that the framework 'demonstrates' something should be hedged to 'suggests' or 'is consistent with' given the sample size.
- {HARD} Figure 3D shows a correlation between log-transformed capacitance and protein identifications (R²=0.998, n=3). The authors state this provides 'a means of linking soma size to proteome recovery,' but n=3 is insufficient to establish a generalizable relationship. No confidence intervals, bootstrap resampling, or discussion of the limited sample size is provided. The perfect R² value (0.998) is suspicious and suggests possible overfitting or that the relationship is being presented without appropriate uncertainty quantification.
- {HARD} The claim in Results that 'Preservation of neuronal spiking during relocation tended to be associated with broader synaptic enrichment' (Abstract) is not quantitatively tested. Figure 4B–C shows that neuron #4 (best spiking) had the most enriched terms, but neuron #7 (reduced amplitude) had nearly as many, and neuron #6 (single spike) had fewer. This is a qualitative pattern in n=3 neurons, not a statistical association. The word 'tended' is appropriate hedging, but the claim should not appear in the abstract as a main finding without statistical support.
- {HARD} The title states 'A Framework for Recording, Retrieval, and Interpretation,' implying a complete, validated framework. However, the framework is presented as a conceptual model (Figure 1) tested on a single brain region (mPFC Layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons) in a single species (rat) with n=12 total neurons. The generalizability of the framework to other neuron types, brain regions, or species is not established. The title should be qualified (e.g., 'A Framework for Interpreting Patch-SCP in Acute Brain Slices: Proof-of-Concept in Rat mPFC Pyramidal Neurons').
- {HARD} The claim that 'retrieval loss has the potential to uncouple SCP from patch-clamp recordings' (Results) is supported by Figure 5C–D showing no correlation between in situ capacitance/RM and protein identifications in neurons that lost the gigaseal (n=6). However, the authors estimate '25–50% of the soma could be lost' (Results) without providing the basis for this estimate. This is a quantitative claim without supporting data or methodology.
- {SOFT} The claim that 'robust in situ recordings cannot compensate for severe retrieval loss' (Results) is based on torn neurons (#11, #12) that produced few proteins. However, torn neurons are a qualitatively different category (visibly damaged) from neurons that lost the gigaseal during retrieval. The comparison conflates mechanical damage with loss of electrical continuity, making the causal inference unclear.
- {SOFT} The statement that 'protein identifications have become less reliable indicators of sample quality' (Results) is inferred from the observation that torn samples still yielded ~1000 proteins. However, this is based on n=2 torn neurons. The claim would be stronger with a larger sample or explicit acknowledgment that this is a preliminary observation.
- {SOFT} The detection of ion channels (Figure 7) is presented as evidence that the workflow can recover 'low-abundance and hydrophobic transmembrane proteins.' However, the authors note that several GPCR families of 'high biological interest' (opioid, adrenergic, serotonergic, CRF1 receptors) were not detected. The claim about successful detection of transmembrane proteins should be qualified to acknowledge the incomplete recovery of functionally important targets.
- {SOFT} The claim that 'SynGO CC enrichments clustered along retrieval category (Figure S3), similar to the pattern observed in the PCA plot, rather than by the number of identified proteins' is presented as evidence that biological content varies independently of protein count. However, PCA and clustering patterns are qualitative; no statistical test (e.g., silhouette analysis, permutation test) is provided to quantify whether the clustering is significant or reproducible.
- {SOFT} The manuscript states that 'at least 1700 total proteins were detected in each' neuron (Figure 3F), but this is based on n=3 neurons. The generalizability of this protein recovery to other neurons or conditions is not established. The phrasing 'at least' is vague—does this mean the minimum across the three, or the minimum per neuron?

## Questions
- Figure 3D: What is the basis for the R²=0.998 correlation with only n=3 data points? Were any statistical tests (e.g., permutation tests, bootstrap confidence intervals) performed to assess the robustness of this relationship? How does this compare to the correlation observed in the gigaseal-lost neurons (Figure 5C–D)?
- The authors estimate '25–50% of the soma could be lost over the course of collection' (Results). What is the basis for this estimate? Is it derived from the change in capacitance during retrieval (Figure 3C), and if so, how was it calculated?
- Figure 4B–C: The claim that preservation of spiking is 'associated with' broader synaptic enrichment is based on qualitative observation of n=3 neurons. Was a statistical test performed (e.g., correlation between spike amplitude and number of enriched GO terms)? If not, should this be presented as a 'trend' rather than an association?
- The framework (Figure 1) is presented as applicable to patch-SCP in acute brain slices generally, but the data come from a single brain region (mPFC Layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons) in a single species (rat). Do the authors expect the framework to generalize to other neuron types (e.g., interneurons, cerebellar neurons) or brain regions? What evidence would be needed to validate this?
- The authors note that several GPCR families were not detected (opioid, adrenergic, serotonergic, CRF1 receptors). Is this a limitation of soma-focused sampling, the DIA-MS workflow, or both? Would targeted acquisition modes (e.g., PRM) improve detection of these proteins?
- Figure 5A groups neurons by patch outcome, but the protein identifications vary widely within each category (e.g., 1,400–2,300 proteins in the 'no gigaseal' group). What factors explain this within-category variability? Is it related to neuron size, location in the slice, or technical handling?
- The authors state that 'neuronal structures can be lost or retained in ways that are almost imperceptible when performing patch-SCP' (Results). How would one detect such imperceptible losses? Are there quantitative metrics (beyond protein count and GO enrichment) that could serve as quality control markers?
- The claim that 'retrieval of the soma from the brain slice can be so disruptive that initial electrophysiology recordings no longer reflect the collected material' (Results) is based on the lack of correlation in Figure 5C–D. However, this could also reflect biological variability in protein expression. How can the authors distinguish between retrieval-induced loss and genuine biological heterogeneity?
