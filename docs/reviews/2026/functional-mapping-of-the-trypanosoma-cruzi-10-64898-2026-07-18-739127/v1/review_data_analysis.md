# Statistics & Data-Analysis Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents a competent activity-based chemoproteomics study with appropriate use of limma for LFQ-MS analysis and STRING for functional enrichment. However, the primary discovery claim (37 enriched SHs) relies on uncorrected p-values (p<0.05, log2FC>1) across multiple probes and proteins, inflating false discovery rate. The authors acknowledge this by reporting BH-adjusted values in supplements but not using them for thresholding, framing the work as discovery-oriented. While defensible, the main text presents the 37-protein list as a 'prioritized set' and 'resource' without quantifying the expected false positives. The whole-cell vs lysate comparison lacks statistical support. GO/PPI analyses are sound but use cross-strain homolog mapping. Overall, conclusions are largely supported but overconfident given the statistical thresholds.

## Strengths
- Appropriate use of limma's empirical Bayes moderation for small-n (n=3) proteomics data, with explicit reporting of biological replication.
- Transparent reporting of the decision to forgo FDR control in favor of sensitivity, with BH-adjusted values provided in supplementary data for reader evaluation.
- Rigorous in silico catalytic triad validation using AlphaFold structures with defined geometric and confidence thresholds (distance <5Å, pLDDT≥80).

## Weaknesses
- The core claim of 37 enriched SHs (35 with conserved catalytic sites) is derived from LFQ-MS using uncorrected p<0.05 thresholds across 7 probes and ~56 candidate SHs, yielding an uncontrolled family-wise error rate. The authors explicitly state they did not use Benjamini-Hochberg correction 'to avoid discarding genuine low-abundance hydrolases,' but then present the resulting list as a 'prioritized set of probe-accessible and catalytically competent targets' and a 'reference resource' without estimating the false discovery proportion. This overstates the reliability of individual hits; a reader cannot distinguish likely true positives from false positives in the final table. The 63% coverage claim (35/56 predicted SHs) inherits this uncertainty.
- The assertion that whole-cell labelling was 'the key enabling step' because lysate-based labelling 'performed poorly' is supported only by qualitative description ('no labelling was observed,' 'poorly resolved, aggregated material'). No quantitative comparison (e.g., number of proteins detected, signal-to-noise) or statistical test is provided. It remains possible that optimized lysate preparation (e.g., detergent screening, deglycosylation) would succeed, making the whole-cell advantage speculative rather than demonstrated.
- The GO enrichment and PPI network analyses map Dm28c proteins to CL Brener homologs in STRING, introducing mapping uncertainty that is not quantified. The reported PPI enrichment p-value <1×10⁻¹⁶ is generated by STRING's internal algorithm (method unspecified) and may reflect network density artifacts rather than biological coherence. The conclusion that 'lipid metabolism is a dominant biological theme' is plausible but the statistical evidence is indirect.

## Questions
- What is the estimated false discovery rate among the 37 reported SHs given the uncorrected p<0.05 threshold across 7 probes? Could the authors provide a sensitivity analysis (e.g., using BH correction or permutation-based FDR) to contextualize the list reliability?
- For the lysate vs whole-cell comparison: were any lysate conditions (e.g., with detergents, chaotropes, or deglycosylation) tested quantitatively? If not, can the authors clarify that the whole-cell advantage is inferred from a single failed lysate attempt rather than a systematic comparison?
- In Figure 3a, the heatmap shows log2 fold-changes but no measure of replicate variability. Can the authors confirm that the limma moderated t-statistics (which borrow information across proteins) were used for the p-values, and that the heatmap values are the corresponding log2FC estimates?
