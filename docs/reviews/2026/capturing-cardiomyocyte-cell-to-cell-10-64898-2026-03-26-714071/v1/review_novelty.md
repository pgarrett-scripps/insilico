# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript presents a technically sound single-cell top-down proteomics workflow applied to 13 mouse cardiomyocytes, identifying 57 proteins and 165 proteoforms with various PTMs. While the work demonstrates rigorous technical validation and precise PTM localization, the novelty claims are substantially overstated relative to recent literature. The core proteoform heterogeneity finding is preempted by Melby et al. (2023), which already demonstrated cell-to-cell heterogeneity in muscle fiber proteoforms; the current work extends this to cardiomyocytes, which is incremental. The methodological contribution (EThcD+HCD in LC-MS) is a practical optimization rather than a fundamental advance, and its relationship to the cited scPiMS platform is not clearly articulated. Six "first" claims about specific modifications lack systematic verification against proteomics databases and literature. The work makes a useful contribution to cardiac proteomics but requires major revisions to reframe novelty claims, verify "first" designations, provide methodological comparisons, and address the limited scope (13 size-selected cells from one mouse with no functional validation).

## Strengths
- Genuine methodological refinement: The combination of EThcD and HCD in a single acquisition method improves both proteoform coverage and backbone fragmentation, with clear evidence (Figure S6) that EThcD outperforms HCD alone, justifying the hybrid approach.
- Rigorous technical validation: The workflow is validated at multiple levels—protein standards (5 of 6 detected), bulk cardiomyocyte replicates (41 proteins, 83 proteoforms with high reproducibility across triplicates, Figure S4), and 13 individual cells with consistent chromatographic profiles (Figure 1), demonstrating robustness.
- Precise PTM localization: The manuscript provides unambiguous site-specific localization of multiple PTMs on single proteins (e.g., MLC-2 with A1 trimethylation and T51 phosphorylation, Figure 3D), supported by diagnostic ions and validated with TDValidator—a genuine strength of top-down over bottom-up approaches.
- Cardiac-relevant protein focus: The selection of functionally important cardiac proteins (MLC-2, MYL3, COX6c, Qcr7) with clear biological relevance to contractility and mitochondrial function strengthens the application domain.
- Honest acknowledgment of limitations: The authors note the mass spectrometer's inability to detect the largest protein standard (~68 kDa, Figure S1) and acknowledge that functional consequences of most modifications "remain to be defined."

## Weaknesses
- Preempted proteoform heterogeneity claim: The manuscript states "This work provides the first insights into the proteoform landscape and heterogeneity of single cardiomyocytes," but Melby et al. (2023, PNAS 120:e2222081120, cited as ref. 10) already demonstrated high sensitivity top-down proteomics capturing single muscle cell heterogeneity in large proteoforms. While the current work applies this to cardiomyocytes specifically, the core finding that individual muscle cells exhibit proteoform heterogeneity is not novel and should be reframed as extending rather than pioneering this analysis.
- Unclear methodological novelty relative to scPiMS: The manuscript cites Su et al. (2023) as demonstrating scPiMS for intact proteoform profiling of individual cells but does not clearly articulate what the LC-MS + EThcD/HCD approach does that scPiMS does not. No quantitative comparison of throughput, sensitivity, cost, or ease of implementation is provided, leaving the claimed "major advance" unsubstantiated.
- Unverified "first" claims: The manuscript makes six "first" claims (MLC-2 trimethylation, MLC-2 concurrent trimethylation+phosphorylation, MYL3 trimethylation, COX6c N-terminal acetylation, Qcr7 succinylation) without systematic verification against proteomics databases (PhosphoSitePlus, ProteomicsDB) or literature searches. For example, N-terminal acetylation is described as "widespread among eukaryotic proteins" (p. 6), yet no evidence is provided that COX6c N-terminal acetylation has not been reported in bulk proteomics studies.
- Incomplete proteoform overlap analysis: Figure 2B shows "high proportion of unique proteoforms in each cell," but the manuscript does not quantify how many proteoforms are singletons (detected in only one cell) versus detected across multiple or all 13 cells. Without this breakdown, the claim that proteoforms "capture subtle cell-to-cell variation masked at the protein level" cannot be evaluated, and the heterogeneity may reflect stochastic sampling rather than biological variation.
- Limited scope of single-cell analysis: Only 13 cardiomyocytes are analyzed, all from a single mouse and size-selected (61–68 µm). This narrow sample limits generalizability to the full size range of mouse cardiomyocytes (35–147 µm, cited from ref. 20), and the title's claim of "capturing cardiomyocyte cell-to-cell heterogeneity" may overstate the breadth of the findings.
- Functional significance remains speculative: The manuscript identifies novel modifications but speculates on their functional roles without experimental evidence. Claims that these proteoforms "may serve as potential biomarkers for cardiac disease" (Summary) are unsupported—no disease samples are analyzed, and no biomarker validation is performed.
- Missing quantitative comparison to bulk sample: The bulk cardiomyocyte sample (10 ng total protein) yields 41 proteins and 83 proteoforms, while single-cell analysis yields 57 proteins and 165 proteoforms from 13 cells. The manuscript does not clarify whether the higher proteoform count reflects genuine cell-to-cell variation or improved per-cell detection sensitivity, and a quantitative comparison of proteoforms per microgram of protein would be informative.
- Absent comparison to bottom-up single-cell proteomics: The manuscript motivates SC-TDP by criticizing bottom-up proteomics for losing PTM co-occurrence information but does not compare actual performance (number of proteins, PTM identifications, reproducibility) to contemporary single-cell bottom-up methods (e.g., SCoPE2, cited as ref. 1), limiting assessment of practical advantages.

## Questions
- Can the authors provide a breakdown of how many proteoforms are detected in all 13 cells, in >50% of cells, and in only 1 cell to clarify whether the "high proportion of unique proteoforms" (Figure 2B) reflects biological heterogeneity or stochastic sampling effects?
- Have the authors systematically searched PhosphoSitePlus, ProteomicsDB, or other proteomics databases to confirm that COX6c N-terminal acetylation, Qcr7 succinylation, and MYL3 trimethylation have not been reported in bulk proteomics studies, and if they have been reported, should the "first in single cardiomyocytes" framing be made explicit?
- What are the quantitative differences in throughput, sensitivity, cost, or ease of implementation between the LC-MS + EThcD/HCD approach and the scPiMS platform, and how does this work represent a practical advance over scPiMS?
- How do proteoform identifications vary across the full size range of cardiomyocytes (35–147 µm), and does the size-selection to 61–68 µm limit the applicability of findings to the broader cardiomyocyte population?
- Can the authors design an experiment to distinguish whether the high proportion of unique proteoforms in Figure 2B reflects true biological heterogeneity or technical variation (e.g., by analyzing replicate cells from the same tissue region)?
- Are there plans to validate the functional significance of the identified proteoforms (e.g., does MLC-2 trimethylation affect contractility in isolated cardiomyocytes or engineered cardiac tissue)?
