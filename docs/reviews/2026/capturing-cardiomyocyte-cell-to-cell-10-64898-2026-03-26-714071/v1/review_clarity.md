# Clarity & Presentation Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript is generally well-written and logically structured, with clear motivation, methods, and results. The central contribution—demonstrating single-cell top-down proteomics (SC-TDP) to capture proteoform heterogeneity in cardiomyocytes—is stated explicitly and the narrative flows coherently from problem to solution to findings. However, there are several HARD clarity issues that impede precise understanding: (1) the exact definition and scope of 'proteoform' as used here is never formally stated, leaving ambiguity about what distinguishes a proteoform from a protein variant; (2) Figure 2's heatmap methodology is not explained in the caption or text, making it unclear how overlap is calculated or what the color scale quantitatively represents; (3) key quantitative claims lack specificity (e.g., 'substantial heterogeneity' and 'extensive overlap' are not quantified); and (4) the relationship between the 57 proteins and 165 proteoforms is never clarified numerically. These issues, while not fatal, create friction for readers trying to understand the precise scope and magnitude of the findings.

## Strengths
- The abstract clearly states the central claim (proteoform profiling from single cardiomyocytes) and the key finding (heterogeneity among cells), making the contribution intelligible standalone.
- The introduction effectively frames the gap: bottom-up proteomics loses PTM co-occurrence information, and single-cell TDP is technically challenging but valuable. The motivation is explicit and non-oversold.
- Methods are presented in logical order (sample prep → LC conditions → MS conditions → data analysis) with sufficient detail to reconstruct the workflow; the Supporting Information reference signals completeness.
- Results flow coherently: validation on bulk sample → single-cell analysis → specific proteoform examples. Each section's purpose is clear.
- Figure 1 is well-designed: the total ion chromatograms across 13 cells with color-coded protein/proteoform detection windows are easy to interpret and support the reproducibility claim.
- Figures 3A–E are detailed fragmentation maps with clear ion annotations (b, c, y, z series) and diagnostic ion labels, making the evidence for specific PTMs and truncations traceable.
- The paper avoids orphaned results; all major findings (MLC-2 trimethylation, phosphorylation, truncation; MYL3 trimethylation; COX6c acetylation; Qcr7 succinylation) are discussed in context with cardiac relevance.

## Weaknesses
- HARD: The term 'proteoform' is never formally defined in the manuscript. The abstract and introduction use it repeatedly but do not state whether it refers only to PTM variants, truncations, or both, or whether it includes sequence variants. A reader cannot determine from the text alone what distinguishes a 'proteoform' from a 'protein isoform' or 'protein variant.' This is critical because the entire contribution hinges on proteoform characterization.
- HARD: Figure 2 caption and legend do not explain the heatmap methodology. Specifically: (1) How is 'overlap' calculated—is it the number of proteins/proteoforms shared, a percentage, or a Jaccard index? (2) What does the light-to-dark yellow scale quantitatively represent—is it 0–100%, 0–1, or something else? (3) Why do some cells show darker overlap with each other than with others, and what is the biological or technical meaning? The caption states 'light-to-dark yellow scale to indicate the degree of overlap' but provides no numerical reference or formula.
- HARD: The relationship between 57 proteins and 165 proteoforms is never quantified or explained. Are these 165 proteoforms distributed across all 57 proteins, or are some proteins represented by only one proteoform? The bulk sample identified 41 proteins and 83 proteoforms; the single-cell sample identified 57 proteins and 165 proteoforms. The text states 'identified biomolecules largely overlapping those observed in the bulk sample' (Figures S7) but does not specify the overlap percentage or distribution. This ambiguity makes it unclear whether the increase in proteoforms reflects greater depth of analysis or genuine biological heterogeneity.
- HARD: The claim 'proteoform composition varied substantially among cells' (abstract) and 'high proportion of unique proteoforms in each cell' (main text, Figure 2B) are not quantified. What percentage of proteoforms are unique to each cell? What percentage are shared across all 13 cells? The heatmap in Figure 2B is meant to show this but is uninterpretable without a numerical scale.
- SOFT: The term 'shotgun' in the title and throughout the text is not defined. In the context of proteomics, 'shotgun' typically refers to untargeted, data-dependent acquisition of peptides, but here it is applied to intact proteins. The term may confuse readers unfamiliar with top-down MS terminology. A brief clarification (e.g., 'untargeted LC-MS analysis of intact proteins') would improve accessibility.
- SOFT: The phrase 'novel proteoform signatures' (summary) is vague. Are these signatures novel because they have not been reported in cardiomyocytes before, or because they are new PTM combinations? The text later clarifies that some modifications (e.g., MLC-2 T51 phosphorylation) are known, but the trimethylation and combinatorial modifications are new. The distinction could be clearer upfront.
- SOFT: The Methods section states that 'Proteins and proteoforms were identified as follows: 1) complete sequences with unexpected modifications were identified using a wide absolute precursor mass tolerance (200 Da)' but does not explain why 200 Da was chosen or how this threshold was validated. The text mentions 'Expanding the delta search to 200 Da revealed many previously unknown structural deviations' but does not justify this choice or discuss false discovery risk.
- SOFT: The paper claims to provide 'the first evidence of MLC-2 trimethylation in single cardiomyocytes from mouse' and 'the first identification of MLC-2 concurrently harboring trimethylation and phosphorylation on a single protein backbone,' but does not cite whether these modifications have been reported in bulk cardiomyocytes or other tissues. The novelty claim would be stronger with explicit comparison to prior literature.

## Questions
- What is the formal definition of 'proteoform' as used in this work? Does it include all PTM variants, truncations, and sequence variants, or only a subset? How does this definition align with the HUPO Proteomics Standards Initiative definition?
- In Figure 2, what is the quantitative scale for the heatmap color gradient? Can you provide the actual overlap percentages or counts for a few representative cell pairs (e.g., the pair with the darkest overlap and the pair with the lightest)?
- Of the 57 proteins identified in the single-cell analysis, how many are represented by only one proteoform, and how many by multiple proteoforms? Is this distribution provided in Table S2?
- What percentage of the 165 proteoforms identified in single cells are unique to one cell, shared by 2–5 cells, or shared by all 13 cells? This quantification would directly support the 'substantial heterogeneity' claim.
- Why was a 200 Da mass tolerance window chosen for the delta search? Was this threshold validated against a false discovery rate, and if so, what was the FDR at this tolerance?
- The bulk sample (10 ng total protein) identified 41 proteins and 83 proteoforms, while the single-cell samples identified 57 proteins and 165 proteoforms. Can you clarify whether the single-cell numbers represent the union across all 13 cells or the average per cell? How does the protein content of a single cardiomyocyte compare to the 10 ng bulk sample?
- For the novel modifications (MLC-2 trimethylation, MYL3 trimethylation, Qcr7 succinylation), have these been reported in bulk cardiomyocyte proteomes or other cardiac tissues? If not, how confident are you that these are true modifications rather than artifacts of the lysis buffer or MS analysis?
