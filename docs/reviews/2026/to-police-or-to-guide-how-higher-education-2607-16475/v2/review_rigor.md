# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This qualitative study investigates how CS instructors design and implement generative AI policies, based on 13 semi-structured interviews. The work is well-executed within its scope and addresses a timely, under-studied question about instructor-student relationships mediated by AI policy. However, the manuscript contains several load-bearing claims that outrun the evidence provided, particularly around causation, generalization, and the effectiveness of proposed interventions. The abstract and title make strong claims about policy outcomes ('worsening relationships', 'extra burden of policing') that are supported only by instructor self-report without independent verification. The distinction between 'learning harms' and 'assessment harms' is conceptually useful but the evidence for their causal separation is correlational. Most critically, the paper recommends 'learning-oriented' policies as superior to 'assessment-oriented' ones, but provides no empirical evidence that these policies are actually effective—only that they are theoretically appealing and that some instructors have tried them. This is a significant gap between the prescriptive claims in the abstract and discussion versus what the data actually establish.

## Strengths
- The research question is timely and addresses a genuine gap: prior work focuses on instructor attitudes toward AI tools, but this study examines how policies actually mediate instructor-student relationships, which is less studied.
- The reflexive thematic analysis methodology is transparently described, with researchers acknowledging their positionality and analytic process rather than claiming false objectivity.
- The interview protocol is well-structured across four sections (teaching context, policy on paper, enforcement, design rationale), capturing both espoused and enacted policies.
- The sample is reasonably diverse in institutional type (9 institutions, mix of public/private, research/non-research) and course levels (CS1 through advanced electives), strengthening the scope within US higher education.
- The distinction between 'learning harms' (cognitive offloading, illusion of competence, social isolation) and 'assessment harms' (inability to tell who learned) is conceptually clarifying and well-illustrated with instructor quotes.
- The paper honestly reports tensions and trade-offs: e.g., instructors acknowledge that paper exams may harm students with test anxiety while still adopting them, and that policing is burdensome.
- The discussion of responsibility-shifting (from instructor policing to student self-regulation to questioning whether students should bear this burden) is nuanced and reflects genuine ethical tension.

## Weaknesses
-  **HARD: Title and abstract claim causal outcomes not established by the data.** The title states 'instructors report extra burden of policing student AI use behaviors and worsening relationships with students.' The abstract claims policies 'can preserve assessment integrity in the short term' but 'instructors report extra burden of policing student AI use behaviors and worsening relationships with students.' These are presented as findings, but the evidence is entirely self-reported instructor perception without independent measurement of relationship quality, student experience, or actual policy outcomes. The phrase 'worsening relationships' implies a causal effect of the policies, but the data show only that instructors perceive strain and that they attribute it to policing—not that the policies demonstrably worsen relationships or that this is the mechanism. This should be reframed as 'instructors report perceiving...' or 'instructors attribute...to...'
-  **HARD: Effectiveness claims for 'learning-oriented' policies lack empirical support.** The abstract and discussion recommend 'AI policies that are more learning-oriented and could guide students toward healthier AI usage instead' and state 'we make recommendations on AI policies that are more learning-oriented.' Section 4.3 ('Guiding AI Use Does not Require Fundamental Changes') presents four approaches: transparent AI instruction, promoting metacognition with formative assessments, inspiring motivation, and modeling good AI use. However, the paper provides no evidence that any of these policies actually improve student learning, reduce harmful AI use, or achieve their stated goals. The authors acknowledge this explicitly: 'this study only involves interviews with instructors, so it does not have the empirical evidence to suggest whether these learning-oriented AI policies are effective' (p. 18). Yet the abstract and title present these as recommendations without this crucial caveat. The claim 'we make recommendations' is not matched by evidence that the recommendations work—only that some instructors have tried them and find them theoretically appealing.
-  **HARD: Causal language used for observational associations.** The paper states 'AI policies primarily seek to AI-proof assessments without directly addressing student learning' (abstract) and 'instructors more readily reacted to assessment harms by AI-proofing their assessment' (p. 14). The evidence is that instructors adopted these policies and cite assessment concerns as their reasoning. However, the data do not establish that assessment harms *caused* the policy adoption or that instructors *would not have* adopted these policies for other reasons. The causal framing ('reacted to', 'in response to') is inferred from instructor statements about motivation, not from a design that manipulates or controls for alternative explanations. This should be hedged: 'instructors cite assessment harms as a primary motivation for...' or 'instructors report that assessment concerns drove...'
-  **HARD: Generalization of 'most instructors' claim without quantification.** The abstract states 'AI policies primarily seek to AI-proof assessments without directly addressing student learning.' The paper claims 'most instructors more readily reacted to assessment harms' (p. 14). However, the paper provides no count or proportion. Of 13 instructors, how many adopted paper exams, weighted exams heavily, or explicitly stated assessment concerns as their primary motivation? The paper notes '9 interviewees adopting some form of' paper exams (p. 10), but does not clearly state how many cited assessment harms versus learning harms as their primary driver, or whether the remaining 4 adopted other assessment-oriented policies. The claim 'most instructors' and 'primarily' requires a transparent count or explicit acknowledgment that this is a qualitative impression, not a quantified finding.
-  **SOFT: The distinction between learning harms and assessment harms is conceptually useful but the evidence for their causal separation is weak.** The paper treats these as two independent problems requiring different interventions. However, the data show that instructors perceive both simultaneously and that the policies often conflate them. For example, paper exams are adopted to address assessment integrity but also affect student learning (negatively for some, per instructor I5). The paper does not establish that these are truly separable harms or that addressing one independently of the other is feasible. The framing risks suggesting a false dichotomy.
-  **SOFT: 'Illusion of competence' is attributed to AI design, but the evidence is indirect.** Section 4.1.2 argues that AI tools like Copilot 'frame themselves as helping tools' and thus 'cause students to erroneously believe' they have learned. The evidence is instructor observation that students conflate code reading with code writing, and quotes from instructors explaining why this happens. However, the paper does not present data on how students actually perceive these tools, whether they read the framing as claimed, or whether the design is the primary driver versus other factors (e.g., time pressure, motivation, prior beliefs). The causal attribution to tool design is inferred, not directly tested. This should be hedged: 'instructors observe that students may conflate...' or 'instructors hypothesize that tool framing contributes to...'
-  **SOFT: Social isolation attributed to AI without ruling out alternative explanations.** Section 4.1.3 reports reduced office hours attendance and help-seeking, attributed to AI use. However, the data are observational and post-hoc. The paper does not rule out alternative explanations: e.g., post-pandemic shifts in student behavior, increased course load, changes in course structure, or student demographics. One instructor (I5) notes students 'seem more depressed, they seem more down' and attributes this to solitude, but acknowledges 'it is difficult to directly attribute...to GenAI usage' (p. 8). The paper should more consistently hedge these causal claims.
-  **SOFT: The paper claims instructors 'recognize that AI tools could harm student learning' (abstract) but the evidence is mixed.** Some instructors express concern about cognitive offloading and illusion of competence, but others (e.g., I8, I10) view AI use as a symptom of lacking motivation rather than a cause of harm. The abstract presents instructor concern as a unified finding, but the data show heterogeneous views. This should be reframed: 'some instructors recognize...' or 'instructors express varying concerns about...'
-  **SOFT: The paper does not clearly distinguish between policies instructors have formally adopted versus those they have considered or discussed.** For example, the paper reports that I1 used a 'honeypot' policy of leaving disallowed code in assignments to detect AI use, but does not clarify whether this is a formal policy or an ad-hoc experiment. Similarly, some instructors discuss policies they have considered but not implemented. The analysis conflates 'implemented' and 'discussed' policies, which affects the claim about what instructors are actually doing.
-  **SOFT: Scope limitations are acknowledged but not fully integrated into claims.** The paper notes it focuses on US institutions, undergraduate CS, and instructor perspectives only (p. 18). However, claims like 'instructors perceive challenges from AI from two dimensions' (p. 9) are presented without consistent reminders that this is a US-specific, instructor-only sample. The generalization to 'CS education' or 'higher education' more broadly is not clearly bounded.
-  **SOFT: The paper does not report negative or null findings about learning-oriented policies.** Section 4.3 presents four learning-oriented approaches, but does not report whether any instructors tried these and found them ineffective, or whether any instructors abandoned them. The absence of such findings is not discussed, which could reflect selective reporting of the more optimistic cases.

## Questions
- In the abstract, you state that policies 'can preserve assessment integrity in the short term' but also 'instructors report extra burden of policing student AI use behaviors and worsening relationships with students.' Can you clarify whether the 'worsening relationships' is an observed outcome of the policies, or an instructor perception/concern? Do you have evidence (e.g., student surveys, behavioral data) that relationships actually worsened, or only instructor self-report?
- You recommend 'learning-oriented' policies in the abstract and discussion, but acknowledge on p. 18 that 'this study only involves interviews with instructors, so it does not have the empirical evidence to suggest whether these learning-oriented AI policies are effective.' Should the abstract be reframed to state that these are 'promising approaches' or 'approaches instructors find theoretically appealing' rather than recommendations? Or should you conduct follow-up work to measure outcomes?
- In Table 2, you list which courses instructors teach, but do not report how many of the 13 instructors adopted assessment-oriented policies (e.g., paper exams, weighted exams) versus learning-oriented policies. Can you provide a count or breakdown? This would clarify whether 'most instructors' truly prioritize assessment-oriented policies, or whether the distribution is more mixed.
- You state that instructors 'recognize that AI tools could harm student learning' (abstract), but Section 4.1 shows that some instructors (e.g., I8, I10) view AI use as a symptom of motivation problems rather than a direct cause of harm. How many instructors expressed each view? Is 'recognize harm' a fair characterization of the full sample?
- In Section 4.1.2, you argue that AI tools like Copilot 'frame themselves as helping tools' and thus cause students to develop an 'illusion of competence.' Did you interview students about how they perceive these tools, or is this inference based on instructor observation? If the latter, should the claim be hedged as 'instructors hypothesize that...' rather than 'tools cause...'?
- In Section 4.1.3, you attribute reduced office hours attendance to AI use, but also note that 'it is difficult to directly attribute...to GenAI usage' (p. 8). What other factors did instructors mention as possible causes? Did you ask instructors to estimate the relative contribution of AI versus other factors (e.g., post-pandemic shifts, course design changes)?
- You note that instructors struggle to enforce AI policies due to detection difficulty. Did any instructors report abandoning or modifying policies they had initially adopted? If so, how many, and what were their reasons? This would strengthen the claim that assessment-oriented policies are unsustainable.
- In Section 4.3, you present four learning-oriented approaches, but do not report whether instructors who tried these found them effective. Did any instructors report that transparent AI instruction, formative assessments, or motivation-building activities reduced harmful AI use or improved learning? Or is the evidence only that these approaches are theoretically appealing?
- You state that 'instructors perceive two types of harms from AI' (p. 3), but the evidence for 'assessment harms' is that instructors cannot tell who learned, not that AI directly harms assessment. Is the distinction between 'learning harms' (direct effects on student cognition) and 'assessment harms' (effects on instructor confidence in grades) clearly supported, or is it a conceptual framework you imposed?
- The paper focuses on instructor perspectives and does not include student interviews or outcome data. How confident are you that instructor perceptions of 'worsening relationships' or 'social isolation' match student experiences? Would student data change your conclusions?
