# Decision Letter

**Decision:** major

## Summary of Evaluation
This is a competently executed qualitative interview study (n=13 US CS instructors, reflexive thematic analysis) with a genuinely useful analytical framing — the learning-harm/assessment-harm distinction and policy as a mediator of instructor-student relationships. Four of seven reviewers judged the work sound within scope (clarity 4@5, ethics 4@5, literature 4@4, novelty 4@4); the critical votes came from methodology (3@4), rigor (3@5) and reproducibility (2@5), and the confidence-weighted mean is 3.41.

I concur with the meta-review's landing on major rather than minor, and my reasoning tracks which criticisms survived the rebuttal. Two do. First, the front-matter overclaiming: the title and abstract state 'worsening relationships' and AI 'harm[s] student learning' as findings when the evidence is instructor perception, and the abstract 'recommends' learning-oriented policies that Section 5.3 concedes are untested. The authors fully concede this and propose a specific, adequate remedy (perception-attributing language audited across title, abstract and Discussion topic sentences; recommendations recast as hypotheses). Because the fix changes what the paper claims rather than how it reads, it is a substantive revision, not a copy-edit. Second, the transparency gap: no codebook, no code-to-quote mapping, no documented disagreement-resolution procedure, no per-theme counts, no data-availability statement. Again conceded, with concrete deposits proposed.

I weighed three of the authors' disagreements as credible and they do not generate required revisions. (i) On verbatim transcript deposition: the authors identify this as the one load-bearing critique they cannot meet, and their reason — a small, identifiable population of US CS instructors, IRB-reviewed anonymity commitments, re-identification risk from institutional and course detail — is legitimate for this genre. I do not require transcript deposition; a codebook, audit-trail memo and an explicit data-availability statement explaining the constraint discharge the obligation. (ii) On inter-rater reliability: the manuscript pre-declares in Section 3.3 that it adopts reflexive TA and documents process 'in lieu of reporting inter-rater reliability'; this is a defensible methodological commitment, not an omission, and I will not require IRR metrics. (iii) On saturation and on the absence of student outcome data: an instructor-perspective study is a legitimate design for RQ1/RQ2, and the flaw is in wording, not design. No new data collection is required.

On novelty, the authors' distinction from Lau & Guo (2023) (anticipated vs. enacted policy, three years on) and Luo (syllabus-based student perceptions) is substantive; it should be stated in the manuscript rather than only in the rebuttal.

The compliance audit flags three HARD methods gaps (consent process, inclusion/exclusion criteria, data-availability statement) and no HARD citation gaps. None of these obstruct evaluation of the central claims, but all must be added. Once the concessions are executed as proposed, I expect this to be acceptable without a further review round.

## Required Revisions
1. Remove causal/declarative framing from the title and abstract. Reword so that perception-based findings are stated as perceptions (e.g. 'instructors report perceiving worsening relationships with students', 'instructors attribute learning harms to student AI use'). Audit every causal verb in the abstract and in the topic sentences of Sections 4 and 5 against this standard, and state in a revision note where each change was made.
2. Recast the abstract's closing recommendation and Section 5.2 so the four learning-oriented approaches are presented explicitly as untested but theoretically motivated hypotheses for future research, not as recommendations. Flag their evidentiary status in the abstract itself, not only in Section 5.3.
3. Replace bare vague quantifiers ('most instructors', 'several instructors', 'many') throughout Section 4 with explicit counts out of 13. Include a table classifying each interviewee as adopting primarily assessment-oriented, primarily learning-oriented, or mixed policies, and give counts for the load-bearing claim that 'most instructors more readily reacted to assessment harms', as well as for the illusion-of-competence and social-isolation themes.
4. Deposit, as a linked supplementary file, (a) the codebook (initial codes, consolidated thematic codes, definitions) and (b) an audit-trail memo describing how the three coders reconciled disagreements across coding meetings. Verbatim transcripts are NOT required; the accepted confidentiality rationale should be stated in the paper.
5. Add an explicit data-availability statement (HARD audit gap) that names what is deposited (codebook, audit memo, interview protocol if permissible) and states plainly why transcripts cannot be shared, citing the identifiability of the participant population and the IRB commitment. Also state whether any custom analysis software was used or whether coding was fully manual.
6. Add a short subsection reporting negative and disconfirming cases: instructors who perceived no learning harms, who were satisfied with purely assessment-oriented policies, or whose framing diverged (e.g. the I8/I10 motivation-centered account), and state how these were handled in theme development.
7. Document the informed-consent process (HARD audit gap): state whether written or verbal consent was obtained, at what point, and whether consent was waived under the exemption.
8. State explicit participant inclusion/exclusion criteria (HARD audit gap) — e.g. current teaching status, country, institution type, course level, recency of teaching — alongside the existing description of purposive and snowball recruitment.
9. Add Funding and Competing Interests statements, including disclosure of any financial or advisory ties to AI tool vendors or ed-tech platforms (or an explicit statement that none exist).
10. Define 'learning harm', 'assessment harm' and 'second-order costs' at first use and apply the terms consistently; reconcile the current alternation between 'assessment harms' and 'assessment integrity'.
11. Move the rebuttal's positioning argument into Section 2.2: state concretely that Lau & Guo (2023) studied anticipated adaptations before multi-semester experience with enacted policy, that Luo (2024/2025) analyses student perceptions of written syllabi, and identify which findings here are new (e.g. the 'honeypot' detection strategy, responsibility-shifting rhetoric, the harm taxonomy).

## Minor Suggestions
- Complete bibliographic details for recent citations flagged by the citation audit — [43] (Lau et al. 2026, no venue) and [72] (Tate et al. 2026, preprint without DOI) — and add DOIs where available for 2025–2026 peer-reviewed items.
- Where the manuscript applies frameworks analogically rather than citing findings (the abstinence-only education analogy in Section 5.1, the harm-reduction framework in Section 5.2), signal in the text that the extension to AI policy is the authors' own reasoning rather than a result from the cited work.
- Add page or chapter specificity to the Vygotsky (1978) citation.
- Consider adding a sentence in Section 3.3 noting that the sample of 13 spans nine institutions and CS1 through advanced electives, and that contextual diversity rather than saturation was the design goal — this pre-empts the saturation objection without conceding a positivist criterion the paper explicitly rejects.
- Consider depositing the full interview protocol as a supplement; Table 1 currently gives only example questions.
