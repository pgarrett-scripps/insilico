# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript makes a real but incremental contribution by reframing AI policies through the lens of learning harms vs. assessment harms and emphasizing instructor-student relationships as a key outcome. However, the core empirical findings—that instructors adopt paper exams, struggle with detection, lack metacognitive awareness in students, and value transparency—are largely consistent with or directly build on Lau & Guo (2023) and Luo (2024, 2025). The manuscript's primary novelty is the explicit categorization and relationship-centered framing, which is solid but not transformative. Three hard issues require attention: insufficient clarity on what is new vs. confirmatory, unsupported causal claims about relationship strain, and recommendations for policies without empirical validation.

## Strengths
- Clear problem reframing: The distinction between learning harms and assessment harms is well-articulated and provides a useful analytical lens that sharpens the policy discussion beyond prior 'ban vs. allow' framings.
- Relationship-centered perspective: Centering instructor-student relationships as a key outcome of AI policies is a legitimate contribution that complements existing tool-focused and student-focused research, correctly identifying that policy is a social contract.
- Honest positioning of limitations: The authors acknowledge that findings are instructor self-reports without independent validation, that the study is US-only and undergraduate-focused, and that they lack empirical evidence on learning-oriented policy effectiveness.
- Methodological transparency: The reflexive thematic analysis approach is clearly documented, including researcher positionality and iterative coding process, supporting auditability.
- Concrete policy examples: The manuscript provides specific, actionable examples of both assessment-oriented policies (paper exams, mandatory exam passing thresholds) and learning-oriented alternatives (transparent AI instruction, formative assessments, modeling good use), grounded in instructor accounts.

## Weaknesses
- Insufficient novelty verification against Lau & Guo (2023) and Luo (2024, 2025): The manuscript's finding that instructors adopt assessment-focused policies appears to be a direct empirical follow-up to Lau & Guo's speculative work rather than a novel discovery, and the core recommendation for transparency directly echoes Luo (2025). The manuscript does not clearly articulate what is new beyond confirming and elaborating on these prior findings.
- Vague claim about 'second-order costs': The manuscript asserts that assessment-oriented policies incur 'second-order costs' to instructor-student relationships, but evidence is largely anecdotal instructor statements about burden and discomfort (e.g., 'very human labor intensive,' not wanting to be an 'AI police officer'), not systematic measurement of actual time cost, relationship deterioration from student perspectives, or empirical relationship strain.
- Unsubstantiated claim about learning-oriented policies: The manuscript recommends four learning-oriented approaches but explicitly states in section 5.2 that 'this study only involves interviews with instructors, so it does not have the empirical evidence to suggest whether these learning-oriented AI policies are effective and should be recommended,' yet the title and framing position these as solutions, reading as advocacy without evidence.
- Positioning relative to Lau et al. (2026): The manuscript cites Lau et al. (2026) as finding that many CS instructors are open to integrating AI tools, yet the current finding that instructors adopt restrictive assessment-focused policies seems to contradict or complicate this openness claim, but the tension is not discussed.
- Overstated novelty of the 'policing vs. guiding' framing: The title suggests a novel dichotomy, but prior work (e.g., Luo 2025 on transparency, harm reduction literature) already discusses the tension between enforcement and support, making the framing useful but not unprecedented.
- Limited scope acknowledged but not addressed: The study is restricted to 13 instructors from 9 US institutions teaching undergraduate CS, but the manuscript makes broad claims about 'CS instructors' and 'CS education' without qualifying representation, and the small sample size may overweight individual outliers like I9's learning-oriented approach.
- Unclear differentiation from syllabus analysis work: The manuscript cites Ali et al. (2025) and Tom Tong et al. (2025) as analyzing AI policies in syllabi but does not clearly compare findings or explain whether reported policies are consistent with written syllabi policies.

## Questions
- Can the authors explicitly state what findings in this manuscript are not already reported or implied by Lau & Guo (2023), Luo (2024, 2025), or other cited prior work? The learning-harm vs. assessment-harm distinction is the clearest candidate, but is this a novel categorization or a restatement of existing concerns?
- What evidence supports the claim that assessment-oriented policies 'strain instructor-student relationships'? The manuscript quotes instructors' discomfort with enforcement, but does it have evidence that students perceive relationship strain, or that policies actually harm relationships (e.g., via reduced help-seeking, increased academic integrity cases, or student surveys)?
- Given that the manuscript explicitly lacks empirical evidence for the effectiveness of recommended policies, how should the recommendations section be framed? Are these exploratory hypotheses for future research, or are the authors claiming they are evidence-based?
- Are there concurrent or very recent studies on instructor AI policies that should be discussed? The search results were limited by rate-limiting, so a more thorough preprint search may reveal recent work that preempts or parallels these findings.
- Is the 'policing vs. guiding' dichotomy novel, or does it restate the tension between enforcement-based and support-based approaches already discussed in prior work on academic integrity, student motivation, and harm reduction?
