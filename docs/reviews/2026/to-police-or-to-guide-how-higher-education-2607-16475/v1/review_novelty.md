# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
This qualitative interview study makes a useful but incremental contribution to understanding how CS instructors design and implement AI policies. The core novelty claim—that prior work was 'tool-oriented' while this work focuses on 'instructor-student relationships'—is overstated; concurrent work (Luo 2025, Ali et al. 2025, Tom Tong et al. 2025) already addresses policies and relationships. The distinction between 'learning harms' and 'assessment harms' is a helpful analytical lens, and the finding that most instructors default to assessment-oriented (policing) rather than learning-oriented (guiding) policies is valuable. However, the manuscript does not clearly articulate what is genuinely novel beyond prior work, and the recommended learning-oriented policies largely synthesize existing pedagogical practices. The work is sound and contextually important, but its novelty and contribution are positioned more strongly than the evidence supports.

## Strengths
- Useful analytical distinction: The explicit separation of 'learning harms' (cognitive offloading, illusion of competence, social isolation) from 'assessment harms' (inability to evaluate true learning) provides a clear framework for understanding instructor decision-making, even if the underlying phenomena are documented in prior work.
- Implementation-level detail: The manuscript goes beyond documenting what policies exist (as in Ali et al. 2025, Tom Tong et al. 2025) to examine how they are enforced in practice and what instructors perceive as their consequences—a finer-grained analysis.
- Honest acknowledgment of instructor burden: The finding that assessment-oriented policies (paper exams, detection labor, high-stakes grading) impose significant costs on instructors and strain relationships is well-documented with quotes and is a useful corrective to policy recommendations that ignore implementation reality.
- Concrete policy alternatives: The manuscript identifies specific learning-oriented alternatives (transparent AI instruction, modeling good use, formative assessment, motivation-building) that instructors have already experimented with, providing a constructive counterpoint to policing approaches.

## Weaknesses
- Overstated novelty claim: The abstract and introduction position this as addressing a gap in 'tool-oriented research' and focusing on 'instructor-student relationships,' but Luo (2025) [51] explicitly studies 'How does GenAI affect trust in teacher-student relationships?' and Lau & Guo (2023) [44] examined instructor perspectives on adaptation. The manuscript does not clearly articulate what is novel beyond these works.
- Insufficient differentiation from concurrent work: The manuscript cites Ali et al. (2025) [3] and Tom Tong et al. (2025) [73], which also analyze instructor AI policies in syllabi, but does not explain how this work's methods, sample, or findings differ. Are the samples overlapping? Do the findings contradict or extend prior work? This is left unclear.
- Learning vs. assessment harms as empirical finding, not novel framework: The manuscript presents the distinction between learning harms and assessment harms as an empirical discovery, but the underlying concerns (cognitive offloading, assessment integrity) are well-established in the cited literature. The novelty of the framing is not clearly articulated—is this a new conceptual model, or a useful way to organize existing observations?
- Learning-oriented policy recommendations lack novelty: The recommended policies (transparency, modeling good use, formative assessment, motivation/engagement) are standard pedagogical practices. The manuscript does not claim to have invented these; rather, it documents that some instructors are already using them. The contribution is thus primarily in connecting these practices to AI policy design, which is incremental.
- Unclear scope of 'first' or 'novel' claims: The manuscript does not use explicit 'first' or 'novel' language, but the framing implies this is the first work to examine the relationship between AI policies and instructor-student relationships. However, Luo (2025) [51] already examines this relationship empirically. The manuscript should clarify what is genuinely new.
- Limited generalizability not acknowledged in contribution claims: The sample is n=13 from US institutions only, undergraduate CS only. The manuscript acknowledges this in limitations but frames findings as broadly applicable. This is a soft issue but worth noting.

## Questions
- How does this work's sample, methods, or findings differ from or extend Ali et al. (2025) [3] and Tom Tong et al. (2025) [73], which also analyze instructor AI policies? Are there overlapping participants or institutions?
- The manuscript distinguishes 'learning harms' from 'assessment harms,' but are these truly novel categories, or a reframing of existing concerns (cognitive offloading, assessment integrity) documented in prior work? If the latter, what is the conceptual contribution of the distinction?
- Luo (2025) [51] explicitly studies trust in teacher-student relationships in the context of AI policies. How does this work's analysis of 'fraying relationships' and 'responsibility rhetoric' extend or differ from Luo's findings?
- The recommended learning-oriented policies (transparency, modeling, formative assessment, motivation) are standard pedagogical practices. What is novel about connecting them to AI policy design, versus applying existing pedagogy to a new context?
- The manuscript states instructors 'perceive' certain effects (e.g., reduced office hours, illusion of competence) but relies entirely on self-report without triangulation. How confident are you in these perceptions as evidence of actual effects, versus instructor interpretations?
