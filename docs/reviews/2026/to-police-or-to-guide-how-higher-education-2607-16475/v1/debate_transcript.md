# Debate Transcript

## Advocate — round 1

This manuscript should be accepted because it makes a genuine, well-executed contribution to an under-studied angle: how AI policy mediates instructor-student relationships, not just instructor-tool attitudes. As the novelty reviewer concedes, the learning-harm vs. assessment-harm distinction 'sharpens the policy discussion beyond prior ban-vs-allow framings,' and the methodology reviewer agrees this is 'a novel and important angle' addressing 'a genuine gap.' The reflexive thematic analysis is appropriately matched to the exploratory RQs, with transparent positionality statements and honest, non-representative framing—exactly what reviewers praise ('authors transparently acknowledge... avoiding false claims of generalizability'). The paper's honesty is itself a strength: it explicitly states in Section 5.3 that it lacks empirical evidence for learning-oriented policy effectiveness, and documents contradictory instructor views (I5's test anxiety concerns alongside adopting paper exams; I8/I10's motivation-based framing versus cognitive-offloading framing)—this is not overclaiming but faithful reporting of a messy reality. The concrete, richly quoted findings (paper exam adoption across 9 of 13 interviewees, the 'honeypot' detection strategy, the reflect forms) give practitioners and researchers immediately useful material, which the literature reviewer confirms is 'not contradicted by cited work' and extends Lau & Guo (2023) and Luo (2024/2025) into genuinely new territory: enacted policy and enforcement, not just speculation or syllabus text. Every substantive weakness raised—hedging language, quantification of 'most,' terminology consistency, data deposition—is a revision-fixable presentation issue, not a flaw in the core empirical contribution or research design.

**Key points:**
- Fills a genuine gap: instructor-student relationship dynamics under AI, not just tool attitudes, as multiple reviewers acknowledge
- Learning-harm vs assessment-harm distinction is a novel, useful analytical contribution per novelty and literature reviewers
- Methodology (reflexive TA) is appropriate for exploratory qualitative aims and transparently documented
- Authors already self-limit overclaiming by stating lack of evidence for policy effectiveness in Section 5.3
- Cited weaknesses (hedging, quantification, terminology, data deposition) are addressable in revision, not fatal design flaws

## Skeptic — round 1

The advocate calls every flaw a 'presentation issue,' but the rigor and methodology reviews identify something deeper: a construct-validity gap baked into the paper's core claims. The abstract asserts policies cause 'worsening relationships' and that AI 'harm[s] student learning'—but as the methodology reviewer states, this is a HARD threat: 'instructor perceptions of learning harms and assessment harms are treated as facts about student learning' with 'no student learning data, grades, retention, or skill assessments.' I5 explicitly says it is 'difficult to directly attribute' observed solitude to AI, yet the paper still frames it as a learning harm. This is FIXABLE by reframing language ('instructors perceive/attribute' rather than declarative causal claims)—but it is not merely stylistic, since the abstract, title, and Section 5.2 recommendations currently rest on this unearned causal footing. More seriously, the rigor reviewer flags that the paper recommends learning-oriented policies as the solution while admitting on p.18 it has 'no empirical evidence' they work—yet the abstract still says 'we make recommendations.' That is advocacy exceeding evidence, and while relabeling as 'promising hypotheses' is fixable, the current manuscript oversells its prescriptive payload, which is the paper's stated contribution. Separately, reproducibility scored 2/5: no transcripts, no codebook, no audit trail for a three-coder reflexive process making strong claims about 13 people's beliefs—this is FIXABLE via deposition but currently blocks any independent check on whether themes reflect data or researcher interpretation. None of these are fatal to the concept, but collectively they mean the manuscript as written overclaims relative to what reviewers agree the data supports.
