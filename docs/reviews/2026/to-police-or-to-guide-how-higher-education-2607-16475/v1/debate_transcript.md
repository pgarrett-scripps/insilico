# Debate Transcript

## Advocate — round 1

This manuscript makes a genuine, well-executed contribution that the panel largely confirms. The methods reviewer calls the thematic analysis 'competently executed with transparent documentation,' and the ethics reviewer finds full IRB compliance and exemplary participant protection. Critically, the core empirical contribution—the learning-harms/assessment-harms distinction—is not merely relabeled prior work but a genuine analytic achievement: it explains a real puzzle in the data, why instructors who identify cognitive offloading, illusion of competence, and social isolation as harms (Section 4.1) nonetheless gravitate toward paper exams and grading-weight policies (Section 4.2) that don't address any of those mechanisms. That gap—documented with 9/13 instructors adopting paper exams while simultaneously voicing discomfort (I5's test anxiety admission, I1's backward-design concern)—is a substantive, quotable finding, not an artifact of framing.

On rigor, the reviewer's central complaint is wording, not substance: claims like 'worsening relationships' should be reframed as 'instructors report burden and adversarial dynamics.' That is a revision instruction, not a reason for rejection—the underlying observation (I4: 'I don't want to run around behind them and try and catch them using AI'; I10 on 'adverse learning environment') is honestly reported as instructor perception throughout the body text, per the rigor reviewer's own concession that 'the paper avoids false certainty about causation in most of the body text.'

On novelty, the reviewer concedes 'the finding that most instructors default to assessment-oriented rather than learning-oriented policies is valuable' and that this offers 'finer-grained analysis' than prior syllabus-coding studies (Ali et al., Tom Tong et al.). The abstract's framing claim can be softened without touching the contribution itself.

The reproducibility and clarity gaps (codebook, abstract wording, quantifier precision) are all textual fixes, achievable in revision without new data collection.

**Key points:**
- Methods and ethics reviewers confirm sound execution: transparent reflexive TA process, IRB exemption, strong participant protection
- The learning-harm/assessment-harm distinction explains a genuine empirical puzzle (why instructors diagnose one problem but treat another), not just relabeling
- Rigor reviewer's critiques are about wording/hedging in the abstract, not about the validity of the underlying qualitative findings, which are appropriately hedged in the body
- Novelty reviewer explicitly concedes the assessment-vs-learning-oriented policy finding is 'valuable' and offers finer-grained analysis than prior syllabus studies
- Reproducibility, clarity, and citation-precision issues are all revision-fixable (codebook release, abstract rewrite, citation checks) without undermining the core contribution

## Skeptic — round 1

The advocate frames rigor's objections as mere wording, but the problem is structural, not cosmetic. The paper's stated contribution is normative: it 'make[s] recommendations on AI policies that are more learning-oriented' (Abstract, Sec 5.2) and claims assessment policies cause 'worsening relationships with students' and 'hinder student learning.' The rigor reviewer flags these as HARD overclaims because no student data, relationship metrics, or outcome measures exist anywhere in the design—only instructor self-report of their own discomfort (I4, I10). If these causal/prescriptive claims are stripped per rigor's own suggested rewording ('instructors report burden' rather than 'policies worsen relationships'), the paper's title, abstract, and Section 5.2 recommendations no longer describe what the evidence supports. That's not a word-swap; it requires re-architecting the paper's central claims, which the novelty reviewer notes are already thin once trimmed—conceding only that the assessment/learning distinction is 'valuable' while stating the recommended policies 'largely synthesize existing pedagogical practices' and that novelty versus Luo (2025) and Ali et al. (2025) is unclear.

Compounding this, reproducibility flags three HARD gaps: no deposited transcripts, no codebook, no full interview protocol. For a study whose entire contribution is an interpretive thematic analysis, this means the 'genuine analytic achievement' the advocate touts cannot be independently verified—we cannot check whether the learning/assessment-harm distinction emerged from data or was imposed by researchers already primed to find it (three authors with prior teaching stakes in the debate, per Sec 3.3).

Methods and ethics soundness are real and conceded. But the paper as written oversells causal, generalizable conclusions its design cannot bear, and lacks the artifacts needed to verify its qualitative claims.

**Key points:**
- FATAL-adjacent (fixable only via substantial rewrite): normative recommendations in Abstract/Sec 5.2 rest on instructor self-report with no student/outcome data—rigor reviewer's HARD flag on 'worsening relationships' and 'hinder learning' claims
- Stripping the causal language per rigor's fix leaves a thinner contribution, which novelty reviewer already scores low (3/5), questioning differentiation from Luo (2025), Ali et al. (2025), Tom Tong et al. (2025)
- FIXABLE but currently unaddressed: reproducibility reviewer's HARD flags—no transcripts, no codebook, no full protocol—prevent verification that the learning/assessment-harm framework emerged from data rather than researcher priors
- Advocate has not engaged with the compounding effect: overclaiming plus unverifiable analysis together undermine confidence in the paper's central contribution, not just its prose
- Concede: methods execution and ethics compliance are genuinely sound, and this is not a fatal flaw in the study's conception—but the manuscript as submitted is not yet ready for acceptance
