# Related-Work & Citations Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript demonstrates sound citation practices with accurate attribution, appropriate use of primary sources, and current references spanning 2023–2026. No false attributions or unsupported load-bearing claims were detected. The related work is well-positioned relative to prior studies on instructor attitudes toward AI (Lau & Guo 2023), student trust and transparency (Luo 2025), and cognitive offloading effects. A few recent papers on AI-integrated assessment and trust moderation could strengthen the literature positioning, but their absence does not undermine the work. The distinction between assessment-oriented and learning-oriented policies appears somewhat novel and is not contradicted by cited work.

## Strengths
- All spot-checked references (Albluwi 2019, Bandura 1977, Baym 2010, Braun & Clarke 2006, Wiggins & McTighe 2005) are real and resolvable.
- Primary empirical sources are cited for specific claims about cognitive offloading [29, 68, 45, 6], assessment integrity [23, 26, 27, 34, 39], and social isolation [33], rather than relying on citation chains.
- Foundational work is appropriately cited: Lau & Guo (2023) on instructor adaptation, Luo (2024, 2025) on trust and transparency, Vygotsky (1978) on social learning theory, and Wiggins & McTighe (2005) on backward design.
- Recent citations (2023–2026) dominate the reference list, reflecting a fast-moving field; 43 of 79 references are from 2024 or later.
- Self-citation is proportionate: two self-citations (Gu & Ericson 2025, Padiyath et al. 2024) among 79 total references, both on relevant topics (AI literacy, social shaping of AI adoption).
- Dual citation of Luo (2024 on assessment policy, 2025 on trust) correctly distinguishes between two separate papers by the same author.

## Weaknesses
- The manuscript could engage more explicitly with recent work on AI-integrated assessment design (e.g., 'Befriending ChatGPT and other superchatbots,' 2023) that directly addresses how to preserve assessment integrity while permitting AI use—a topic adjacent to the learning-oriented policies recommended.
- Recent frameworks on AI literacy competencies (e.g., 'AI & Data Competencies: Scaffolding holistic AI literacy in Higher Education,' 2025) exist but are not cited, though the manuscript does cite Gu & Ericson (2025) on AI literacy; a brief comparison of frameworks could strengthen Section 4.3.1.
- The manuscript cites Marlatt (1996) on harm reduction from addiction studies but does not discuss whether this framework has been previously applied to educational technology or AI use in learning contexts; a brief note on the novelty of this application would clarify the conceptual move.

## Questions
- In Section 5.2, you invoke 'harm reduction' [56] from addiction studies (Marlatt 1996). Has this framework been previously applied to AI use in education, or is this a novel application? A sentence clarifying the conceptual transfer would strengthen the argument.
- Reference [43] (Lau et al. 2026) is cited for instructor barriers to AI integration. How does this 2026 work differ from or build on Lau & Guo (2023) [44]? The distinction between the two papers could be made more explicit in the text.
- You cite [31] (Gu & Ericson 2025) on AI literacy instruction. Are there other recent AI literacy frameworks in higher education (e.g., Long & Magerko 2020 [49], or the 2025 'AI & Data Competencies' framework) that should be compared or contrasted in Section 4.3.1?
- In Section 2.1, you note that 'numerous studies attempted to gauge student AI usage and reported a wide range of results' [60, 55]. Have any meta-analyses or systematic reviews synthesized these conflicting findings since 2024?
