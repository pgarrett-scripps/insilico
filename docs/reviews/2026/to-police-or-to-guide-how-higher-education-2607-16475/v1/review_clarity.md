# Clarity & Presentation Reviewer

## Summary
This qualitative study is clearly written and well-structured overall, with transparent methods and honest reporting of limitations. The narrative flows logically from motivation through findings to discussion. However, there are several soft presentation issues that create minor friction: inconsistent terminology around 'assessment harms' versus 'assessment integrity,' some vague quantifiers that could be more precise, and occasional ambiguity in how instructor quotes map to the thematic categories. The abstract and research questions are intelligible and well-framed. Tables and figures are appropriately used and referenced. The manuscript would benefit from tightening terminology consistency and slightly more explicit signposting of how individual findings support the overarching argument, but these are refinements rather than barriers to understanding.

## Strengths
- The research questions are clearly stated upfront (RQ1 and RQ2) and remain the organizing principle throughout.
- The methods section transparently describes the reflexive thematic analysis approach, including researcher positionality and the iterative coding process, making the analysis procedure recoverable.
- Table 2 effectively summarizes participant teaching contexts with clear categories (institution type, courses, class size), and is appropriately referenced in text.
- The narrative structure follows a logical progression: problem identification (learning vs. assessment harms) → policy responses → second-order costs → alternative approaches → discussion of implications.
- The abstract is intelligible standalone and accurately previews the main claims without overselling.
- Limitations are explicitly acknowledged (US-only sample, undergraduate CS focus, self-reported data, lack of empirical validation of learning-oriented policies), demonstrating candor about scope and generalizability.
- Representative quotes are well-chosen and clearly attributed to specific interviewees (I1–I13), making the evidence traceable.

## Weaknesses
- Terminology inconsistency: the manuscript uses 'assessment harms,' 'assessment integrity,' 'assessment harm to instructors,' and 'second-order harms' somewhat interchangeably. Section 4.1.4 introduces 'assessment harm' but later sections refer to 'assessment integrity' and 'second-order costs' without always clarifying whether these are synonymous or distinct concepts. For example, the phrase 'second-order harms from assessments' (mentioned in methods as a thematic code) is never formally defined in the results.
- Vague quantifiers in key claims: phrases like 'most instructors' (section 4.2.2: 'many instructors opted to prevent the use of AI') and 'several instructors' appear frequently without precise counts. For instance, 'The practice of switching to paper exam is surprisingly common, with 9 interviewees adopting some form of it' is clear, but earlier claims like 'most instructors more readily reacted to assessment harms' lack this specificity. How many of 13 is 'most'?
- Ambiguous mapping of quotes to themes: in section 4.3.1 on 'Transparent AI Instruction,' the claim that 'a few prior studies have explored how students react to course AI policies' cites Luo (2025) and others, but it is unclear whether the subsequent instructor quotes (e.g., from I13, I11) are being presented as novel findings or as corroboration of prior work. The transition between prior research and new findings could be sharper.
- Soft inconsistency in how 'learning-oriented' policies are framed: section 4.3 presents three categories (Transparent AI Instruction, Promote Metacognition, Inspire Motivation), but the relationship between these and the earlier distinction between 'learning harms' and 'assessment harms' is implicit rather than explicit. A reader must infer that each of these three policy types addresses one or more of the learning harms identified in section 4.1.
- The phrase 'second-order costs' in section 4.2 is introduced without definition. The manuscript states 'AI policies that respond primarily to assessment harms exact second-order costs on instructor-student relationships' but does not clearly define what 'second-order' means in this context (costs beyond the primary goal of assessment integrity? unintended consequences?). This becomes clearer only through reading the subsections.
- Section 4.4 ('Whose Responsibility Is It to Learn?') introduces a new organizing principle (responsibility shifting) that, while important, is not clearly connected back to the two research questions stated in the introduction. The reader must infer that this is a meta-finding about how instructors are renegotiating roles in response to the policies discussed earlier.

## Questions
- In section 4.1.4, you distinguish 'assessment harm' from 'learning harm,' but later sections use 'assessment integrity' and 'second-order harms' to describe related concepts. Can you clarify whether these terms are synonymous or whether they capture distinct phenomena? A brief definition early in the results would help.
- When you state 'most instructors more readily reacted to assessment harms' (p. 9), how many of the 13 interviewees does 'most' represent? Similarly, for claims like 'several instructors have identified strategies' (p. 12), can you provide the count?
- In section 4.3.1, you note that 'a few prior studies have explored how students react to course AI policies' and cite Luo (2025) and others. Are the instructor quotes that follow (I13, I11, I9, I12) presented as novel findings from your study, or as corroboration of those prior studies? The transition could be clearer.
- The term 'second-order costs' appears in section 4.2 without explicit definition. Do you mean unintended consequences of assessment-oriented policies, or costs that are secondary to the primary goal of assessment integrity? A one-sentence definition at first use would clarify.
- In section 4.4, you introduce the theme 'Whose Responsibility Is It to Learn?' as a core insight. How does this theme relate to your original research questions (RQ1 and RQ2)? Is it a meta-finding about how instructors are renegotiating their roles, or a finding about the content of their policies?
- You note in the limitations that 'the findings should be interpreted as what the instructors perceive from their personal experiences, but need to be verified by future work.' Given this, how should readers interpret claims in the discussion (e.g., section 5.1) that state 'assessment-oriented policies are often deemed the only way to counter the threat to assessment integrity'? Is this a finding from your data or a claim about the field more broadly?
