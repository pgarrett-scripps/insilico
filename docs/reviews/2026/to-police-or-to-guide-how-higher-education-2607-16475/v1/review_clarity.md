# Clarity & Presentation Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript is clearly written and well-structured overall, with a coherent narrative arc from motivation through methods to findings and discussion. The research questions are explicit, the thematic analysis methodology is transparently described, and the interview data are presented with representative quotes that ground claims. However, there are several soft presentation issues—primarily inconsistent terminology, some vague quantifiers, and occasional pronoun ambiguity—that create minor friction. One hard issue exists: the abstract does not clearly state the central contribution or the distinction between learning harms and assessment harms that is central to the paper's argument. These are readily fixable and do not block understanding, but they should be addressed before publication.

## Strengths
- The research questions (RQ1 and RQ2) are stated explicitly and early, making the study's purpose clear before methods are introduced.
- The methods section transparently documents the reflexive thematic analysis process, including researcher positionality and the iterative coding procedure, allowing readers to understand how findings were constructed.
- The narrative flows smoothly from motivation (instructor-student relationships matter) through findings (two types of harms, assessment-oriented policies, learning-oriented alternatives) to discussion (implications for policy design).
- Representative quotes are abundant and well-chosen, grounding abstract claims in instructor voices (e.g., I4's 'I can't really give out course credit for that anymore' illustrates assessment harm concretely).
- Terminology is generally consistent: 'learning harms' and 'assessment harms' are used throughout to distinguish the two dimensions, and 'AI policies' is defined early and applied uniformly.
- The limitations section (5.3) is candid about scope (US only, undergraduate CS only, self-reported data without validation), which supports the authors' claim to exploratory rather than representative findings.
- Table 2 clearly presents participant contexts with institution type, courses taught, and class size, enabling readers to assess diversity of the sample.

## Weaknesses
- The abstract (lines 1–10) does not explicitly state the paper's central contribution or the distinction between learning harms and assessment harms that structures the entire analysis. A reader encountering only the abstract would not know the paper's core argument. The abstract should name these two harm types and state that most policies address assessment harms rather than learning harms.
- The term 'AI-proof' appears frequently (e.g., 'AI-proof assessments', 'AI-proofing their assessment') but is never formally defined. While context makes the meaning recoverable (preventing AI use in assessments), explicit definition at first use would improve clarity.
- Quantitative claims about policy adoption lack precision in places. For example, '9 interviewees adopting some form of it [paper exams]' is stated, but the text does not clearly indicate whether this is 9 out of 13 total (69%) or some other denominator. The phrase 'surprisingly common' (page 10) is vague; a percentage would be clearer.
- Section 4.4 uses the pronoun 'it' ambiguously in places. For instance, 'When instructors move away from policing their students' AI use, whether due to the intractability of detecting AI use or their teaching philosophy, it does not change the fact that...' — the referent of 'it' is unclear (the action of moving away? the intractability?). Restructuring would help.
- The phrase 'most instructors more readily reacted to assessment harms' (page 2, abstract) uses 'most' without quantification. The findings section shows 9 of 13 adopted paper exams, but the claim about 'most' instructors' general tendency is not precisely supported by the data presented. Clarify whether 'most' means >50% of the 13, or is a qualitative observation.
- Inconsistent reference to the same concept: instructors' shift from 'policing' to 'guiding' is framed as a role change, but the paper sometimes refers to this as a shift in responsibility and sometimes as a shift in philosophy. A single consistent framing would reduce friction.
- The term 'second-order costs' (page 10, section 4.2) is introduced without definition. While context suggests it means costs that result from the policy itself (not from AI), explicit definition at first use would clarify.
- Figure/table check: Table 1 (interview protocol) lists example questions but does not indicate how many questions were asked per section or the full protocol structure. This is a soft issue (the protocol is described in text), but the table's utility is limited.

## Questions
- The abstract states 'AI policies primarily seek to AI-proof assessments without directly addressing student learning.' Does this mean (a) all policies are assessment-focused, or (b) the majority are? The findings show 9 of 13 adopted paper exams (assessment-focused), but several instructors also adopted learning-oriented policies (transparency, metacognition, motivation). How should the abstract characterize the distribution?
- In section 4.2.1, you note that detecting AI use via 'uncovered code' can lead to 'false positives, where students who genuinely learned the content from other sources would be penalized.' Can you clarify what 'other sources' means here? Does it include legitimate learning resources, or does it refer to students who learned the concept but happened to use a feature not yet taught?
- Section 4.3.1 states that 'instructors adopting this approach would need to diligently explain to students what their expectations with AI are and why that is.' Is this a recommendation you are making, or a finding from the interviews? If a finding, which instructors did this and what was the outcome?
- The paper distinguishes 'learning harms' (cognitive offloading, illusion of competence, social isolation) from 'assessment harms' (inability to tell who learned). Are these mutually exclusive categories, or can a single policy address both? For instance, does a formative assessment address learning harm, assessment harm, or both?
- In section 4.4.2, you write that some instructors 'count on their assessments to show students who overuse AI the consequences.' Is this a learning-oriented or assessment-oriented policy? It seems to blur the distinction you've established. Clarify the categorization.
- Table 2 lists 'Recently Taught Courses' but does not indicate whether each instructor teaches all listed courses simultaneously or sequentially. Does this affect the generalizability of their AI policy accounts? For instance, did I1 design one policy for all courses or different policies per course?
- The reflexive thematic analysis section (3.3) states that 'the findings from the analysis are not meant to be representative of a broader population.' Yet the abstract and discussion make claims like 'instructors perceive two types of harms' and 'most instructors more readily reacted to assessment harms.' How do you reconcile the exploratory framing with the generalizing language in the abstract and findings?
- You note that 'not all policies are always enforced as written' (page 1), and section 4.4.1 confirms this (e.g., I13's policy is 'an empty threat'). How did you distinguish between stated policies and actual practices in your analysis? Did you code them separately, or merge them?
