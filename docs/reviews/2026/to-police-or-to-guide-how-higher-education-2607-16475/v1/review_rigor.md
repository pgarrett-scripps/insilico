# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This qualitative study of CS instructor AI policies makes carefully hedged claims overall and is generally transparent about its limitations. The core finding—that instructors adopt assessment-oriented policies despite recognizing learning harms, and that this creates second-order costs—is well-supported by the interview data. However, several load-bearing claims in the abstract and introduction use causal or directional language ('AI policies…can hinder student learning', 'AI is disrupting', 'frayed instructor-student relationships') that outrun what observational interview data can establish. The study documents instructor perceptions and stated policies, not measured outcomes or causal mechanisms. The recommendations for 'learning-oriented' policies are presented as promising but explicitly lack empirical validation. With targeted rewording to match the observational scope, this becomes a solid contribution; as written, some headline claims exceed the evidence.

## Strengths
- The study is transparent about its reflexive thematic analysis methodology and explicitly acknowledges that findings are exploratory, not representative of a broader population (Section 3.3).
- The distinction between 'learning harms' and 'assessment harms' is clearly operationalized and consistently applied throughout the analysis, allowing readers to track which instructor concerns drive which policies.
- The authors document a genuine tension: instructors recognize cognitive offloading, illusion of competence, and social isolation as learning problems, yet adopt policies (paper exams, high-stakes grading) that address assessment integrity instead. This mismatch is well-evidenced by representative quotes.
- Limitations are explicitly stated: US-only sample, undergraduate CS only, self-reported instructor observations without independent validation, no empirical evidence on effectiveness of recommended policies (Section 5.3).
- The paper avoids false certainty about causation in most of the body text, using phrases like 'instructors observe', 'instructors report', and 'could harm' appropriately in Sections 4.1–4.3.

## Weaknesses
-  **HARD: Abstract claim on policy effects.** The abstract states 'policies such as switching to paper exams can preserve assessment integrity in the short term, [but] instructors report extra burden of policing student AI use behaviors and worsening relationships with students.' The evidence for 'worsening relationships' is instructor self-report of burden and adversarial framing (e.g., I4: 'I don't want to run around behind them and try and catch them using AI'), not measured relationship quality or student-reported erosion of trust. The causal link 'policies → worsening relationships' is inferred from instructor discomfort with enforcement, not from data on relationship outcomes. This should be reworded to 'instructors report experiencing burden and express concern about adversarial dynamics' or similar.
- **HARD: Title and introduction use 'disrupting' and 'reshaping' without establishing causation.** The title claims policies are 'reshaping the relationships between instructors and students', and the introduction states 'AI is directly altering the nature of instructor-student interactions' (p. 3). The study documents instructor perceptions that AI has changed student behavior (less office hours traffic, more perfect homework + poor exams) and instructor policy responses, but does not measure instructor-student relationship quality before and after AI, nor isolate AI as the cause of observed changes. Instructors' own accounts (e.g., I5 on student solitude) are anecdotal and attributed speculatively to AI. The claim should be hedged: 'instructors perceive AI as altering interactions' or 'instructors report policy changes in response to perceived AI effects.'
- **SOFT: Causal language in policy outcome claims.** Section 4.2.2 states 'paper exams do not directly intervene on AI's learning harms' and 'giving students paper exams…restore[s] their confidence in the assessment.' The first is correct (paper exams don't teach better debugging). The second uses 'restore' as if confidence was previously high and is now recovered, but the study has no baseline. Reword to 'instructors believe paper exams restore their confidence' or 'instructors report increased confidence in assessment integrity.'
- **SOFT: Generalization of 'second-order costs' beyond the sample.** The abstract and Section 5.1 generalize that assessment-oriented policies 'exact second-order costs on instructor-student relationships' as a broad claim. The evidence is 9 of 13 instructors adopting paper exams and several expressing discomfort with policing. The study does not measure whether these policies actually harm relationships (no student data, no relationship metrics), only that instructors find them burdensome and philosophically uncomfortable. The claim should be: 'instructors report that assessment-oriented policies incur costs in terms of workload and perceived classroom dynamics.'
- **SOFT: Mechanism claims about cognitive offloading lack direct evidence.** Section 4.1.1 attributes student struggles to 'cognitive offloading' based on instructor observations (e.g., 'students who I think, honestly, cheated their way through previous courses…don't know anything'). This is consistent with the cognitive offloading hypothesis but does not isolate it as the mechanism; students could have struggled for other reasons (poor prior preparation, insufficient effort, etc.). The authors appropriately use 'instructors observe' and 'instructors believe', but the Discussion (Section 5.1) states 'AI impacts students cognitively, metacognitively, and socially' as if established, when it is inferred from instructor accounts. Reword to 'instructors attribute observed struggles to cognitive offloading and related mechanisms.'
- **SOFT: Novelty claim in introduction.** The introduction (p. 2) states 'In contrast to existing tool-oriented research on how instructors view and adopt AI, this study investigates how instructors think about their roles and responsibilities to students through their course AI policies.' This is a reasonable positioning, but the claim of contrast is not rigorously established—the authors do not systematically compare their findings to prior work on instructor roles. This is a positioning issue, not a factual error, but the claim is somewhat overstated.
- **SOFT: Recommendations lack empirical grounding.** Section 4.3 and the Discussion present 'learning-oriented' policies (transparent AI instruction, modeling good use, formative assessments, motivation-building) as 'promising' alternatives. The authors explicitly state (p. 18) 'this study only involves interviews with instructors, so it does not have the empirical evidence to suggest whether these learning-oriented AI policies are effective.' Yet the abstract and conclusion present these as actionable recommendations. The recommendations are defensible as harm-reduction approaches, but should be framed as 'exploratory suggestions warranting empirical validation' rather than as evidence-based guidance.

## Questions
- In the abstract, what specific evidence supports the claim that assessment-oriented policies lead to 'worsening relationships with students'? Is this measured relationship quality, or instructor-reported burden with enforcement?
- The title claims policies are 'reshaping the relationships between instructors and students.' Can you clarify whether the study measures relationship changes (e.g., pre/post AI, or student-reported trust) or documents instructor perceptions of AI's effects on interactions?
- Section 4.1.1 attributes student struggles to 'cognitive offloading.' What alternative explanations for the observed patterns (perfect homework, poor exams) did instructors consider, and how do you rule them out?
- In Section 5.1, you state 'AI impacts students cognitively, metacognitively, and socially' as a finding. Is this a direct result of your analysis, or a synthesis of instructor perceptions with prior literature? If the latter, please clarify the boundary.
- The study recommends learning-oriented policies but acknowledges (p. 18) lacking empirical evidence on their effectiveness. How should practitioners weigh these exploratory suggestions against the documented burden of assessment-oriented policies, given the lack of outcome data for either approach?
- You note (p. 16) that some instructors recognize students face external pressure to use AI (e.g., from employers). Does your analysis explore how institutional or curricular factors (e.g., time pressure, grading incentives) interact with AI availability to shape student behavior, or is the focus solely on instructor policy responses?
- The study is limited to US institutions and undergraduate CS. Do you expect the findings (e.g., prevalence of paper exams, instructor burden) to generalize to other countries or educational levels, or should readers treat these as context-specific?
