# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
This is a qualitative interview study using reflexive thematic analysis to understand how CS instructors design and implement AI policies. The design is appropriate for the exploratory research questions and the analysis method is well-suited to the topic. However, there are several methodological concerns that prevent a higher score. The sample is small (n=13) and recruited via purposive and snowball sampling without clear inclusion/exclusion criteria or saturation justification, raising questions about representativeness and whether data saturation was achieved. The study relies entirely on instructor self-reports of student behavior and policy effects without independent verification—instructors' perceptions of AI use, student learning harms, and policy outcomes cannot be validated against actual student data, behavioral traces, or longitudinal assessment records. The reflexive thematic analysis, while methodologically sound in principle, lacks transparency in how codes were consolidated into themes, how disagreements were resolved, and whether the final thematic structure was validated against the data. The authors acknowledge these limitations but do not adequately address the construct validity threat: instructor perceptions of 'learning harms' and 'assessment harms' are treated as facts about student learning, when they are actually instructor interpretations that may not reflect actual student outcomes. The paper makes causal and comparative claims (e.g., 'assessment-oriented policies can hinder student learning,' 'paper exams do not directly intervene on AI's learning harms') that outrun the evidence available from interviews alone.

## Strengths
- The research questions are well-motivated and address a genuine gap: prior work focuses on instructor attitudes toward AI tools, but this study examines how policies mediate instructor-student relationships, which is a novel and important angle.
- The sample spans nine institutions of different types and teaches diverse course levels (CS1 through advanced electives), providing reasonable breadth across US higher education contexts.
- The interview protocol is structured across four meaningful sections (teaching context, policy on paper, enforcement, design rationale), allowing systematic exploration of the gap between written and enacted policies.
- The authors are transparent about their positionality and acknowledge that reflexive thematic analysis does not aim for inter-rater reliability or representativeness, which is appropriate for exploratory qualitative work.
- The distinction between 'learning harms' and 'assessment harms' is analytically useful and well-illustrated with concrete instructor quotes.
- The paper honestly reports tensions and contradictions in instructor reasoning (e.g., instructors acknowledge paper exams may harm students with test anxiety but feel forced to use them), avoiding oversimplification.
- The recommendations in Section 5.2 are grounded in instructor accounts and framed as 'promising sites for future research' rather than proven interventions, which is appropriately cautious.

## Weaknesses
- Sample justification and saturation: The paper does not report whether data saturation was achieved or how the sample size of 13 was determined. With purposive and snowball sampling, saturation is a key validity criterion for qualitative research, yet it is not discussed. The authors state findings 'are not meant to be representative of a broader population' but do not explain what population the 13 instructors do represent or whether the sample is sufficient to answer the research questions.
- Lack of independent verification of instructor claims: All evidence comes from instructor self-reports. Instructors report observing 'cognitive offloading,' 'illusion of competence,' 'social isolation,' and 'scores not reflective of learning,' but these are interpretations, not independently verified facts. For example, I4 states 'I used to have a reasonable idea that if they completed those assignments, then they understood programming' but 'now I have no reasonable way to know whether they actually struggled through them, or if they just generated them with AI'—this is a perception of uncertainty, not evidence that students are actually using AI or that learning is harmed. The design does not rule out alternative explanations: instructors may be more suspicious of student work post-AI without actual changes in student behavior, or they may be attributing pre-existing performance gaps to AI.
- Construct validity of 'learning harms' and 'assessment harms': The paper treats instructor perceptions of learning harms as equivalent to actual learning outcomes. For instance, I5 observes students are 'more solitary' and 'seem more depressed,' but acknowledges 'it is difficult to directly attribute I5's observed solitude among students to GenAI usage.' The paper then treats this as evidence of a learning harm caused by AI. This is a HARD construct validity threat: the measured construct (instructor perception) does not reliably map to the claimed construct (actual student learning harm). No student learning data, grades, retention, or skill assessments are presented.
- Thematic analysis transparency: The paper describes the six-phase process but does not report how many initial codes were generated, how codes were consolidated into themes, how disagreements between the three researchers were resolved, or whether the final thematic structure was member-checked or validated against the data. The authors state they 'renamed and consolidated themes where necessary' but provide no detail on this process. This limits reproducibility and raises questions about whether the themes reflect the data or the researchers' interpretations.
- Causal claims unsupported by design: The paper makes causal claims that outrun the evidence. For example: 'assessment-oriented policies only indirectly address AI use at best, while directly pressuring students to bear the negative consequences of AI misuse' (abstract). This is a causal claim about the effects of policies on students, but the study has no data on student experiences, student behavior change, or actual policy effects—only instructor accounts of what they believe their policies do. Similarly, 'paper exams do not directly intervene on AI's learning harms' is stated as fact, but the design cannot support this: no student data on learning outcomes with or without paper exams are presented.
- Comparative claims without fair comparators: The paper compares 'assessment-oriented' vs. 'learning-oriented' policies and argues the latter are more promising, but provides no evidence that learning-oriented policies are actually more effective. The paper states 'this study only involves interviews with instructors, so it does not have the empirical evidence to suggest whether these learning-oriented AI policies are effective' (Section 5.3), yet the abstract and discussion frame learning-oriented policies as preferable. This is overclaiming relative to the design.
- Selection bias in snowball sampling: While purposive sampling is appropriate, snowball sampling (where interviewees refer others) can introduce bias toward instructors with similar views or social networks. The paper does not discuss whether this may have skewed the sample toward instructors more reflective about AI policies or more willing to discuss them.
- Lack of negative cases or disconfirming evidence: The paper does not report whether any instructors disagreed with the identified patterns (e.g., instructors who believe assessment-oriented policies are effective, or who report no learning harms from AI). The absence of discussion of negative cases or alternative perspectives raises questions about whether the analysis is balanced.

## Questions
- How was the sample size of 13 determined? Was data saturation assessed, and if so, how? What is the justification for stopping at 13 interviews?
- What were the explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria for recruiting instructors? The paper states 'purposive sampling' but does not specify what characteristics were targeted.
- Did the three researchers conduct independent coding of all transcripts, or did they divide the transcripts? If divided, how was consistency ensured across coders?
- How many initial codes were generated, and how were they consolidated into the final themes? Were there disagreements between researchers, and if so, how were they resolved?
- Was the final thematic structure validated against the data (e.g., via member checking with a subset of interviewees, or by re-coding a sample of transcripts to verify the themes)?
- The paper reports instructor perceptions of student behavior (e.g., 'students are shortcutting their learning,' 'students have an illusion of competence'). Did you collect any independent data on actual student behavior, learning outcomes, or AI use (e.g., surveys, log data, grade comparisons, or interviews with students) to validate these perceptions?
- For the claim that 'assessment-oriented policies can hinder student learning' (abstract), what evidence from the study supports this? The design provides instructor concerns about paper exams and test anxiety, but no data on whether student learning actually worsens under these policies.
- The paper states that learning-oriented policies 'could guide students toward healthier AI usage' (abstract) and are 'promising sites for future research' (Section 5.2). Can you clarify whether you are claiming these policies are effective, or only that they warrant investigation? The abstract and introduction frame them as preferable, but the limitations section notes lack of empirical evidence.
- Were there any instructors in the sample who disagreed with the identified patterns (e.g., who reported no learning harms, or who found assessment-oriented policies effective)? If so, how are they represented in the analysis?
- The paper distinguishes between policies 'on paper' and policies 'in practice,' which is valuable. However, the evidence for enforcement comes only from instructor accounts of what they do (e.g., 'I check for uncovered code features'). Did you observe any enforcement, review student submissions, or interview students about their experiences with enforcement?
