# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
This is a qualitative interview study investigating how CS instructors design and implement AI policies in their courses. The methodology is appropriate for the research questions and the reflexive thematic analysis is competently executed with transparent documentation of the analytical process. However, the design has notable limitations that constrain the strength of causal and comparative claims. The study relies entirely on instructor self-report without independent verification of policy effects, student experiences, or actual learning outcomes. The sample is small (n=13) and geographically limited (US only), which the authors acknowledge but which limits generalizability. Most critically, the study cannot support causal claims about whether assessment-oriented policies actually harm learning or whether learning-oriented policies improve it—these are presented as findings but rest on instructor perception alone. The work makes a solid contribution to understanding instructor reasoning and experiences, but overclaims on causality and effectiveness of policy approaches.

## Strengths
- The reflexive thematic analysis methodology is well-documented, with explicit acknowledgment of researcher positionality and active role in interpretation, following Braun & Clarke's framework transparently rather than obscuring subjectivity with false inter-rater reliability metrics.
- The interview protocol is thoughtfully structured across four sections (teaching context, policy on paper, enforcement, design rationale) that directly address the research questions about both stated and enacted policies.
- The study captures an important distinction between learning harms and assessment harms that instructors perceive, which is a genuine conceptual contribution to understanding instructor decision-making.
- Diverse institutional contexts are represented (9 institutions, mix of public/private, research/non-research, small/medium/large classes), which strengthens the scope within the US context.
- The authors explicitly acknowledge limitations of self-reported data and the exploratory rather than representative nature of findings, appropriately hedging claims.

## Weaknesses
- The study makes causal and comparative claims about policy effects that the design cannot support. For example, the conclusion that 'assessment-oriented policies can be stress-inducing, less effective at evaluating learning' (p.17) and that they 'hinder student learning' (p.3) rests entirely on instructor perception without independent measurement of student stress, learning outcomes, or actual policy effectiveness. This is a HARD design limitation: there is no control group, no measurement of actual student learning, no comparison of outcomes between classrooms with different policies.
- The claim that paper exams 'do not necessarily help students learn better—it might actually cause some students to perform worse' (p.10) is supported only by I5's statement about test anxiety, not by any systematic comparison or measurement. The design cannot distinguish between instructor speculation and actual effects.
- No independent verification of whether stated policies match enacted policies. While the protocol asks about enforcement, the answers are instructor self-reports. The manuscript notes 'not all policies are always enforced as written' (p.1) but provides no systematic method to verify actual enforcement or its consistency.
- The study cannot support the recommendation that 'learning-oriented AI policies' are more effective than assessment-oriented ones. Section 4.3 presents these approaches as promising, but the authors acknowledge 'this study only involves interviews with instructors, so it does not have the empirical evidence to suggest whether these learning-oriented AI policies are effective' (p.18). Yet the abstract and discussion frame these as recommendations, which overstates what the design supports.
- No student perspective or outcome data. The study examines how instructors perceive student learning harms, social isolation, and illusion of competence, but has no independent measure of whether students actually experience these effects or how they perceive policies. Instructor observations of 'less office hours traffic' (p.8) could reflect multiple causes (student scheduling, pandemic effects, etc.) not controlled for.
- The sample size (n=13) and recruitment method (purposive + snowball) introduce selection bias. Instructors who volunteer to discuss their AI policies may differ systematically from those who do not. No information is provided on recruitment response rate or characteristics of non-respondents.
- Temporal confound: the study was conducted at a single time point (interviews conducted remotely over Zoom, no date specified in methods, though references suggest 2025-2026). Instructors' policies and perceptions may have changed since implementation. No baseline or longitudinal comparison exists.
- The distinction between learning harms and assessment harms, while conceptually useful, is not operationalized in a way that allows systematic comparison. Some policies are classified as 'assessment-oriented' but may have learning effects; the design does not isolate which policies actually address which harms.

## Questions
- Can the authors clarify what specific evidence would be needed to support the causal claims about policy effects? For instance, the statement that assessment-oriented policies 'hinder student learning' (p.3)—what would constitute evidence of this beyond instructor perception?
- The study notes that 'not all policies are always enforced as written' (p.1). Did the interview protocol include questions to verify the gap between stated and enacted policies? If so, how systematic was this verification, and can you provide examples of discrepancies found?
- For the claim that paper exams 'do not reliably measure student learning without having the curriculum designed around it' (p.10), is this based on instructor statements or on any measurement of exam validity? The evidence presented appears to be I1's comment about lack of time for backward design, not a validity analysis.
- The study identifies social isolation as a learning harm (section 4.1.3), citing reduced office hours attendance. What alternative explanations for reduced office hours traffic were considered (e.g., pandemic effects, shift to online resources, student scheduling)? How does the timing of policy implementation relate to these observations?
- Section 4.3 presents learning-oriented policies as alternatives, but the design does not compare outcomes between instructors using these approaches versus assessment-oriented approaches. Can you clarify whether the study is presenting these as instructor innovations observed in the sample, or as evidence-based recommendations?
- The authors state they 'opted to take extra care in protecting the participants' identities and not report their demographic information' (p.4). Does this mean instructor demographics (e.g., career stage, prior experience with AI, disciplinary background) were not collected, or simply not reported? If not collected, does this limit understanding of whether policy choices vary by instructor characteristics?
- How were the three researchers' differing levels of AI skill and trust (noted on p.6) managed during analysis to avoid bias in interpreting instructor statements about AI harms? Were codes and themes reviewed independently before discussion, or only discussed collaboratively?
