## Concessions
- **rigor** — Abstract/title use causal language (e.g., 'worsening relationships', AI 'harm[s] student learning') that outruns instructor-perception evidence.
  - Proposed change: Revise the title, abstract, and Section 4/5 topic sentences to use perception-attributing language throughout (e.g., 'instructors report perceiving worsening relationships' instead of 'worsening relationships'; 'instructors attribute learning harms to AI use' instead of declarative causal claims). We will audit every causal verb in the abstract and Discussion against this standard in the revised draft.
- **rigor** — Vague quantifiers ('most instructors', 'several instructors') are used without counts, most notably the claim that 'most instructors more readily reacted to assessment harms.'
  - Proposed change: Add a table (or inline counts) reporting, for each of the 13 interviewees, whether they adopted primarily assessment-oriented policies, primarily learning-oriented policies, or a mix, and replace all bare vague quantifiers in the Analysis section with explicit counts (e.g., '9 of 13 adopted some form of proctored/paper exam' is already reported; we will do this consistently for other claims like the illusion-of-competence and social-isolation themes).
- **rigor** — Learning-oriented policies are recommended in the abstract/title despite Section 5.3 admitting no empirical evidence of their effectiveness.
  - Proposed change: Reframe Section 5.2 and the abstract's closing sentence to present the four learning-oriented approaches explicitly as 'untested but theoretically motivated hypotheses for future research' rather than 'recommendations,' and add a sentence in the abstract flagging this evidentiary status up front rather than only in the limitations section.
- **clarity** — Terminology is inconsistent across 'assessment harms,' 'assessment integrity,' and 'second-order costs,' none of which are formally defined at first use.
  - Proposed change: Add a one-paragraph definitional box or footnote at the start of Section 4.1.4 defining 'learning harm' vs. 'assessment harm,' and define 'second-order costs' explicitly at its first use in Section 4.2 as 'costs to instructor labor and instructor-student relationships that arise as side effects of assessment-oriented policies, distinct from the assessment-integrity problem those policies were designed to solve.'
- **ethics** — No funding sources or competing-interest statement are disclosed.
  - Proposed change: Add an explicit Funding and Competing Interests statement in the final version, disclosing any grants/institutional support and stating that no author has financial ties to AI tool vendors or ed-tech platforms (or disclosing any that exist).
- **reproducibility** — No codebook, code-to-quote mapping, or documentation of the disagreement-resolution process among the three coders is provided.
  - Proposed change: Deposit a supplementary codebook (initial codes, consolidated thematic codes, and definitions) and a brief audit-trail memo describing how the three researchers reconciled disagreements during coding meetings, as a supplementary file linked from the paper.
- **methodology** — The paper does not discuss whether any interviewees offered disconfirming views (e.g., instructors who saw no learning harms or found assessment-oriented policies effective).
  - Proposed change: Add a short subsection or paragraph explicitly reporting negative/disconfirming cases from the dataset (e.g., I8/I10's motivation-centered framing is already partially reported; we will make this contrast more explicit and note any instructors who were satisfied with purely assessment-oriented approaches).

## Disagreements
- **reproducibility** — Reviewer calls for depositing full interview transcripts and inter-rater reliability metrics to allow independent verification of the analysis.
  - Response: Full transcript deposition is not feasible given the small, identifiable population of US CS instructors and our IRB-reviewed commitment to protecting participant anonymity; even de-identified transcripts risk re-identification given the specificity of institutional and course details discussed. We will deposit the codebook and audit-trail memo (see concessions) as the appropriate transparency mechanism for this genre of study. Similarly, inter-rater reliability metrics are inconsistent with the reflexive thematic analysis methodology we explicitly adopted, which treats coding as an interpretive act rather than a measurement to be reliability-tested.
  - Manuscript reference: Section 3.3: 'reflexive thematic analysis is an interpretivist method that acknowledges how the meaning of interviewees' words is constructed by the researchers... researchers should recognize their active and reflexive role in analyzing data, instead of obscuring their subjectivity with more positivist metrics, such as computing inter-rater reliability [15]... we will document our analysis process in detail in lieu of reporting inter-rater reliability.'
- **methodology** — The study treats instructor perceptions of 'learning harms' as equivalent to actual student learning outcomes, which is a hard construct-validity threat given the absence of student data.
  - Response: We agree instructor perception is not the same as verified student outcome, and we do not claim otherwise as a matter of design: the study's explicit object of inquiry is instructor beliefs and the policies those beliefs generate, not ground-truth learning outcomes. We already flag this scope limitation and will strengthen the causal-language hedging (see concessions), but the underlying design—studying instructor perception as the mediator of policy—is appropriate to RQ1 and RQ2 as stated, not a flaw to be corrected by adding student data this cycle.
  - Manuscript reference: Section 5.3 (Limitations): 'The findings should therefore be interpreted as what the instructors perceive from their personal experiences, but need to be verified by future work,' and Introduction: 'this study investigates how instructors think about their roles and responsibilities to students through their course AI policies.'
- **methodology** — No discussion of data saturation or justification for stopping at 13 interviews.
  - Response: Saturation as a stopping criterion is itself a contested, largely positivist concept within reflexive thematic analysis, which we explicitly adopted per Braun & Clarke's guidance that theme development is a generative, researcher-driven process rather than an exhaustive discovery of a fixed truth 'out there' in the data. Thirteen interviews across nine institutions and course levels (CS1 through advanced electives) provided the diversity of teaching contexts necessary to answer RQ1/RQ2, which does not require or claim statistical saturation.
  - Manuscript reference: Section 3.3: '...reflexive thematic analysis is an interpretivist method that acknowledges how the meaning of interviewees' words is constructed by the researchers, instead of objectively representing the interviewees' interior thoughts and can "emerge" for researchers to find... the findings from the analysis are not meant to be representative of a broader population.'
- **novelty** — The core findings are largely confirmatory of Lau & Guo (2023) and Luo (2024, 2025) rather than novel.
  - Response: Lau & Guo (2023) interviewed instructors in early 2023 about anticipated, speculative adaptations to AI before most instructors had multi-semester experience with enacted policies; Luo's work analyzes student perceptions of policy transparency from written syllabi. Our contribution is the empirical, retrospective account of enacted (not merely planned) policy and its enforcement three years into widespread GenAI use, including previously undocumented patterns such as the 'honeypot' detection strategy, the responsibility-shifting rhetoric, and the explicit learning-harm/assessment-harm distinction as a taxonomy organizing why particular policies were chosen.
  - Manuscript reference: Section 2.2: 'These studies' interview topics are speculative and focus on instructor reactions to the technology of GenAI itself... these studies leave gaps in understanding how instructors are managing and reacting to student AI usage since 2022, and how the instructor-student relationship has been affected by AI.'
