# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript's central claim to provide a "practical and empirically grounded framework" is undermined by a critical overreach: the evidence presented does not support the claim that LATTICE successfully integrates all five modalities. The key results show that adding chromatin/histone features (M4-M5) *reduces* agreement with the transcriptomic reference and the authors offer no positive evidence that these modalities were meaningfully integrated, only that they changed the embedding. The core contribution is therefore unsupported as stated. The work is methodologically sound for RNA-based integration (M1-M3) but the headline multimodal claim is not licensed by the data.

## Strengths
- The study design using a clear "modality ladder" (M1-M5) provides a structured, quantitative way to assess the contribution of each data source.
- The evaluation includes a bespoke "multimodal utility score" (MUS) that attempts to capture aspects beyond simple RNA-reference agreement, a thoughtful addition.
- The manuscript is generally clear about its limitations, such as the patient-specific nature of treatment shifts and the trade-off between RNA agreement and spatial contiguity.

## Weaknesses
- LOAD-BEARING: Unsupported claim of full multimodal integration. The title and abstract claim LATTICE learns from "harmonized multimodal features" and integrates five modalities. The key result in Table 2 and Figure 2 shows that adding spatial ATAC and CUT&Tag (M4, M5) *decreases* ARI/NMI versus the RNA reference. The authors argue this is because embeddings "captured chromatin and histone structure beyond transcriptomic similarity," but provide no positive evidence that this captured structure is biologically meaningful or correct. The alternative explanation—that the model failed to align these noisy or incompatible epigenetic modalities with the transcriptional signal, simply degrading the RNA-informed embedding—is not ruled out. The increased MUS score for M4/M5 is driven largely by spatial contiguity, which could be achieved by the model ignoring the epigenetic features and smoothing based on coordinates alone. To support the integration claim, the authors need evidence that the M4/M5 embeddings contain *specific, validated* information from the epigenetic assays that is not present in the M2/M3 embeddings.
- LOAD-BEARING: Overstated conclusion from ablation study. The claim that "removing spatial regularization lowers spatial contiguity most, supporting the spatial loss" (Section 4.4) is an overinterpretation. Table 3 shows that without spatial regularization, contiguity drops from 0.850 to 0.783, but the model *with* masking but *without* spatial reg still has a contiguity of 0.849. This suggests the graph structure itself (via the GNN) provides most of the spatial smoothing, and the explicit `L_spatial` term adds little. The conclusion that the spatial loss is "supported" is therefore soft; a more accurate statement is that the graph convolutional framework enforces spatial coherence, with a marginal possible contribution from the explicit regularization term.
- SOFT: The claim that LATTICE "demonstrated stable optimization behavior" (Abstract) is imprecise. The manuscript reports that runs completed without failure, which is operational stability, not optimization stability in the machine learning sense (e.g., low variance across random seeds). The reported reproducibility across "analysis seeds" refers to clustering seeds, not training seeds.
- SOFT: The description of the MUS metric is incomplete in the main text. It is defined as an average of four normalized quantities, but the normalization (min-max across all methods) means its absolute value is cohort- and benchmark-dependent, not an intrinsic property of the embedding. This should be stated where MUS is first presented to avoid misinterpretation.
- HARD: Contradiction in robustness claim. Section 4.4 states "Robustness therefore means operational stability, not guaranteed improvement." This directly follows the observation that withholding chromatin channels at test time yields "mixed" metric changes. However, the Abstract claims LATTICE provides "complete multimodal integration across all samples." "Complete integration" implies the modalities are functionally used; "operational stability" in the face of their removal suggests they might be functionally optional. These statements conflict.
- Sweep: The claim that "LATTICE M1 underperforms RNA-specialized baselines because the architecture... is designed for multimodal representation learning" (Section 4.2) is an untested post-hoc explanation. The design difference is a plausible reason, but the performance deficit could also be due to suboptimal hyperparameters for the RNA-only case.
- Sweep: The statement that adding scMultiome RNA "substantially improved concordance with Space Ranger clusters" (Abstract) uses "substantially" informally. A +0.157 ARI increase is a clear quantitative improvement, but the term "substantially" should be tied to the scale of the metric or field context.

## Questions
- For the M4 and M5 models, can you provide direct evidence that the learned embeddings specifically encode the spatial ATAC or CUT&Tag signal? For example, can you predict held-out epigenetic features from the M5 embedding significantly better than from the M2 embedding?
- You state the epigenetic modalities "need not follow RNA-defined domains." Can you show a specific, biologically plausible spatial region where the M4/M5 embedding cluster differs from the M2 cluster and correlates with a clear epigenetic signal (e.g., high ATAC in a promoter region)?
- Please clarify: when you report "reproducible embeddings across analysis seeds," are you referring to the seed for the Leiden clustering algorithm, or to seeds for model weight initialization and training? If the latter, please report the variance in key metrics (e.g., ARI) across training seeds.
