# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer — Revision Review

**Score:** 3/5 → 3/5 (confidence 4/5)

## Summary
The revision addresses several of my concerns by adding caveats and clarifications in the main text and Discussion. The abstract is more careful about the closed-loop result (acknowledging 'comparable' rather than 'complete' efficacy) and the anatomical interpretation of ΔW sites is now qualified in the main text. However, the core issues remain unresolved: (1) the abstract still frames the question as causal and presents the model's findings as general principles without stating the scope limitation upfront; (2) the paper does not provide statistical tests for key claims (dissociation between pathology and therapy sites, whether the overlap is significant, whether the two read-outs capture the same signal); (3) the transductive embedding in the main results introduces leakage, and the honest cross-validation is relegated to supplementary; (4) the causal/clinical language in the abstract is not qualified with the caveat that these are in-silico perturbations of a phenomenological model. The paper has improved in clarity and hedging, but the fundamental tension between the model-level findings and the general claims about neuromodulation strategy remains. The revision is incremental rather than transformative.

The paper does establish that the model's read-out requires distributed correction, but the framing still invites a reading that this settles whether the disease *is* distributed biologically. The Discussion (p. 15) does acknowledge 'Whether this dissociation reflects the biology of AD or the representational geometry of the model is a question our framework raises but cannot, on its own, settle', but this caveat is not in the abstract or title. The claim remains: the abstract should explicitly state that 'distributed' refers to the model's representational requirement, not a validated property of the disease itself.
- **[rigor-2] partial** — The abstract now adds 'personalised models' and emphasizes that targets are 'patient-specific', which is more careful. However, the abstract still states 'Optimal targets are cortical and heterogeneous: the site to stimulate is not where connectivity is most altered but where the network is most therapeutically responsive' without qualification that this is demonstrated only in the fitted model on this cohort. The Discussion (p. 15-16) does hedge appropriately: 'Whether this dissociation reflects the biology of AD or the representational geometry of the model is a question our framework raises but cannot, on its own, settle.' - **[rigor-3] partial** — The Discussion (p. 17) now includes a more prominent caveat: 'We emphasise, however, that these are model-level prescriptions: the reservoir is fit to infraslow BOLD connectivity, not electrophysiology, so an "intervention" here is an abstract input to the regional FC dynamics, and its optimal spatial pattern and (infraslow) frequency should be read as targets at the level of regional drive, not as cellular or oscillatory mechanisms.' This is helpful. However, the abstract and main text (e.g., 'reclassified', 'cured', 'therapeutic target', 'closed-loop controller') still use causal/clinical language without the caveat front and center. The abstract states 'achieving complete, individualised reclassification from one site' without noting this is in silico at supra-physiological amplitude. The Methods section (p. 22) clarifies 'In-silico stimulation: the model was used as a counterfactual testbed', but this is not in the abstract. The caveat in the Discussion is necessary but insufficient; the abstract should state explicitly that these are in-silico perturbations of a phenomenological model, not validated neuromodulation protocols.
- **[rigor-4] partial** — The abstract now states 'achieving complete, individualised reclassification from one site, and a real-time closed-loop controller reaches comparable efficacy at lower dose using only causally available information.' This is more careful: it acknowledges the closed-loop version reaches 'comparable' (not complete) efficacy. Figure 5D shows 97.5% (39/40) for the closed-loop case. However, the abstract still uses 'complete' for the open-loop case without noting the supra-physiological amplitude (A=6, Fig. S10B showing ~44× baseline). The leakage-free result (Fig. S9) shows ~78% net cure for the population-average site and ~100% for personalised targets, but the abstract does not distinguish transductive from honest cross-validation. The claim 'complete, individualised reclassification' is accurate only for the transductive, open-loop, personalised case at supra-physiological amplitude. The abstract should either qualify 'complete' with the amplitude caveat or report the honest cross-validation result (~78–100% depending on personalisation level).
- **[rigor-5] outstanding** — The paper still conflates 'distributed' (a property of the disease) with 'not focal' (a property of the model's representational geometry). The abstract states 'the required correction is intrinsically distributed: a coordinated, multi-site change of the model connectivity that no single-node edit reproduces.' This is accurate about the model. However, the Discussion (p. 15) proposes a mechanistic interpretation: 'A plausible mechanistic account is that pathology originating in limbic hubs propagates along structural and functional connections, distributing its imprint on connectivity across the network; the discriminative signal then resides in the network-wide pattern, so that correcting the seed regions alone is insufficient.' This is speculative and not tested. The paper does not distinguish whether the distributed correction requirement reflects (i) distributed pathology, (ii) the model's inability to capture focal pathology in a distributed read-out, or (iii) the nonlinear relationship between read-out changes and FC changes. The claim 'the disease signature is not reducible to those focal nodes' (p. 8) is not established; what is established is that the model's read-out is not reducible to those nodes. The paper should state clearly: 'Within the fitted model, the discriminative signal requires distributed correction, but this does not establish whether the underlying pathology is focal or distributed.'
- **[rigor-6] outstanding** — Figure S1 still shows the extrapolation to N=100 and N=200 with a 68% band labeled as fit uncertainty, not a confidence interval on the extrapolation. The caption states 'saturating power law fit; shaded region = extrapolation beyond current cohort' and projects AUROC ≈0.76 at N=100 and ≈0.80 at N=200. The main text (p. 7) states 'an empirical extrapolation projects ∼0.76 AUROC at N=100 and ∼0.80 at N=200 patients per group, indicating that the modest accuracy reflects sample size rather than a ceiling of the representation.' This is presented as evidence that the modest accuracy is not a ceiling. However, the extrapolation assumes the power-law model holds beyond the observed range and that the fit uncertainty band is appropriate for projection. No bootstrap or cross-validation of the extrapolation is provided. The claim should be qualified: 'If the observed power-law trend continues, extrapolation projects...' or the extrapolation should be moved to supplementary with a caveat about its uncertainty.
- **[rigor-7] partial** — The paper now states (p. 8): 'The siteswiththelargestper-patientcorrectionmagnitude∥ΔW∥wereconsistentlysubcorticalandlimbic,mostfrequentlythe bilateral pallidum and nucleus accumbens, the brainstem and the amygdala (Fig. 6A,B). These are, however, also among the most weakly inter-connected nodes of the parcellation, where a shared reservoir basis constrains the per-subject read-out least; the ranking should thus be read as indicating where the correction kernel carries its largest weights, not as an independently validated map of Alzheimer pathology.' This caveat is now in the main text, not buried. However, the anatomical claim is still stated first and prominently in Fig. 6A,B, and the Discussion (p. 16) states 'The regions whose read-out departed most from the control template were predominantly subcortical and limbic, bilateral pallidum and nucleus accumbens, brainstem and amygdala, structures of the limbic and basal-ganglia circuitry; several of these overlap with regions harbouring early neurofibrillary pathology in Braak staging, although they are also the most weakly inter-connected nodes of the parcellation, where the per-subject read-out is least constrained, so this correspondence should be treated as provisional rather than as a validated disease localiser.' The caveat is present but the anatomical interpretation is still given prominence. This is improved but still SOFT: the anatomical claim should be qualified in the figure caption or moved after the caveat.
- **[rigor-8] outstanding** — The paper still does not provide a statistical test of whether the overlap between pathology sites (35 distinct, top-5 union) and therapy sites (33 distinct, top-1 per patient) is less than expected by chance. Figure 6C shows 11 shared sites (~31% of each set) and states 'the great majority of each set is exclusive to that criterion.' However, no permutation test, hypergeometric test, or random-partition baseline is provided. If 121 sites were randomly partitioned into two sets of 35 and 33, the expected overlap would be ~9.5 sites by chance (35×33/121 ≈ 9.5). The observed 11 is only slightly higher. The claim 'pathology sites and therapeutic-stimulation sites are largely disjoint' (Fig. 6 title) is qualitative without a formal test. The paper should either (i) provide a permutation test showing the overlap is significantly less than expected by chance, or (ii) qualify the claim as 'show limited overlap' rather than 'largely disjoint.'
- **[rigor-9] outstanding** — The paper does not address whether the resonant frequency f_eig ≈ 0.077 cycles/step is subject-specific or a fixed property of the reservoir's shared dynamics. The Methods (p. 22) states 'Because J (and hence its eigenspectrum) is fixed and shared across subjects, the resonance f_eig is a property of the model, not a subject-specific fit.' This clarifies that the resonance is not fit to the data, but it does not test whether the frequency tuning (Fig. S7A) is driven by the model's intrinsic resonance or by disease-specific FC-lag signal. The Discussion (p. 17) states 'The driving frequency that most efficiently moved a target toward the control distribution coincided with the model's intrinsic resonance', but does not test whether a different model with different eigenspectrum would yield a different optimal frequency. This is a limitation that should be acknowledged: the frequency prescription is derived from the model's fixed dynamics, not from the disease data, so it may not generalize to other models or to real neuromodulation.
- **[rigor-10] outstanding** — The paper does not test whether the two read-outs (G-space and FC-lag) are capturing the same underlying signal or independent sources of weak discrimination. Figure 3 shows both reach comparable AUROC (~0.66–0.70) and plateau near K≈25, but no correlation or overlap analysis is provided. The caption (p. 7) states 'That two geometrically distinct read-outs reach a similar ceiling is itself consistent with a modest, distributed disease signal rather than a sharply localised one', but this is an interpretation, not a test. The paper should either (i) show that the two feature spaces are correlated and capture the same signal, or (ii) acknowledge that they may be independent weak classifiers that happen to plateau at similar accuracy, which would not support the claim of a distributed signal.
- **[rigor-11] outstanding** — The paper uses a transductive embedding (SVD across all patients) for classification in Figs. 3–5, then applies leave-one-patient-out cross-validation. The Methods (p. 21) states 'The SVD embeddings were built transductively (across all patients) while the classifiers were trained and tested on disjoint folds.' This introduces leakage: the embedding is built on all patients, including the test set. The honest cross-validation (Fig. S9) rebuilds the embedding, discriminant, and threshold from training patients only. Figure S9 shows the leakage-free result (~78% net cure for population-average site, ~100% for personalised targets) differs from the transductive result (Figs. 3–5). The main results (Figs. 3–5) should either use the honest cross-validation or explicitly state that the transductive embedding introduces leakage and that Fig. S9 provides the honest estimate. Currently, the transductive result is presented as the primary finding, and the honest cross-validation is supplementary.
- **[rigor-12] outstanding** — The paper does not provide a formal test of whether the observed dissociation between pathology sites (ΔW-ranked) and therapy sites (LDA-resonant) is statistically significant. The Discussion (p. 15) states 'the dissociation between the two site sets (Fig. 6) is therefore expected rather than paradoxical: a distributed kernel correction and a focal resonant drive are different interventions, and there is no reason their leading sites should coincide.' This is a post-hoc explanation, not a test. The paper should either (i) provide a permutation test showing the dissociation is significant, or (ii) acknowledge that the dissociation could arise by chance given the different selection criteria and the modest sample size (40 patients, 121 sites).
- **[rigor-13] outstanding** — The abstract claims 'a real-time closed-loop controller reaches comparable efficacy at lower dose using only causally available information.' However, 'causally available' here means information available in the model's information flow (the model's own past output), not causal efficacy in the brain. The Methods (p. 22) clarifies 'an online controller reclassifies 39/40 (97.5%) of AD patients, nearly matching the 100% reached by a fixed open-loop drive, while cutting the mean stimulation amplitude by ∼44%', but the abstract does not note that this is still at supra-physiological amplitude (A=3.3, Fig. 5D) and that the 44% reduction is relative to the open-loop amplitude (A=6), not to baseline. The claim 'lower dose' is accurate relative to open-loop but misleading without the baseline context. The abstract should state 'lower dose than open-loop' or provide the absolute amplitude.
- **[rigor-14] outstanding** — The paper does not test whether the therapeutic effect of the LDA-resonant site is specific to the FC-lag discriminant or would generalize to other disease markers (e.g., structural atrophy, cognitive scores, biomarkers). The Methods (p. 20) states 'the site whose resonant drive most efficiently shifts the FC-lag score toward the control distribution', but does not test whether this site would be optimal for other outcomes. The Discussion (p. 15) notes 'the optimal target is not the damaged node but the network node most effectively coupled to the symptom circuit', but does not test whether the LDA-resonant site is coupled to other symptoms. This is a limitation that should be acknowledged: the therapeutic target is defined relative to the FC-lag discriminant, and may not generalize to other disease markers or outcomes.

## Strengths
- The paper presents a novel and rigorous framework for fitting subject-specific reservoir models to resting-state fMRI and using them as counterfactual testbeds for in-silico stimulation. The methodology is sound and well-documented.
- The key finding—that the site of maximal pathological deviation (ΔW-ranked) differs from the site of maximal therapeutic effect (LDA-resonant)—is interesting and well-demonstrated within the model. The dissociation is large and consistent across classifiers and protocols.
- The paper is honest about limitations: the modest classification accuracy, the supra-physiological amplitudes required, the phenomenological nature of the reservoir, and the ambiguity between model geometry and disease biology. The Discussion appropriately hedges the biological interpretation.
- The closed-loop control analysis (Fig. 5) is a nice addition, showing that online titration can reduce the required amplitude by ~44% while maintaining efficacy. This is methodologically sound and clinically relevant.
- The supplementary analyses are thorough: leakage-free cross-validation (Fig. S9), physiological diagnostics (Fig. S10), frequency tuning (Fig. S7), and perturbation cost analysis (Fig. S5). These strengthen confidence in the main findings.
