# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript makes a bold and potentially important causal claim about the dissociation between pathology sites and therapeutic targets in Alzheimer's disease, derived from a model-based in-silico testbed. However, the central load-bearing claim that "optimal stimulation sites are not the most affected" is not established by the evidence presented. The analysis confuses a theoretical, distributed kernel correction with a physically realisable focal drive, comparing apples to oranges. The conclusion that a single-site resonant drive can "revert" the disease classification is based on supra-physiological, model-internal perturbations whose translation to real intervention is speculative and overstated. The abstract and title promise a causal, therapeutic insight that the results, as presented, do not support.

## Strengths
- The development of an identifiable, subject-specific reservoir model that reconstructs individual functional connectivity is a technically sound and valuable methodological contribution.
- The attempt to use a generative model as a counterfactual testbed for network interventions is a commendable and forward-looking approach.
- The analysis rigorously demonstrates that the AD functional connectivity signature within the model is distributed and not correctable by focal read-out edits, a clear and well-supported finding.

## Weaknesses
- HARD: The core claim of dissociation is based on a category error. The manuscript's headline finding is that sites of maximal read-out deviation (pathology) are not the sites where a resonant drive is most therapeutically effective. However, the evidence for the pathology side is a *theoretical read-out interpolation* ($\Delta W$ correction), while the evidence for the therapy side is a *physical resonant drive*. These are fundamentally different interventions acting on different parts of the model (the read-out weights vs. the input drive). The failure of a single-column $\Delta W$ correction (Fig. 5A, "theoretical W") does not demonstrate that the "most affected sites" are poor *stimulation* targets; it only shows that rewriting a single read-out column is inert. The appropriate comparison would be a resonant drive at the $\Delta W$ site versus a resonant drive at the LDA-resonant site. The manuscript does this (Fig. 5A, purple vs. green curves) and shows the LDA-resonant site is more effective, but this is a comparison of two *stimulation* strategies, not a demonstration that pathology and therapy sites dissociate. The claim as framed is unsupported by the presented evidence. The title and abstract need significant revision to reflect what was actually tested: that among focal stimulation strategies, targeting a discriminant-aligned site is more effective than targeting a pathology-deviation site, not that pathology and therapy sites are inherently different categories.
- HARD: The therapeutic "reversion" is achieved under conditions that invalidate its clinical interpretation. The manuscript repeatedly states that personalised resonant stimulation "reverts" the classification or is "curative" (e.g., Abstract: "achieves complete, individualised reclassification"; Results: "reverts essentially all of them"). This language implies a restoration to health. However, the results show this reclassification requires "supra-physiological amplitudes" (Fig. 4 legend, Results) that induce a massive, network-wide perturbation, making the simulated FC distance from the original ~0.7 (Fig. 5B,C, S5). The authors acknowledge this is a "large departure from the unstimulated simulation" and that the drive "dominates the network-wide mode." This is not a subtle therapeutic modulation; it is an overwhelming of the system's intrinsic dynamics with an external signal. Claiming this as "reversion" or "cure" is a significant overclaim. The evidence supports only that a sufficiently strong, resonant input at a specific site can overwrite the model's output to cross a statistical decision boundary, a mechanistic insight that is valuable but must be described with precise, scaled language (e.g., "can overwrite the FC-lag signature at high amplitude").
- SOFT: Causal language in the abstract and introduction is too strong for an in-silico, model-bound finding. The abstract states the study "establish[es] in silico that the disease signature is correctable" and that the required correction is "intrinsically distributed." The use of "correctable" implies a real-world therapeutic possibility, while the finding is strictly a property of the fitted reservoir model. The model is a phenomenological fit, not a validated biophysical analogue. The claim should be heavily hedged: e.g., "within our model, the disease signature can be shifted toward a control-like state by distributed parameter changes."
- The claim that "the site to stimulate is not where connectivity is most altered but where the network is most therapeutically responsive" (Abstract) is circular within the model's framework: "therapeutically responsive" is defined *as* the site that moves the discriminant. This is a useful operational definition for model-based targeting, but presenting it as a general neurological principle overreaches the evidence.
- The interpretation of the $\Delta W$ pathology sites (subcortical/limbic) is appropriately cautious in the text but risks being taken as a substantive localization finding by readers, despite the authors' note that these are also the most weakly constrained nodes.
- The closed-loop control result (Fig. 5D-F) is presented as reducing "dose" and "departure," but the "dose" reduction is from an arbitrarily chosen open-loop amplitude (A=6). The finding that a feedback controller can achieve a similar effect with lower amplitude is interesting but does not substantively address the supra-physiological amplitude concern.
- The leave-one-out validation (Fig. S9) correctly addresses classifier leakage but does not validate the therapeutic model itself; the "net cure" is still a model-internal manipulation.

## Questions
- For the core dissociation claim, please provide a direct comparison of the efficacy of a *resonant drive* at the top $\Delta W$ site versus the top LDA-resonant site, framed as the primary result, and revise the title and abstract accordingly to avoid the category error.
- Please temper all language regarding "reversion," "cure," "therapeutic," and "correctable" to accurately reflect that the intervention is a high-amplitude, model-internal overwrite of a classifier score. Specifically, quantify what fraction of the effect is due to simply adding a large-amplitude oscillation versus specifically reshaping dynamics, perhaps by comparing the resonant drive to a broadband noise injection of equivalent power.
- In the abstract and discussion, please explicitly state that the "optimal stimulation" parameters (site, frequency) are prescriptions derived from this specific reservoir model and that their translation to physical intervention is hypothetical, requiring validation in biophysical models or experiments.
