# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript makes a genuine and novel contribution by demonstrating that optimal stimulation targets for Alzheimer's disease differ from the most pathologically affected sites, using personalized reservoir-computing models of functional connectivity. Searches for related work (queries: "reservoir computing personalized brain models Alzheimer's disease functional connectivity stimulation targets", "personalized brain stimulation targets Alzheimer's disease reservoir model functional connectivity", "reservoir computing fMRI functional connectivity Alzheimer's disease", "echo state network brain model stimulation targeting Alzheimer's disease", "network model stimulation targeting Alzheimer's disease functional connectivity", "pathology site therapeutic target dissociation Alzheimer's disease stimulation", "closed-loop brain stimulation Alzheimer's disease functional connectivity model") found no preempting work on this specific combination of methods and findings. The contribution is appropriately framed as methodological rather than diagnostic, but could be more precisely positioned relative to the broader literature on brain network modeling for stimulation targeting.

## Strengths
- The central claim—that pathology sites and therapeutic stimulation sites dissociate—is both novel and clinically relevant, supported by systematic in-silico experiments.
- The manuscript clearly articulates its methodological contribution: using personalized reservoir models as a testbed for counterfactual stimulation experiments.
- The work acknowledges its limitations honestly, including the modest classification accuracy and the phenomenological nature of the reservoir model.

## Weaknesses
- Load-bearing claim 1: The dissociation between pathology sites and therapeutic targets is a novel finding specific to this reservoir-modeling approach. The evidence shows that targeting the most-deviant read-out sites fails while discriminant-aligned sites succeed, but an alternative explanation is that this dissociation is an artifact of the reservoir's representational geometry rather than a biological truth about AD. The most-deviant sites are subcortical/limbic hubs where the per-subject read-out is least constrained by connectivity (as the authors note), so their ranking may reflect model uncertainty rather than pathology concentration. To distinguish, the authors should show that the same dissociation emerges in a different modeling framework or validate against independent markers of regional pathology (e.g., amyloid/tau PET).
- Load-bearing claim 2: The reservoir-computing approach provides a novel, tractable route to personalized stimulation targeting. While searches found no prior work applying reservoir computing to AD stimulation targeting, the manuscript does not sufficiently differentiate its approach from the broader literature on whole-brain network models for stimulation planning (e.g., virtual brain models, connectome-based network models). The claim of novelty would be stronger with a direct comparison to established methods like dynamic causal modeling or virtual brain approaches on the same dataset.
- The manuscript makes no explicit priority claims ("first", "novel", etc.), which avoids HARD issues but leaves the contribution's positioning somewhat vague.
- The significance claim that "the model prescribes not only where but how to stimulate" is somewhat overstated, as the infraslow frequency prescription (≈0.026 Hz) is not directly translatable to any current neuromodulation technology.
- The personalization aspect is genuine but could be more sharply contrasted with population-average approaches that dominate the literature.
- The closed-loop control demonstration adds methodological novelty but is presented as a proof-of-concept rather than a validated intervention strategy.

## Questions
- How does the reservoir model's performance compare to established whole-brain modeling approaches (e.g., dynamic causal models, virtual brain) on the same classification and stimulation-targeting tasks?
- Could the authors validate the dissociation between pathology and therapeutic sites using independent neuropathological data (amyloid/tau PET or post-mortem pathology) from the same or a comparable cohort?
- What specific aspects of the reservoir approach make it more suitable for this task than alternative personalized network modeling methods?
