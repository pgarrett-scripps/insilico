# Related-Work & Citations Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents an innovative computational approach to personalized neuromodulation targeting in Alzheimer's disease. However, the related work section contains critical omissions and inaccuracies that must be addressed. The manuscript fails to cite directly competing work on model-based personalized stimulation targeting and misattributes key conceptual frameworks. These issues undermine the proper contextualization of the contribution and require substantial revision before the work can be properly evaluated.

## Strengths
- The manuscript clearly articulates a novel and clinically important research question about the dissociation between pathology and therapeutic leverage.
- The use of subject-specific reservoir models as causal testbeds is methodologically sophisticated and well-suited to the research question.
- The authors demonstrate commendable transparency about the limitations of their phenomenological model and the preliminary nature of their stimulation prescriptions.

## Weaknesses
- Load-bearing: Missing foundational work on model-based personalized stimulation targeting. The manuscript's core methodological contribution—using a generative model to identify personalized, discriminant-aligned stimulation targets—directly competes with an established literature not cited. My searches confirmed the existence of this literature, including: Gu et al., "Controllability of structural brain networks" (Nature Communications, 2015) which established the network control theory framework for brain stimulation; Stiso et al., "White Matter Network Architecture Guides Direct Electrical Stimulation through Optimal State Transitions" (Cell Reports, 2019) which applied network control theory to identify personalized stimulation targets; and recent work like Luppi et al., "Connectome-Guided Personalization of Optimal tDCS Intervention Selection in Alzheimer's Disease" (eNeuro, 2026). The omission of this directly competing paradigm, which uses generative models (often virtual brain models) to predict optimal stimulation sites based on their effect on network state transitions, is a HARD weakness that inflates the perceived novelty of the methodological approach.
- Load-bearing: Inaccurate attribution of the "network-medicine principle" for stimulation targeting. The manuscript cites Fox et al. (2014) for the principle that "the optimal target is not the damaged node but the network node most effectively coupled to the symptom circuit." While Fox et al. provides an excellent review of network effects in stimulation, this specific causal formulation of targeting based on network coupling to symptom circuits is more accurately attributed to the network control theory literature that formalized these concepts (e.g., Gu et al., 2015). Citing Fox et al. for this precise claim represents a citation chain without traceable origin for the conceptual framework being invoked.
- The claim of "closed-form, analytically tractable prescription" is overstated. The manuscript states the model provides this, but identifying the LDA-resonant site requires an empirical search over all 121 nodes for each patient; only the response *given* a target site is analytically tractable via the linear transfer function.
- Incomplete coverage of reservoir computing applications in neuroscience. While citing foundational reservoir papers, the manuscript omits relevant applications of reservoir computing for modeling whole-brain dynamics and predicting stimulation outcomes, which would provide better methodological context.
- The discussion of frequency-specific neuromodulation in AD is narrow, focusing only on 40 Hz gamma stimulation while omitting work on other frequency bands (theta, alpha) for AD, as confirmed by my search revealing relevant literature including Benussi et al. (2024) on alpha tACS for dementia.
- Self-citation is moderately high (6/36 references to authors' own work), creating some insularity in the methodological framing.

## Questions
- Please address the omission of the model-based personalized stimulation targeting literature, specifically Gu et al. (2015) on network controllability and Stiso et al. (2019) on personalized targeting via network control theory. How does your "discriminant-aligned" targeting differ from the "optimal state transition" approaches in that literature?
- The attribution of the network-medicine targeting principle to Fox et al. (2014) appears to conflate a review of network effects with the formalization of a causal targeting framework. Please provide a more precise citation for this claim or revise the text to accurately reflect Fox et al.'s contribution versus later formalizations.
- Given that identifying the LDA-resonant site requires an empirical search over all nodes, could you clarify the statement about "closed-form, analytically tractable prescription"? Is the analytic tractability limited to calculating the response *given* a target site?
