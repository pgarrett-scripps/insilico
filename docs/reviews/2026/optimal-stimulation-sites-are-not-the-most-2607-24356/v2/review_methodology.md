# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript's central causal claim—that optimal stimulation sites are not the most affected—is not earned by the experimental design. The in-silico intervention tests a model's response to a perturbation, not a causal relationship in the disease. The key comparison between "pathology" and "therapy" sites is confounded by the fact that the "pathology" metric ($\Delta W$) is a model-fitting residual, not an independent measure of disease severity. The design lacks a control for the possibility that the "therapy" sites are simply those where the model's dynamics are most sensitive to any input, irrespective of therapeutic effect. While the methodological framework is ambitious and the closed-loop control is a clever application, the core conclusion is not supported by the presented evidence.

## Strengths
- The use of a subject-specific, identifiable reservoir model as a testbed for in-silico interventions is a sophisticated and promising methodological framework.
- The implementation of a real-time, closed-loop controller to titrate stimulation amplitude is a clever and rigorous application of the model.
- The manuscript honestly reports the modest diagnostic performance of the functional model and contrasts it with stronger structural markers, setting appropriate expectations.

## Weaknesses
- The design does not test the stated causal claim. The paper concludes that "optimal stimulation sites are not the most affected." This is presented as a causal, biological insight about Alzheimer's disease. However, the evidence comes entirely from manipulating a fitted model. The "most affected" sites are defined by the magnitude of the model parameter deviation ($\Delta W$), and the "optimal" sites are defined by their effect on the model's own classifier score. An alternative explanation is that $\Delta W$ is a poor proxy for true pathological burden (it may simply reflect regions where the model is poorly constrained), and that "therapy" sites are those where the model's linear dynamics are most sensitive to any resonant input. The design lacks a control that would distinguish a therapeutically meaningful site from a generically drivable one. For instance, stimulating at random sites or at sites with high nodal connectivity (strength) could produce a similar pattern of "reclassification," which would undermine the specific claim about therapeutic targeting.
- The comparison between full-W correction and focal stimulation is not a fair test of "distributed vs. focal" pathology. The full-W correction replaces *all* model parameters with the control mean, which is an extreme, non-physiological intervention. Its success does not prove the disease signature is "distributed"; it only proves that overwriting the entire model with a healthy template makes the model look healthy. The failure of focal $\Delta W$ correction could simply mean that correcting a single parameter in a high-dimensional, coupled system is insufficient, which is a property of the model's architecture, not necessarily evidence of distributed pathology in AD. A more telling design would be to iteratively correct the *most* deviant parameters until the classifier flips, to see if a small subset suffices.
- The "physiological diagnostics" (Fig. S10) show that focal corrections require "supra-physiological" amplitude, but the threshold for "physiological" is arbitrarily defined relative to the model's own baseline drive, with no link to actual biological stimulation limits.
- The selection of the "personalised LDA-resonant" site uses the held-out patient's own data (an argmax over 121 sites), creating a risk of overfitting, which the leave-one-out analysis only partially mitigates for the population-average target.
- The resonance frequency $f_{eig}$ is a property of the fixed, shared reservoir dynamics, not of individual patients, so the "personalised" aspect of the stimulation is only in the site selection, not the frequency.
- The key finding of site heterogeneity (33 distinct therapy sites) is based on only 40 AD patients, and the analysis does not test whether this heterogeneity is greater than chance or structured by an underlying variable (e.g., disease severity).
- The comparison between the "pathology" and "therapy" site sets (Fig. 6C-E) is descriptive; a statistical test of their dissimilarity is absent.
- The design uses the same FC-lag features for both defining the disease discriminant *and* for evaluating the therapeutic effect of stimulation, which is circular, though the leave-one-out analysis attempts to address leakage.

## Questions
- For the "therapy" sites, what is the correlation between a site's therapeutic efficacy (LDA-score reduction) and its nodal strength or other measures of network centrality? If high-strength hubs are generally more effective, it would suggest the result is more about network topology than a specific therapeutic alignment.
- Was the "resonant drive" tested against a non-resonant drive of the same amplitude at the same "therapy" sites? The text states it outperforms an "off-resonance" one (fig. S7), but was a simple constant (DC) input or a broadband noise input tested? This would help isolate the effect of frequency-specific entrainment from generic input amplification.
- The manuscript states the anatomy of $\Delta W$ "should be read as indicating where the correction kernel carries its largest weights, not as an independently validated map of Alzheimer pathology." Given this major caveat, on what basis can the subsequent dissociation between "pathology" and "therapy" sites be interpreted as a meaningful biological insight rather than a model artifact?
