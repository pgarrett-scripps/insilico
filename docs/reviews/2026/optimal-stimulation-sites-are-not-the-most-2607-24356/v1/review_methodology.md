# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents a methodologically ambitious study using reservoir computing to model individual resting-state fMRI connectivity and test whether Alzheimer's disease signatures are distributed or focal. The core design is sound in principle—fitting subject-specific generative models and using them as counterfactual testbeds to probe intervention targets—but several design choices introduce confounds and validity threats that weaken the causal claims. The transductive embedding of classifiers, the lack of a truly independent validation cohort, and the absence of a biophysical ground truth for the 'stimulation' prescriptions create ambiguity about whether the dissociation between pathology and therapy sites reflects genuine network properties or artifacts of the model's representational geometry. The leakage-free cross-validation (Fig. S9) partially addresses classifier leakage but does not resolve the deeper issue: the model is fit to the same data it is used to perturb, and the 'optimal' therapy sites are selected by their effect on a discriminant trained on the same cohort. These are not fatal flaws, but they limit the strength of causal inference and require careful hedging of claims.

## Strengths
- The core methodological innovation—using a subject-specific, identifiable generative model as both a biomarker and a counterfactual testbed—is well-motivated and addresses a genuine gap: observational group comparisons cannot answer 'what would happen if we corrected site X?' (Introduction, p. 2).
- The design includes multiple robustness checks: two independent read-outs (G-space and FC-lag) reach similar classification accuracy (Fig. 3A–D), two classifiers (LDA and RF) show consistent results, and the key therapy-target finding survives leave-one-AD-patient-out cross-validation (Fig. S9), reducing (though not eliminating) concerns about transductive leakage.
- The dissociation between pathology sites (subcortical/limbic, ranked by ||ΔW||) and therapy sites (cortical, selected by effect on discriminant) is large and consistent across protocols (Fig. 6, Fig. 5A), making it unlikely to be a statistical artifact.
- Physiological diagnostics (Fig. S10) confirm that focal strategies fail despite supra-physiological amplitudes, whereas distributed correction succeeds at near-baseline amplitudes, ruling out the alternative that focal strategies simply need higher dose.
- The closed-loop control experiment (Fig. 5D–F) demonstrates that the same biomarker can close a feedback loop in real time, a methodologically distinct validation that the discriminant is causally available and actionable.
- The manuscript is transparent about limitations: cross-sectional design, modest cohort size, transductive embedding, phenomenological (not biophysical) model, and the gap between in-silico amplitude and deliverable dose (Discussion, p. 18).
- Learning-curve analysis (Fig. S1) shows accuracy has not saturated, supporting the claim that modest classification performance reflects sample size rather than a ceiling of the representation.

## Weaknesses
- HARD: The classifier is trained and tested on the same cohort (transductive embedding, Methods p. 21), and the therapy-target selection criterion is defined by its effect on that same discriminant. This creates a closed loop: the sites that 'cure' the patient are those that move the patient's score toward the CC distribution as defined by the training set. The leave-one-AD-patient-out cross-validation (Fig. S9) rebuilds the discriminant per fold, but the therapy-target selection still uses a per-patient argmax over 121 sites, each scored against the train-only threshold. This is not a fully independent validation—the personalised targets are still selected post-hoc to maximize effect on the discriminant, which is a form of overfitting to the classification objective. The manuscript acknowledges this as an 'upper bound' (Methods, p. 23), but the distinction between the personalised and population-average targets in Fig. S9 is not clearly explained in the main text, leaving ambiguity about which result is the honest one.
- HARD: The model is fit to each subject's own data (Methods, Eq. 2), and the perturbation analysis (Figs. 4–5) modifies the read-out W or its input, then re-scores the same model on the same data (teacher-forced FC-lag features). This is not a true out-of-sample validation of the therapy effect. The closed-loop free-run (Fig. 5) is a step toward independence, but it still uses the same fitted model and the same subject's data. There is no held-out test set or independent cohort to verify that the therapy sites identified in one patient generalize to another, or that the dissociation between pathology and therapy sites is not an artifact of the model's fitting procedure.
- HARD: The 'stimulation' is an abstract modification of the read-out W or its input, not a literal physical protocol. The mapping between in-silico amplitude (α or A) and a deliverable dose is 'approximate' (Discussion, p. 18), and the optimal frequency (f_eig ≈ 0.026 Hz) is derived from the BOLD-based model, not from electrophysiology or any independent measure of brain dynamics. The claim that 'the model prescribes where and how to stimulate' (Abstract, p. 1; Discussion, p. 17) overstates the translational validity of the prescription. The model is phenomenological and fit to infraslow BOLD correlations; it does not capture cellular mechanisms, neuromodulatory systems, or the actual biophysics of stimulation. The authors acknowledge this ('the reservoir is fit to infraslow BOLD connectivity, not electrophysiology', Discussion, p. 18), but the framing in the abstract and introduction suggests a more direct path to intervention than the evidence supports.
- SOFT: The choice of reservoir architecture (fixed random recurrent weights, linear read-out, N=2000 units, spectral radius 0.95, τ ≈ 0, σ_in = 0.01) is not justified or ablated. Why these specific hyperparameters? How sensitive are the results to changes in N, spectral radius, or τ? The read-out regularisation σ is swept (Fig. 3C,D), but the reservoir itself is not. This is a potential confound: the dissociation between pathology and therapy sites might depend on the reservoir's representational geometry, which is fixed by these choices. A sensitivity analysis or ablation would strengthen the claim that the dissociation is a property of the network, not the model.
- SOFT: The comparison to the tangent-space SVM benchmark (Fig. 3E, AUROC 0.75±0.12) is fair, but it uses a different preprocessing (global signal regression, 557 CC + 145 AD sessions) and a different cross-validation scheme (nested 5×5 subject-grouped, train-only tangent reference) than the main analysis (40 CC + 40 AD subjects, transductive embedding). The two are not directly comparable on the same data/splits, making it hard to assess whether the reservoir-based read-outs are truly at the 'lower end' of the benchmark's spread or whether the difference is methodological.
- SOFT: The empirical lagged FC (143 CC, 40 AD; Fig. 3, text p. 6) is classified at AUROC ≈ 0.71, comparable to the model-based FC-lag (AUROC ≈ 0.70). This is presented as evidence that the model 'repackages the same signal', but the sample sizes differ (143 vs 40 CC), and the comparison is not on identical data/splits. If the empirical lagged FC is equally informative, why use the model at all for classification? The answer (stimulation acts on dynamics, not tissue loss) is valid, but the comparison should be on matched data to support the claim that the model adds no diagnostic information.
- SOFT: The selection of the 'most-affected' sites by ||ΔW|| is acknowledged as problematic (Discussion, p. 16: 'these are also among the most weakly inter-connected nodes of the parcellation, where the per-subject read-out is least constrained'). This is a confound: the sites with the largest ||ΔW|| may be those where the read-out is most poorly estimated, not those where pathology is greatest. The authors note this caveat, but it undermines the interpretation of Fig. 6A,B as a map of pathology, and raises the question: are the therapy sites (Fig. 6D) effective because they are well-connected hubs, or because they are the sites where the model's read-out is most constrained and hence most responsive to perturbation?
- SOFT: The oscillatory stimulation protocol (Methods, p. 22) uses the leading eigenmode frequency f_eig of the fixed reservoir J, which is a property of the model, not the subject. This is not subject-specific, yet the therapy-target selection (LDA-resonant) is highly personalised (33 distinct sites for 40 patients, Fig. 6D). The interaction between site and frequency is explored (Fig. S6), but it is unclear whether the personalised sites are effective because they are individually optimal or because they are cortical hubs that couple well to the dominant mode regardless of subject. A per-subject frequency optimization would test this.
- SOFT: The manuscript claims the AD signature is 'functionally irreducible' to focal sites (Introduction, p. 2; Discussion, p. 15), but the evidence is that focal sites fail to revert the classification at physiological amplitudes. This is consistent with a distributed signature, but it does not rule out the possibility that a multi-site distributed intervention (e.g., simultaneous stimulation of 5–10 sites) could be more efficient than a single site. The full-W correction (Fig. 4B,E) succeeds, but it is not a realistic intervention; the paper does not explore whether a sparse, multi-site pattern could achieve similar efficacy at lower total dose.

## Questions
- In Fig. S9, the 'personalised LDA-resonant' result (100% net cure) uses a per-patient argmax over 121 sites. Is this a true out-of-sample prediction, or is it overfitting to the held-out patient's own data? If the latter, what is the honest, fully independent estimate of the therapy effect?
- The reservoir hyperparameters (N, spectral radius, τ, σ_in) are fixed across all subjects. How sensitive are the pathology/therapy dissociation and the classification accuracy to these choices? Has an ablation or sensitivity analysis been performed?
- The 'most-affected' sites (Fig. 6A,B) are ranked by ||ΔW||, but the authors note these are weakly inter-connected nodes where the read-out is least constrained. Could the large ||ΔW|| reflect poor estimation rather than pathology? How would you distinguish these?
- The empirical lagged FC (AUROC ≈ 0.71) is as informative as the model-based FC-lag (AUROC ≈ 0.70). Why not use the empirical lagged FC directly for the stimulation analysis, rather than fitting a model? Does the model add any diagnostic or mechanistic insight beyond repackaging the same signal?
- The optimal driving frequency (f_eig ≈ 0.026 Hz) is derived from the BOLD-based model. Is there any independent evidence (e.g., from electrophysiology, animal models, or prior human studies) that this frequency is biologically relevant for AD or neuromodulation?
- The therapy-target selection criterion (LDA-resonant) is defined by its effect on the disease discriminant. Is there a risk that this criterion simply selects sites that are most effective at moving the score, regardless of whether they restore healthy connectivity or merely shift the FC-lag features toward the CC distribution? Could the 'cure' be a statistical artifact rather than a biological restoration?
- In Fig. 5, the personalised LDA-resonant site achieves 100% reclassification at supra-physiological amplitude (A ≈ 6), whereas the population-average site reaches only ≈65%. How much of this difference is due to personalisation per se, versus overfitting to the training cohort? What would the per-patient effect be under true leave-one-patient-out cross-validation of the site selection?
- The manuscript states that 'no single anatomical landmark serves all individuals' (Discussion, p. 17), yet the population-average LDA-resonant site (left Default-network prefrontal cortex, Fig. S3A) is effective for ≈65% of patients (Fig. S9B). Is this sufficient to justify a one-size-fits-all intervention, or is personalisation truly necessary?
- The closed-loop controller (Fig. 5D–F) uses a sliding-window estimate of the FC-lag biomarker. How is this window chosen (length, update frequency)? How sensitive is the closed-loop efficacy to these parameters?
- Could the dissociation between pathology sites (subcortical/limbic) and therapy sites (cortical) reflect the structure of the parcellation or the connectivity of the atlas, rather than a genuine property of the AD network? For example, if cortical hubs are more densely connected, they may be more effective at propagating perturbations, regardless of where pathology originates.
