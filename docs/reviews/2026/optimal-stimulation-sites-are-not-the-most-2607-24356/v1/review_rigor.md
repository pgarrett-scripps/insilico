# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript makes a central claim that the AD functional-connectivity signature is distributed and cannot be reduced to focal sites, supported by in-silico stimulation showing that targeting the most-deviant sites fails while discriminant-aligned targeting succeeds. The core logic is sound and the evidence for the distributed nature is strong. However, several load-bearing claims are over-stated or under-hedged relative to what the evidence actually establishes, particularly around causality, generalizability, and the interpretation of the dissociation between pathology and therapy sites. The work is methodologically sophisticated but makes claims that exceed the scope of a phenomenological model fit to a modest, cross-sectional cohort.

## Strengths
- The central empirical contrast—that ΔW-ranked (pathology) sites fail to revert classification while LDA-resonant (therapy) sites succeed—is clearly demonstrated across both read-outs and multiple protocols (Figs. 4–6), establishing a genuine dissociation.
- The authors transparently acknowledge limitations: cross-sectional design, modest sample size, transductive embedding, phenomenological rather than biophysical model, and the gap between in-silico amplitude and deliverable dose (Discussion, Methods).
- Leakage-free cross-validation (leave-one-AD-patient-out, Fig. S9) is performed and shows the therapeutic effect survives honest evaluation, strengthening claims about the reclassification.
- The closed-loop adaptive control result (Fig. 5D–F) is a genuine methodological contribution, demonstrating that a causal biomarker can titrate dose in real time using only past output.
- Physiological diagnostics (Fig. S10) honestly document that focal strategies require supra-physiological amplitudes, whereas distributed correction stays near baseline—a key negative result properly reported.

## Weaknesses
-  **HARD: Title and abstract claim causality without sufficient warrant.** The title states 'a distributed functional-connectivity signature of Alzheimer's disease' and the abstract claims 'the ideal correction that maps a patient's dynamics onto the control template is a distributed change'—but this is an observation about what the fitted model requires, not a causal discovery about the disease itself. The model is fit to cross-sectional data; it does not establish that the disease *is* distributed in the biological sense, only that the model's read-out deviations are distributed. The claim 'whether its FC signature reduces to a few focal sites has not been tested causally' (Introduction) frames the question as causal, but the answer—that the model requires distributed correction—does not settle whether the underlying disease pathology is focal or distributed; it only shows the model's representational geometry. This is a SOFT-to-HARD boundary: the framing invites a causal/biological reading that the evidence does not support.
- **HARD: Generalization beyond the fitted model.** The abstract and Results claim 'the site to stimulate is not where the read-out deviation is largest but where the network is most therapeutically responsive' as a general principle. However, this is demonstrated only within the fitted reservoir model on this cohort. Whether this dissociation holds in actual brain tissue, in other models, or in other cohorts is not tested. The claim is presented as a finding about neuromodulation strategy in general ('Optimal targets are cortical and heterogeneous: effective neuromodulation requires model-informed, personalised targeting'), but it is a finding about this model on this data. The Discussion does hedge ('Whether this dissociation reflects the biology of AD or the representational geometry of the model is a question our framework raises but cannot, on its own, settle'), but the abstract and main narrative do not, creating a scope mismatch.
- **HARD: Causal language for in-silico perturbations.** The paper uses 'stimulation', 'intervention', 'drive', and 'cure' throughout to describe read-out interpolations and input oscillations applied to a phenomenological model. These are not stimulations in the neurobiological sense; they are mathematical modifications of a fitted linear read-out or inputs to a fixed random reservoir. The closed-loop section (Fig. 5) calls the controller 'adaptive (closed-loop) neuromodulation' and states 'a causally available, minimal-dose perturbation', but causality here is causal availability in the model's information flow, not causal efficacy in the brain. The Discussion acknowledges 'the reservoir is fit to infraslow BOLD connectivity, not electrophysiology, so an "intervention" here is an abstract input to the regional FC dynamics', but the main text and abstract do not carry this caveat, and the language ('reclassified', 'cured', 'therapeutic') implies a real intervention. This is HARD because the causal framing is misleading without the caveat front and center.
- **SOFT: Claim about 'complete reclassification' under-hedged.** The abstract states 'selecting each patient's site by its effect on the disease discriminant achieves complete, individualised reclassification from one site'. Figure 5A shows 100% reclassification in the open-loop case, but this is achieved at supra-physiological amplitude (A=6, Fig. S10), and the closed-loop version reaches only 97.5% (39/40, Fig. 5D). The leakage-free version (Fig. S9) reaches ~100% for personalised targets but ~78% for the population-average site. The word 'complete' is accurate for the open-loop transductive case but not for the honest cross-validation, and the amplitude caveat is not in the abstract.
- **SOFT: 'Distributed' vs 'not focal' conflation.** The paper argues that because targeting the top-1 or top-5 most-deviant sites fails, the signature is 'distributed'. However, 'distributed' could mean (i) the pathology is spread across many regions, (ii) the discriminative signal requires many regions to read out, or (iii) the therapeutic effect requires many regions to achieve. The evidence shows (ii) and (iii) within the model, but does not distinguish whether the underlying pathology is truly distributed or whether the model's representational geometry simply requires a distributed read-out to capture a focal lesion. The Discussion hints at this ('Whether this dissociation reflects the biology of AD or the representational geometry of the model'), but the main claims treat 'distributed' as a property of the disease, not the model.
- **SOFT: Learning-curve extrapolation (Fig. S1) presented without uncertainty bounds on the projection.** The paper extrapolates AUROC to N=100 and N=200 patients per group, projecting ~0.76 and ~0.80 respectively. These are point estimates from a power-law fit; the 68% band shown is the fit uncertainty, not a confidence interval on the extrapolation. The claim 'the modest present accuracy reflects sample size rather than a ceiling of the representation' is reasonable but depends on the extrapolation being accurate, which is not guaranteed. This is SOFT because the caveat is present but the projection is given prominent placement in the main narrative.
- **SOFT: Anatomical interpretation of ΔW sites under-hedged.** The paper states 'The siteswiththelargestper-patientcorrectionmagnitude∥ΔW∥wereconsistentlysubcorticalandlimbic,mostfrequentlythe bilateral pallidum and nucleus accumbens, the brainstem and the amygdala', then notes 'these are, however, also among the most weakly inter-connected nodes of the parcellation, where a shared reservoir basis constrains the per-subject read-out least; the ranking should thus be read as indicating where the correction kernel carries its largest weights, not as an independently validated map of Alzheimer pathology.' This caveat is appropriate but buried in the text; the anatomical claim is stated first and prominently (Fig. 6A,B), creating an impression of validated pathology localization that the caveat undermines.
- **HARD: Claim that 'pathology sites and therapeutic sites are largely disjoint' (Fig. 6 title) is not tested statistically.** The paper shows 11 of 35 pathology sites and 11 of 33 therapy sites overlap (~31% each), and states 'the great majority of each set is exclusive to that criterion'. However, no statistical test of whether this overlap is less than expected by chance is provided. If the 121 sites were randomly partitioned into two sets of 35 and 33, what overlap would be expected? Without this, the claim of dissociation is qualitative. This is HARD because the headline claim (Fig. 6 title, Discussion) rests on a quantitative comparison that is not formally tested.

## Questions
- The abstract claims the signature is 'distributed' and 'not reducible' to focal sites. Can the authors clarify whether this is a claim about the disease's biological pathology, the model's representational geometry, or both? The Discussion acknowledges the ambiguity, but the abstract does not.
- In Fig. 5A, the open-loop LDA-resonant drive achieves 100% reclassification at A=6, but Fig. S10B shows this requires ~44× baseline amplitude at the driven site. How does this amplitude compare to typical non-invasive neuromodulation (e.g., tDCS, TMS) in physical units, and does the paper intend to claim feasibility or only methodological proof-of-concept?
- The leakage-free cross-validation (Fig. S9) shows the population-average LDA-resonant site achieves ~78% net cure, not 100%. Should the abstract's claim of 'complete, individualised reclassification' be qualified to reflect the honest cross-validation result, or is the transductive result the intended headline?
- The paper argues that the dissociation between pathology sites (ΔW-ranked) and therapy sites (LDA-resonant) is 'expected rather than paradoxical' because they answer different questions. Can the authors provide a formal test (e.g., permutation test, overlap-by-chance calculation) to quantify whether the observed disjointness (Fig. 6C) is statistically significant, or is the visual dissociation sufficient for the claim?
- The resonant frequency f_eig ≈ 0.077 cycles/step corresponds to ~0.026 Hz in physical time. The paper notes this is 'in the well-established infraslow band in which resting-state connectivity itself fluctuates', but does not test whether this frequency is specific to AD or is a property of the reservoir's fixed dynamics shared across all subjects. Is the resonance subject-specific or a fixed property of J?
- The paper claims the model 'prescribes not only where but how to stimulate' (Discussion), but the frequency prescription is derived from the reservoir's eigenspectrum, not from the AD data. How much of the frequency tuning (Fig. S7A) is driven by the model's intrinsic resonance versus the disease-specific FC-lag signal? Could a different model with different eigenspectrum yield a different optimal frequency?
- Figure 3 shows the two read-outs (G-space and FC-lag) reach comparable AUROC (~0.66–0.70) and plateau near K≈25. Is there evidence that these two feature spaces are capturing the same underlying signal, or could they be independent sources of weak discrimination that happen to plateau at similar accuracy?
- The paper uses a transductive embedding (SVD across all patients) for classification, then applies leave-one-patient-out cross-validation. Does the transductive embedding introduce leakage in the main results (Figs. 3–5), and should the honest cross-validation (Fig. S9) be the primary reported result rather than a supplementary check?
