# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript presents a reservoir-computing framework to model resting-state fMRI connectivity in Alzheimer's disease and uses in-silico perturbations to test whether the AD functional signature is distributed or focal. The work is methodologically sound, honestly executed, and addresses a genuine causal question. Strengths include rigorous identifiability validation, clear negative results (focal pathology targeting fails), leakage-free cross-validation, and a plausible closed-loop controller. However, novelty is mixed: reservoir computing for brain dynamics is established; AD classification accuracy (~0.66–0.70 AUROC) is modest and incremental; the dissociation between pathology and therapy sites, while concrete, reflects an application of existing network-medicine principles rather than a new conceptual contribution; and the positive result (discriminant-aligned targeting succeeds) is somewhat circular—it succeeds because it is selected to succeed on the discriminant. The manuscript is publishable and will interest computational neuroscientists, but claims of novelty should be scaled to reflect that this is primarily a careful application of existing methods to a specific question, with useful but not transformative findings. Minor revisions should address transductive embedding concerns, clarify the circular logic in discriminant-aligned targeting, and temper the framing of the dissociation as a discovery vs. an application of network medicine.

## Strengths
- Honest framing of classification performance: authors explicitly acknowledge ~0.66–0.70 AUROC is below structural atrophy (~0.84) and justify focusing on functional read-out because stimulation acts on dynamics, not tissue loss.
- Rigorous identifiability validation: Figure 2 demonstrates subject-specific models with within-subject FC reconstruction (r ≈ 0.72) far exceeding cross-subject (r ≈ 0.34), validating the model as a counterfactual testbed.
- Clear negative result on focal pathology targeting: demonstration that targeting most-affected sites (subcortical/limbic, ‖ΔW‖ ranked) fails to revert classification even at supra-physiological amplitudes (Figs. 4C,F, S10).
- Leakage-free cross-validation of therapeutic effect: Figure S9 and Methods show reclassification effect survives leave-one-AD-patient-out reconstruction of embedding, discriminant, threshold, and site selection.
- Closed-loop controller with real-time biomarker feedback: online amplitude titration achieves 97.5% reclassification while reducing mean dose by ~44% and perturbation cost by ~7%.
- Frequency prescription from reservoir eigenspectrum: efficacy sharply tuned to dominant eigenmode (f_eig ≈ 0.077 cycles/step, ~0.026 Hz) in infraslow band where resting-state connectivity fluctuates, providing testable prediction.
- Anatomical dissociation well-documented: Figure 6 clearly shows pathology sites (subcortical/limbic, 10 sites) and therapy sites (cortical, 33 sites) overlap minimally (~31–33%), with minimal shared anatomy.

## Weaknesses
- Circular logic in discriminant-aligned targeting: LDA-resonant site is selected because its resonant drive moves FC-lag score toward control distribution—it succeeds by construction on the same discriminant used for diagnosis, not because it identifies an independent property of therapeutic responsiveness.
- Transductive embedding and per-subject read-out fitting: FC-lag and G-space embeddings built transductively across all patients (including test patients) before classifiers trained on disjoint folds; while classifiers are cross-validated, feature space itself is not blind to test set, potentially inflating apparent stability or generalizability.
- Supra-physiological amplitudes required for reclassification: successful discriminant-aligned single-site resonant drive requires A ≈ 6–7 (RMS_pert/RMS_base ≈ 6–7), far beyond endogenous drive amplitude; closed-loop reduces to A ≈ 3.3 (~3× baseline), still not clearly physiological.
- Modest sample size and cross-sectional design: main cohort is 36 CC and 40 AD subjects (one session each); learning-curve analysis projects AUROC ≈0.76 at N=100 and ≈0.80 at N=200, indicating present accuracy is sample-size-limited; cross-sectional design means disease signature based on group differences, not longitudinal tracking.
- Phenomenological rather than biophysical model: reservoir is fixed random recurrent network with linear read-out, not biophysically grounded; stimulation is idealized modification, not literal physical protocol; infraslow frequency (0.026 Hz) appropriate for BOLD but may not translate to electrophysiological or optogenetic protocols.
- Limited systematic comparison to prior work on distributed vs. focal AD signatures: claim that 'whether its FC signature reduces to a few focal sites has not been tested causally' (Abstract) is strong but not fully supported by systematic literature review; prior work on Braak staging and network diffusion models of tau spread not deeply engaged.
- Heterogeneity of per-patient therapy sites not validated: 33 distinct sites selected across 40 AD patients (each site selected by ≤3 patients); heterogeneity presented as evidence for personalized targeting, but stability under perturbations (resampling, noise injection, parameter variation) not reported, leaving unclear whether this reflects genuine biological variability vs. model noise.
- Limited engagement with network-medicine mechanism: authors invoke principle that optimal target is network node most effectively coupled to symptom circuit, but do not formally test this—no comparison to coupling measures, network centrality, or betweenness; dissociation is documented but mechanistic explanation (cortical hubs propagate corrections) is plausible but not rigorously tested.
- Oscillatory stimulation results are secondary and not clearly translatable: infraslow prescription (0.026 Hz) is in different band than gamma (40 Hz) explored in recent AD neuromodulation trials; authors acknowledge 'we make no claim about a specific deliverable protocol' but do not clearly explain why infraslow should be preferred.

## Questions
- Were the G-space and FC-lag results in Figs. 3–6 recomputed under a fully honest (non-transductive) embedding, where the SVD basis is built only on the training set? If not, can you provide this analysis or clarify how much the transductive construction inflates apparent discriminability?
- How would you distinguish between (a) the LDA-resonant site being a genuine property of the network's therapeutic responsiveness, and (b) it succeeding simply because it is optimized on the discriminant? Could you propose independent validation—e.g., does the LDA-resonant site generalize to a held-out test set or to a different classifier (e.g., random forest)?
- You note that pathology sites are 'among the most weakly inter-connected nodes of the parcellation, where the per-subject read-out is least constrained' (p. 9). Could the ranking of pathology sites be an artifact of regularization or fitting procedure rather than true disease severity? How sensitive is the ranking to choice of regularization noise σ?
- Is the per-patient site selection stable under perturbations? For example, if you add small noise to FC-lag features or resample the training set, do selected sites remain the same, or does heterogeneity reflect genuine biological variability vs. model noise?
- Is the resonant frequency f_eig ≈ 0.077 cycles/step (0.026 Hz) specific to AD patients or the same for controls? If the same, does this suggest the frequency is a property of the fixed reservoir J rather than a disease-specific feature?
- You show full-W correction (all 121 sites) reverts classification while focal corrections fail. How many sites are needed to achieve reclassification? Is there a threshold (e.g., 10, 20, 50 sites) above which correction becomes effective? This would clarify whether the signature is truly distributed or merely requires a small number of coordinated sites.
- How does your finding that pathology concentrates in subcortical/limbic regions (pallidum, accumbens, brainstem, amygdala) compare to Braak staging and neuropathological maps of tau and amyloid in AD? Are identified sites consistent with early pathology or do they reflect a different aspect of disease?
