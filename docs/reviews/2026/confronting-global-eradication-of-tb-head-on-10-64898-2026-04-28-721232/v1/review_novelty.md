# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript presents a substantial data compilation of 821 drug-resistant TB mutations from 31,073 isolates across 149 studies, applying existing bioinformatics tools (ConSurf, SIFT, PolyPhen-2, I-MUTANT, mCSM, AutoDock) to propose a four-tier ranking system (lethal/moderate/mild/neutral). While the scale of curation is commendable and the multi-tool consensus approach is sound, the work suffers from a critical novelty flaw: the claim of being "the first comprehensive" study is contradicted by the authors' own citation of Portelli et al. (2018), which conducts a similar analysis without explicit differentiation provided. More fundamentally, the central claimed contribution—the ranking system—lacks experimental validation, is applied uniformly despite incomplete structural data for ~226 emb mutations, and relies on post-hoc categorization of existing tool predictions rather than novel methodology. The framing of observational mutation frequency patterns as discovery of "universal bacterial survival strategies" exceeds what the data can support without experimental evidence. The work represents a useful data resource but requires major revisions to address novelty claims, validate the ranking system, and reframe conclusions appropriately.

## Strengths
- Large-scale data compilation of 31,073 isolates across 149 publications spanning 30 years represents significant curation effort and creates a useful reference resource for TB drug resistance research.
- Multi-tool consensus approach using three independent functional prediction tools (SIFT, PROVEAN, PolyPhen-2) and two stability assessment tools (I-MUTANT, mCSM) reduces single-tool bias and strengthens confidence in predictions.
- Systematic categorization framework with four-tier ranking system (lethal/moderate/mild/neutral) provides structured integration of multiple bioinformatic predictions into actionable categories.
- Global geographic analysis stratifying mutations by WHO regions and examining regional variation in mutation patterns adds epidemiological context often missing from purely structural studies.
- Hypothesis-driven framing with explicit hypothesis that bacteria favor mutations minimizing fitness cost while maximizing drug resistance provides clear conceptual scaffolding for the analysis.

## Weaknesses
- The claim of being "the first comprehensive and systematic in-depth study" (Abstract, Discussion) is contradicted by Portelli et al. (2018)—cited as ref. [8]—which appears to conduct a similar in silico analysis of drug-resistant mutations in TB drug targets; no explicit differentiation or head-to-head comparison is provided, constituting a hard novelty flaw.
- The bioinformatics pipeline (Fig. 3b) applies standard published tools sequentially with no novel algorithm, tool, or computational method introduced; the work represents data application rather than methodological innovation, yet is framed as a novel contribution to understanding drug resistance mechanisms.
- The four-tier ranking system is defined post-hoc based on concordance of existing tool predictions with no experimental validation, no comparison to published fitness cost measurements, and no independent dataset to test predictive power; this central claimed contribution remains untested and unvalidated.
- Incomplete structural modeling for arabinosyltransferase (emb) with only C-terminal domain of embC available means ~226 embB/embA/embC mutations lack structural analysis, yet the ranking system is applied uniformly to all 821 mutations without discussing the impact on reliability for emb mutations.
- AutoDock binding energy calculations assume rigid protein and flexible ligand without discussion of appropriateness for large conformational changes; small absolute differences in reported binding energies (e.g., -4.99 to -5.2 kcal/mol for katG S315 variants) raise questions about signal-to-noise ratio and biological significance, and no validation against experimental structures or mutagenesis data is provided.
- Geographic variation explanations are speculative, attributing higher mutational burden in Western Pacific and Southeast Asia to "prolonged exposure to drugs with probable variable commitment to treatment regimen, weather conditions and poor performance of some sequence-based diagnostic testing" without supporting data, while authors acknowledge "available data were not granular enough to ascertain the probable reason."
- The conclusion that bacteria "favour less harmful mutations in the drug binding region without compromising conservancy while destabilising the drugs" is presented as a discovered universal principle but is an inference from observational data without mechanistic proof.
- Compensatory mutations are repeatedly referenced as important for fitness restoration but are not systematically analyzed; the statement "Compensatory mechanisms are an important aspect to explore in future" acknowledges this gap, leaving a key aspect of the proposed survival strategy unexamined.
- The practical utility of the "atlas" contribution over existing mutation databases (TB-Profiler, PhyResSE, TBDB) is not discussed, and what distinguishes this as an "atlas" rather than a frequency table is unexplained.
- Mutation frequency calculation methodology is unclear regarding the denominator: does "total number of resistant isolates in a drug target having that mutation" mean isolates with any mutation in that target or isolates with that specific mutation, potentially inflating frequencies?
- No statistical analysis is reported including confidence intervals, p-values, or significance tests for any comparisons; concordance analysis is qualitative ("765 mutations with destabilizing effect") rather than quantitative (e.g., Cohen's kappa, percentage agreement).
- Scope creep in claims: the manuscript begins as a study of drug resistance mechanisms but expands to claims about "bacterial survival strategies," "evolutionary pathways," and "universal strategies" that exceed what mutation frequencies and bioinformatic predictions can support without experimental validation.

## Questions
- How does your study explicitly differentiate from Portelli et al. (2018) [ref. 8], which appears to conduct a similar in silico analysis of drug-resistant mutations in TB drug targets? Please provide a detailed comparison table or discussion addressing scope, methodology, and findings.
- Have you validated your lethal/moderate/mild/neutral rankings against published experimental fitness cost measurements (e.g., from refs. [30–33])? Can you provide a validation dataset or at least a sensitivity analysis showing how ranking assignments change with different threshold choices?
- Given that ~226 embB/embA/embC mutations lack complete structural models, how confident are you in the ranking assignments for these mutations? Should they be flagged as "uncertain" in the supplementary tables?
- Have you validated AutoDock poses against crystal structures of drug-target complexes (where available, e.g., katG-INH, rpoB-RIF)? What is the RMSD of predicted versus experimental binding modes?
- In your single mutation frequency formula, does "total number of resistant isolates in a drug target having that mutation" mean isolates with any mutation in that target, or isolates with that specific mutation? Please clarify with a concrete example.
- You mention compensatory mutations (ahpC, rpoA, rpoC) as critical to fitness restoration but do not analyze them systematically. Can you provide data on their frequency in your dataset and their co-occurrence with primary resistance mutations?
- Are you aware of other concurrent or recent preprints on TB mutation analysis (e.g., 2025 preprints on in-host mutational adaptation or Gambian TB genomics)? How does your work relate to these?
- Will the mutation atlas (Supplementary Table S3) be made available as a searchable web database or integrated into existing TB mutation databases (TB-Profiler, PhyResSE, TBDB) to substantially increase utility?
