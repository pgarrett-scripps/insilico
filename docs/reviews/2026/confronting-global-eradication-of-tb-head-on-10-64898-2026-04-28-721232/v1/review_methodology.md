# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
This study claims to uncover bacterial survival strategies in TB drug resistance by analyzing 821 mutations across 31,073 isolates and ranking them into lethal/moderate/mild/neutral categories. However, the core design has a fundamental flaw: the ranking scheme conflates multiple independent dimensions (sequence conservation, functional impact, structural stability, drug binding) into a single ordinal category without a principled weighting or validation framework. The authors use computational predictions from multiple tools (SIFT, PolyPhen-2, PROVEAN, I-MUTANT, mCSM, AutoDock) but provide no evidence that consensus across these tools validates the predictions against experimental data, nor do they justify why a mutation predicted as 'deleterious' by all three tools should be ranked 'lethal' rather than 'moderate.' Most critically, the study has no experimental validation, no independent test set, and no comparison to known fitness costs measured in vitro or in vivo. The claim that 'Mtb favours mild mutations in essential targets' (Discussion) is not tested—the design shows only that frequent mutations happen to be in non-conserved regions and have moderate predicted effects, but causality and preference are not demonstrated. The geographic and prevalence analyses are descriptive and sound, but the mechanistic conclusions about bacterial strategy rest on unvalidated computational rankings.

## Strengths
- Large, systematic data collection from 149 studies covering 31,073 isolates and 821 non-synonymous mutations, with transparent inclusion/exclusion criteria and a well-documented atlas (Supplementary Table S3).
- Thoughtful use of multiple orthogonal bioinformatics tools (ConSurf, SIFT, PROVEAN, PolyPhen-2, I-MUTANT, mCSM, AutoDock) to assess different aspects of mutation impact (conservation, function, stability, binding), reducing single-tool bias.
- Clear descriptive findings on global mutation patterns: S315T in katG (60%), S450L in rpoB (56%), M306V in embB (29%), and Q10P in pncA (1.4%), with documented regional variation across WHO regions.
- Appropriate stratification of mutations by location (site-1 binding region, site-2 supporting region, site-3 other) and conservation status, revealing that frequent mutations cluster in non-conserved regions.
- Honest acknowledgment of limitations, including unavailable structural data for embC and embA, and discussion of compensatory mechanisms as an open question.

## Weaknesses
- **HARD: No validation of the ranking scheme.** The authors rank 821 mutations into four categories (lethal, moderate, mild, neutral) by combining predictions from multiple tools via 'concordance analysis' (Section 3.6.2.5), but provide no definition of how concordance is computed, no weighting of the five dimensions (conservation, function, stability, binding, site location), and no experimental or clinical validation. For example, they state 'Functionally deleterious mutations that compromise residue conservation with reduced structural stability and binding affinity were categorized lethal' but do not specify: (i) how many of the five criteria must be met, (ii) whether all are equally weighted, (iii) how a mutation can be 'deleterious' by SIFT but 'neutral' by PolyPhen-2 and still be ranked, or (iv) whether the ranking predicts actual fitness cost or drug resistance in vivo. The ranking is presented as fact (Table 2: '340 lethal, 284 moderate, 185 mild, 12 neutral') without sensitivity analysis or cross-validation.
- **HARD: No experimental validation of computational predictions.** The study relies entirely on in silico tools (MODELLER, AutoDock, etc.) to predict functional impact and binding affinity. No wet-lab experiments, clinical data, or published experimental fitness measurements are used to validate whether mutations predicted as 'mild' actually have low fitness cost, or whether those predicted as 'lethal' are actually rare in clinical isolates. The authors acknowledge that 'Analyzing the large amount of data (821 drug resistant mutations) by wet-lab experiments would be difficult, time-consuming, and expensive' but offer no alternative validation (e.g., comparison to published in vitro fitness assays, clinical outcome data, or phylogenetic inference of selection coefficients).
- **HARD: Causality claim unsupported by design.** The Discussion states: 'Mtb follows some guided evolutionary pathway to balance the impact caused by drug resistant mutations on the fitness of Mtb' and 'Mtb prefers mild mutations in targets essential for its survival.' However, the study observes only that frequent mutations happen to be mild and in non-conserved regions; it does not test whether bacteria actively select for mild mutations or whether mild mutations are simply more likely to arise and survive by chance. No phylogenetic analysis, experimental evolution, or comparison of mutation rates in conserved vs. non-conserved regions is provided to support 'preference' or 'strategy.' The design shows correlation (frequent mutations are mild) but not causation.
- **HARD: No independent test set or cross-validation.** All 821 mutations are analyzed using the same tools and ranked using the same concordance criteria. There is no held-out test set, no cross-validation, and no comparison of the ranking to an independent cohort of mutations with known fitness costs. The claim that the ranking reflects a 'universal strategy' (Abstract) cannot be tested without external validation.
- **HARD: Confounding between mutation frequency and computational prediction.** The study observes that the most frequent mutations (S315T, S450L, M306V) are predicted to have mild effects. However, this could reflect: (i) a true biological preference for mild mutations, (ii) ascertainment bias (mild mutations are more likely to be detected and reported in clinical isolates because they confer resistance without severe fitness cost), or (iii) computational tool bias (the tools may systematically underestimate the impact of common mutations). The design does not distinguish these alternatives. A positive control—e.g., comparing predictions for known high-fitness-cost mutations (e.g., rpoB S450L compensated by rpoA mutations) vs. low-fitness-cost mutations—would help.
- **HARD: Binding affinity predictions not validated.** AutoDock is used to compute binding energies (ΔG in kcal/mol) for mutant vs. wild-type proteins. However, no experimental binding data (e.g., from surface plasmon resonance, isothermal titration calorimetry, or published Kd values) are provided to validate these predictions. The claim that 'All mutations reduced drug binding affinity' (Abstract) rests on unvalidated docking scores. The resolution of the predictions (differences of ~0.1–0.5 kcal/mol between mutations) is within the noise of typical docking tools.
- **HARD: Structural models for embCAB incomplete.** The authors state: 'We were not able to construct all the mutant structures for arabinosyltransferase (emb) because to date, only structural information on the C-terminal domain of embC is available.' This means 226 embCAB mutations are analyzed without complete structural models, yet the ranking and conclusions treat embB mutations (e.g., M306V as 'mild') as equally supported. The impact of missing structural data on the ranking is not quantified.
- **SOFT: No sensitivity analysis of the ranking scheme.** The authors do not test how the ranking changes if: (i) a different concordance threshold is used (e.g., 2/3 tools agree vs. all 3), (ii) weights are reassigned to the five dimensions, or (iii) a single tool is excluded. This would reveal whether the conclusions are robust or dependent on arbitrary choices.
- **SOFT: Geographic analysis descriptive only.** While the regional variation in mutation frequency is well-documented (Fig. 5–6), the authors acknowledge that 'the available data were not granular enough to ascertain the probable reason for the differences' and speculate on poverty, diagnostic quality, and drug exposure. No multivariate analysis or causal inference is attempted. The claim that 'Mtb will follow a similar pattern in future' (Discussion) is extrapolation without support.
- **SOFT: Compensatory mutations mentioned but not analyzed.** The Discussion cites literature on compensatory mutations (e.g., rpoA/rpoC mutations compensating for S450L) but does not analyze whether the 821 mutations in the dataset include compensatory mutations or how their ranking would differ. This is a missed opportunity to refine the fitness cost estimates.
- **SOFT: Overclaiming of 'universal strategy.'** The Abstract and Discussion repeatedly claim a 'universal strategy' of bacterial survival, but the evidence is limited to four drug targets in one species (Mtb) over 30 years. The generalizability to other pathogens, other drugs, or future mutations is not tested. The claim should be hedged to 'consistent pattern in these four targets over this period.'

## Questions
- How is 'concordance' computed for ranking mutations? Specifically: (i) how many of the five criteria (conservation, function, stability, binding, site location) must be met for a mutation to be 'lethal' vs. 'moderate'? (ii) Are all five weighted equally, or are some prioritized? (iii) What happens when tools disagree (e.g., SIFT says 'deleterious' but PolyPhen-2 says 'benign')—is the mutation excluded, or is a majority rule applied?
- Can the authors provide experimental validation of the ranking scheme? For example: (i) comparison of predicted fitness costs to published in vitro growth assays for a subset of mutations, (ii) clinical outcome data (e.g., treatment success rates for patients with 'lethal' vs. 'mild' mutations), or (iii) phylogenetic inference of selection coefficients from sequence data?
- The claim that 'Mtb prefers mild mutations' implies active selection. What evidence rules out the alternative that mild mutations are simply more likely to arise and persist by chance, or that ascertainment bias (mild mutations are more likely to be detected in clinical isolates) explains the pattern?
- Why are the binding affinity predictions (AutoDock ΔG values, Table 1, last column) not validated against experimental data? Are there published Kd or IC50 measurements for any of these drug-target pairs that could serve as a benchmark?
- For the 226 embCAB mutations, how does the incomplete structural data (only C-terminal domain of embC available) affect the ranking? Were embB mutations ranked using homology models, and if so, how reliable are these models? Should embCAB mutations be excluded from the ranking until complete structures are available?
- The study observes that frequent mutations are in non-conserved regions and have mild predicted effects. Is this a causal relationship (bacteria select for mild mutations in non-conserved regions) or a consequence of ascertainment bias (mild mutations are more likely to be detected and reported)? How would you distinguish these?
- The Discussion mentions compensatory mutations (e.g., rpoA/rpoC mutations compensating for S450L in rpoB) but does not analyze them. Are any of the 821 mutations in the dataset compensatory mutations? If so, how should their ranking differ from primary resistance mutations?
- How sensitive are the conclusions to the choice of bioinformatics tools? For example, if only SIFT predictions were used (instead of consensus across three tools), would the ranking of frequent mutations change? Is there a sensitivity analysis?
- The claim of a 'universal strategy' is based on data from four drug targets in Mtb over 30 years. How generalizable is this to other pathogens, other drugs, or future mutations? Should this be hedged in the Abstract and Discussion?
- For pncA, the most frequent mutation (Q10P) has only 1.46% frequency, much lower than S315T (60%) or S450L (56%). The authors suggest this reflects 'limited exposure to the drug.' But could it also reflect that pncA mutations are less likely to confer resistance, or that they have higher fitness costs? How would you test this?
