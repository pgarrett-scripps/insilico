# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript contains several load-bearing claims that are overstated relative to the evidence presented. The central conclusion that multivariate scores are a "viable alternative" is supported, but the claim that they "explicitly represent scale-awareness" is not, as the core experiments do not test scale-aware variants. The abstract's framing and several specific statements in the results over-interpret the findings, particularly regarding the superiority of certain methods and the generalizability of conclusions. These issues require substantial textual revision to align claims with evidence.

## Strengths
- The study design is clear and the comparison between scoring rules is well-structured.
- The manuscript is transparent about its experimental limitations (e.g., smaller models, shorter training).
- The analysis of spectra provides concrete, quantitative evidence for the impact of scale-aware losses.

## Weaknesses
- The abstract states the study compares scoring rules that "aim to explicitly represent scale-awareness in the loss function." This is a HARD overclaim. The first set of experiments (CRPS vs. Global Energy vs. Graph Energy Score) compares **non-scale-aware** versions of these scores. Scale-awareness is only introduced in the second set of experiments (Sec. 3.2), which are separate and not used for the forecast skill comparison in Sec. 4.1. Therefore, the headline finding that "multivariate scores are a viable alternative" is based on experiments that do **not** test the "scale-aware" property highlighted in the abstract. The claim implies a tested feature that was not part of the primary skill comparison.
- The results state: "In the tropics, however, the graph energy score experiment appears to perform best, while the energy score experiment shows some degradation relative to the other two experiments." This is a SOFT overclaim leaning toward HARD. The evidence (Fig. 2) shows very small differences, with confidence intervals/error bars not reported. Describing one as "best" and another as showing "degradation" implies a definitive ranking that the presented data may not support without statistical testing or an assessment of practical significance. A more accurate claim would be that differences are small, with a slight visual advantage for the graph score in the tropics.
- The conclusion that "the choice of multivariate score has a smaller effect" than weighting on spectral fidelity (Discussion) is a SOFT overstatement, as the study did not conduct a controlled comparison holding weighting constant; it compares experiments with different scores **and** different weighting schemes, making it difficult to isolate the independent effect of the score choice.
- The claim that scale-aware losses "improve small-scale variability and lead to more realistic forecast fields" (Results, Sec. 4.2) is supported for the tested configurations, but stating they "lead to" realism is a SOFT causal overreach; the evidence is correlational within this specific model framework.
- Referring to the graph energy score as a "promising alternative" (Discussion) is appropriate, but stating it "performed best" in the tropics (Results, Sec. 4.1) repeats the over-strong ranking noted above.

## Questions
- For the forecast skill comparison (Fig. 2), please provide a measure of uncertainty (e.g., confidence intervals across forecast dates) to justify the qualitative descriptions "best" and "degradation."
- The abstract and introduction frame the work around "scale-aware scoring rules." Please revise to clarify that the primary forecast skill comparison (Sec. 4.1) is between standard, non-scale-aware versions of the scores, while scale-awareness is explored separately in Sec. 4.2.
- The use of an "almost fair" CRPS (α=0.95) is noted, but the rationale for this specific α value over the fair (α=1) or standard (α=0) versions is not discussed. A methodological reviewer should assess if this choice influences the comparability with the fully fair energy score variants.
