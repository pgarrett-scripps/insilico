# Clarity & Presentation Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript is generally well-structured and clearly written, with a logical progression from motivation to results. The central claims are explicitly stated, and the technical definitions are thorough. However, there are several instances of ambiguous pronoun reference and imprecise quantitative language that constitute HARD clarity issues, as they obscure the exact meaning of key comparative statements. These are concentrated in the results section. A minor revision to correct these specific ambiguities is required.

## Strengths
- The manuscript excels at clearly defining all non-standard terms, scoring rules, and experimental configurations upfront, which is essential for a technical audience.
- The narrative structure is strong, with a clear separation of the two experimental aims and a logical flow from question to method to result for each.
- The figures are referenced comprehensively in the text, and the captions are detailed, allowing the reader to understand the findings even without the visual aids.

## Weaknesses
- (HARD - Load-bearing) The central comparative claim in Section 4.1 is stated with ambiguous pronouns, making the precise finding unclear. The text states: "In the tropics, however, the graph energy score experiment appears to perform best, while the energy score experiment shows some degradation relative to the other two experiments." The phrase "the other two experiments" is ambiguous: does it refer to the CRPS and graph energy score experiments (i.e., the energy score is worse than both), or to the CRPS and global energy score experiments (i.e., the energy score is worse than CRPS but compared to an undefined baseline)? This ambiguity directly impacts the interpretation of the primary result. The evidence (Figure 2) cannot resolve this textual ambiguity on its own.
- (HARD - Sweep) Quantitative comparisons in the results are often imprecise. For example: "In the extratropics, we do not find a noticeable difference between the three training objectives." The term "noticeable" is subjective and not quantified. A reader cannot determine if differences were statistically non-significant, below a pre-defined threshold, or visually indistinguishable on the plot. This is a HARD issue for a comparative claim.
- (HARD - Sweep) The description of the multi-scale loss weighting is unclear about a critical operational detail: "We used a similar approach for the spectral losses with per-waveband weighting. These weighting factors are ad hoc and were chosen only to be of the right order of magnitude - estimated from the data - rather than tuned." The method is not recoverable. A reader cannot determine *how* the weights were "estimated from the data" (e.g., by inverse variance, by scaling factor) or what the "right order of magnitude" means procedurally.
- (SOFT - Sweep) The term "scale-aware" is used frequently but is initially defined only implicitly through the description of the multi-scale and spectral loss formulations. A brief explicit definition early on (e.g., "explicitly weighting or scoring different spatial frequency bands in the loss function") would improve clarity for non-specialist readers.
- (SOFT - Sweep) In Section 4.2, the narrative jumps between discussing spectra for different variables (geopotential, wind, temperature). A clearer signposting sentence at the start of each paragraph (e.g., "For geopotential, the results show...") would reduce reader friction.

## Questions
- In the sentence "In the tropics, however, the graph energy score experiment appears to perform best, while the energy score experiment shows some degradation relative to the other two experiments," to which two experiments is "the other two" referring? Please clarify the baseline for comparison.
- For the statement "we do not find a noticeable difference," please specify the metric or threshold used to define "noticeable" (e.g., the magnitude of difference in CRPS, a statistical test result).
- Please specify the exact procedure used to "estimate" the ad hoc weighting factors for the spectral losses from the data, so the method is reproducible.
