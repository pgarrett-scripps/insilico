# Statistics & Data-Analysis Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents a technically sound comparative study of different probabilistic scoring rules for training machine-learned weather models. The central quantitative claims about relative forecast skill are based on a fair verification metric (fair CRPS) applied to a single year of data. While the experimental design is clear, the statistical foundation for concluding that one score is "best" or shows "degradation" is weak due to the lack of any measure of uncertainty or significance testing for the skill differences. The spectral analyses are descriptive and lack a formal test for whether the differences between experiments are meaningful or consistent across initializations.

## Strengths
- The paper clearly defines and implements several sophisticated, fair, and proper scoring rules, a rigorous foundation for probabilistic model training.
- The experimental design is transparent, allowing for a direct comparison of loss objectives while controlling for architecture and training data.
- The analysis of forecast spectra provides a valuable, physically-motivated diagnostic that goes beyond standard integrated skill scores.

## Weaknesses
- Load-bearing: The claim of differential forecast skill lacks statistical support. The manuscript states that forecast skill is "broadly similar" but that in the tropics the graph energy score "performs best" while the energy score "shows some degradation" (Fig. 2 caption and text). This conclusion is drawn from visual inspection of skill curves for a single verification year (2022). There is no reported measure of variability (e.g., confidence intervals across bootstrap samples of dates, or inter-annual variability), no stated test for significance of differences, and the sample size (n=1 year) is far too small to support a claim about the relative performance of these methods. The observed differences could easily be due to random sampling of weather patterns in 2022. A proper evaluation would require testing over multiple independent years (or a large set of bootstrap samples from a longer period) and reporting the mean difference in skill with a confidence interval or the result of a paired significance test.
- Load-bearing: The evaluation of spectral improvements is purely qualitative. The conclusion that "scale-aware losses improve spectral fidelity" is based on visual comparison of many spectral plots (Figs. 3-14). The manuscript does not quantify the discrepancy between forecast and ERA5 spectra (e.g., via an integrated squared error metric) or test whether the differences between experiments are statistically significant. Without a quantitative metric and a test, it is impossible to judge the magnitude of the improvement or to rigorously rank the different scale-aware methods. The statement that "the edge-CRPS experiment and the spectral magnitude CRPS experiment seem to be slightly more successful" is an impression, not a supported finding.
- The ensemble size for forecast evaluation is M=8. The properties of verification scores (like fair CRPS) can be sensitive to ensemble size, especially for multivariate scores. The conclusions about relative skill might change with a larger evaluation ensemble, but this sensitivity is not explored or discussed.
- For the spectral loss experiments, the waveband and variable weighting factors are described as "ad hoc" and chosen to be "of the right order of magnitude". This is a valid pragmatic choice, but it means the performance of these experiments is contingent on an untuned hyperparameter. The study does not include a sensitivity analysis to show how critical these weights are to the result.
- The analysis uses accumulated tendencies over 84 initializations. While this provides multiple samples, the initializations are only 4 days apart, violating the assumption of independence required for standard statistical tests. The effective sample size for estimating spectral properties is less than 84.

## Questions
- For the forecast skill comparison in Section 4.1, could you please calculate the mean difference in fair CRPS (or energy score) between the experiments (e.g., Graph Energy vs. CRPS) over a multi-year test period (e.g., 2017-2021), and report this difference with a 95% confidence interval obtained via bootstrapping across dates?
- For the spectral analysis in Section 4.2, please provide a quantitative summary: for each experiment and variable, compute the root-mean-square error (RMSE) between the forecast and ERA5 log-spectrum (averaged over lead times 24h and 48h, and over all initializations). A table of these RMSE values would allow a direct, objective comparison of which loss best reproduces the reference spectrum.
- The manuscript states the graph energy score "may fail to be strictly proper". For the combined loss `L_graph = fGES_graph + 0.1 fES`, is the resulting composite score guaranteed to be strictly proper? If not, what are the implications for using it as a training objective?
