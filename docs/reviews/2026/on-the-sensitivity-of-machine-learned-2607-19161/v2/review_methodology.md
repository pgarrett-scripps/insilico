# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript's core comparative claims are not fully supported by its experimental design. The primary conclusions about the relative performance of different scoring rules rest on comparisons where key variables (model capacity, training schedule, and loss weighting) are not adequately controlled or isolated. While the study explores an important question and the multi-scale experiments are well-conceived, the design does not permit a clean attribution of differences in forecast skill or spectra to the choice of scoring rule alone. Major revision is needed to strengthen the causal inference.

## Strengths
- The study tackles a relevant and timely methodological question in machine-learned weather forecasting.
- The multi-scale and spectral loss experiments are a robust design for probing how scale-awareness is enforced.
- The use of a consistent framework (Anemoi) and the public implementation of scores are commendable for reproducibility.

## Weaknesses
- LOAD-BEARING: The forecast skill comparison (Section 3.1) confounds the choice of scoring rule with differences in effective loss weighting and model optimization. The claim that "multivariate scores are a viable alternative to CRPS-based training" and that the graph energy score "performs somewhat better" in the tropics is not earned by the presented design. The experiments use different loss formulations (e.g., CRPS vs. fGESgraph+0.1 fES) which have different functional forms and, critically, may apply different implicit weights to spatial scales and forecast errors. An alternative explanation for the observed skill differences is not that the *scoring rule type* (univariate vs. multivariate) is decisive, but that the specific *weighting of spatial information* in the loss function differs. The design does not control for this confound. To isolate the effect of the scoring rule, one would need experiments where the spatial weighting (e.g., through a multi-scale decomposition or spectral band weights) is held constant across all scoring rules being compared.
- LOAD-BEARING: The spectra comparison (Section 3.2) is a well-designed test of scale-awareness, but it conflates the mechanism of scale-awareness with the scoring rule itself. The conclusion that "scale awareness alone does not guarantee realistic variability" and that "weighting... can matter as much as... the specific mechanism" is supported. However, the design cannot support broader inferences about the superiority of one *scoring rule* (e.g., edge-CRPS) over another for spectral fidelity, because the experiments differ in multiple simultaneous factors: scoring rule type, use of multi-scale decomposition, and the application of ad-hoc variable/scale weights (Table 2). The "single-scale CRPS" and "global energy score" are poor baselines for assessing spectral performance of other rules, as they lack any explicit scale constraint. A stronger design would apply the *same* multi-scale or spectral weighting scheme to each scoring rule variant, so that differences could be attributed to the rule itself.
- The training schedules and model sizes differ between the two main experimental sets (Sections 3.1 and 3.2), making it difficult to synthesize a unified conclusion about the sensitivity to scoring rules.
- The use of "ad hoc" weighting factors for geopotential and pressure in multi-scale/spectral experiments, while pragmatic, introduces an uncontrolled variable that complicates the interpretation of between-experiment differences.
- The claim that the graph energy score "may fail to be strictly proper" is noted, but the design does not test or discuss the practical consequences of this potential lack of strict propriety on forecast quality.
- For the forecast skill comparison, verification is done at a degraded resolution (1.5°), which may blunt the detection of differences in small-scale spatial coherence that multivariate scores are intended to improve.

## Questions
- For the forecast skill experiments (3.1), could you run an additional "multi-scale CRPS" experiment with the same model size and training schedule as the "graph energy" experiment, to provide a baseline where scale-awareness is controlled for?
- In the spectra experiments (3.2), the "multi-scale graph energy" experiment includes a weak global anchor. What was the impact of removing or varying the weight (0.1) of this anchor on the spectra?
- (For the Data Analysis reviewer) The manuscript states the spectral experiments used "weighting factors... chosen only to be of the right order of magnitude - estimated from the data - rather than tuned." Please provide the specific estimated values and the method of estimation, as this choice significantly affects the loss landscape.
