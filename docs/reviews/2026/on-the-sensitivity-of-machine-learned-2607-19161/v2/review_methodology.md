# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript compares different scoring rules (CRPS, energy score, graph energy score, and variants) as training objectives for machine-learned weather forecasting, and examines how scale-aware loss formulations affect forecast field spectra. The core experimental design has merit—comparing training objectives under matched conditions—but suffers from significant confounds and incomplete controls that prevent firm conclusions about which scoring rules or scale-awareness mechanisms are superior. The first experiment (Section 3.1) compares only three loss functions with no ablation to isolate the source of differences; the second (Section 3.2) tests twelve configurations but lacks systematic controls to disentangle the effects of multivariate localization, scale decomposition, weighting schemes, and spectral transforms. The claim that 'scale-aware losses improve spectral fidelity' is supported by the spectral analysis, but the claim that 'graph energy score performs best' in the tropics (Section 4.1) rests on a single comparison without replication or sensitivity analysis. The paper reads as a preliminary exploration rather than a definitive sensitivity study, and the authors acknowledge limitations (smaller models, low resolution, short training) that substantially constrain generalizability.

## Strengths
- The manuscript introduces a clear taxonomy of scoring rules (CRPS, energy score, graph energy score, graph variogram score, graph edge energy score, CRPS edge score) with precise mathematical definitions and a schematic (Figure 1) that aids understanding of the localization strategies.
- The experimental setup in Section 3.1 maintains matched architecture, training schedule, and data across the three main experiments, which is a necessary condition for fair comparison of training objectives.
- The spectral analysis in Section 4.2 provides a quantitative diagnostic of forecast field realism across scales, moving beyond large-scale skill metrics to examine physical plausibility—a valuable contribution to understanding trade-offs in loss design.
- The authors acknowledge key limitations (model capacity, resolution, training duration) and note that their conclusions may not generalize to larger, higher-resolution settings, which is honest and appropriate for a preliminary study.
- The use of the Anemoi framework and torch.compile for efficient score computation is well-motivated and reproducible.

## Weaknesses
- HARD: Section 3.1 compares three loss functions (CRPS, global energy score, graph energy score) but does not isolate the source of differences. The graph energy score experiment includes a weak global energy score anchor (L_graph = fGESgraph + 0.1 fES), while the global energy score experiment uses only fES. This confounds the effect of localization (graph vs. global) with the effect of the anchor term. To isolate localization, the design should compare fGESgraph + 0.1 fES (graph with anchor) against fES + 0.1 fES (global with anchor) or fGESgraph alone (graph without anchor). As written, the superiority of graph energy in the tropics (Figure 2) cannot be attributed to the graph structure itself.
- HARD: Section 3.2 tests twelve loss configurations (Table 2) but lacks a systematic factorial design to decompose the effects of (i) multivariate vs. univariate scoring, (ii) localization strategy (global, graph, edge), (iii) scale decomposition (single-scale vs. multi-scale), and (iv) weighting (unweighted vs. per-scale/per-variable weighting). The authors state 'weighting of different fields, scales, and variables can matter as much as, or more than, specific mechanism used to inject spatial awareness' (Section 5), but this conclusion rests on visual inspection of spectra (Figures 3–14) rather than a controlled comparison. For example, comparing 'Multi-scale CRPS' (Table 2) to 'Spectral magnitude CRPS weighted' conflates scale decomposition method, weighting scheme, and spectral representation. A factorial design with orthogonal variation would clarify which factors drive the observed differences.
- HARD: The claim that 'scale-aware losses improve spectral fidelity' (Section 5) is based on visual comparison of spectral ratios in Figures 3–14, but no quantitative metric (e.g., RMS error in spectral amplitude, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test against ERA5 spectrum) is provided to support this claim. The figures show that different losses produce different spectra, but 'improvement' is not formally defined or measured. Which loss is 'better'—one that matches ERA5 at all scales, or one that slightly overcompensates at small scales (as noted in Section 4.2)? Without a metric, the comparison is subjective.
- HARD: The experiments in Section 3.2 use a 'smaller model' and 'shorter training schedule' (Section 3.2) to reduce computational cost, but the manuscript does not specify the model size, training duration, or learning rates for these experiments. This prevents assessment of whether the spectral results are robust to the reduced training regime or whether they reflect incomplete convergence. The authors note that 'greater model capacity and longer training may improve performance of losses without scale awareness or reduce differences between experiments' (Section 5), but this is speculation without evidence.
- HARD: The manuscript does not report confidence intervals, error bars, or statistical significance tests for the forecast skill comparisons in Figure 2 or the spectral comparisons in Figures 3–14. The results are presented as point estimates from a single training run per configuration (implied by 'we run three experiments' in Section 3.1 and the singular 'we use' in Section 3.2). Without replication, it is impossible to distinguish signal from noise, especially in the tropics where differences appear small (Figure 2, right panels).
- HARD: The paper claims that 'graph energy score experiment performs best' in the tropics (Section 4.1, Figure 2), but the figure shows only modest visual differences between the three experiments, and no quantitative comparison (e.g., mean absolute error, correlation) is provided. The claim of superiority is not supported by a statistical test or a clear quantitative metric.
- SOFT: The choice of hyperparameters for the multi-scale and spectral experiments (e.g., kernel widths of 100, 200, 400, 800 km; spectral truncation T191; waveband groupings) is described as 'ad hoc and chosen only to be of right order of magnitude—estimated from data—rather than tuned' (Section 3.2). This introduces an uncontrolled source of variation: different hyperparameter choices might favor different loss functions. A sensitivity analysis varying these parameters would strengthen the conclusions.
- SOFT: The manuscript does not compare the proposed graph-based localization to the patch-based localization of Pacchiardi et al. (2024, ref. 19), which is cited as motivation. The authors state that graph-based localization is 'more flexible' but provide no empirical evidence that it outperforms patch-based approaches on the same task.
- SOFT: The spectral analysis aggregates results over all 84 initialization dates in 2022 but does not stratify by weather regime (e.g., blocking, cyclones, trade winds) or season. Spectral properties may vary substantially across regimes, and a loss function that performs well on average may perform poorly in specific conditions. Regime-stratified analysis would provide more actionable insights.
- The manuscript uses 'accumulated tendency spectra' (differences between forecast and initial state) as the diagnostic, but does not justify why this is the appropriate metric for assessing forecast realism. Accumulated tendencies reflect the model's ability to generate changes, but they may not directly correlate with forecast skill or physical plausibility of the forecast state itself.

## Questions
- Section 3.1: Why does the graph energy score experiment include a weak global energy score anchor (L_graph = fGESgraph + 0.1 fES) while the global energy score experiment does not? To isolate the effect of localization, should both experiments include an anchor term?
- Section 3.1: Figure 2 shows only modest differences between the three training objectives, especially in the extratropics. Are these differences statistically significant, or could they reflect noise from a single training run? Can the authors provide error bars or confidence intervals?
- Section 3.2: How were the hyperparameters (kernel widths, spectral truncation, waveband groupings) chosen? Were they tuned on a validation set, or estimated from the training data? How sensitive are the spectral results to these choices?
- Section 3.2: The authors state that 'weighting of different fields, scales, and variables can matter as much as, or more than, specific mechanism used to inject spatial awareness' (Section 5). Can you provide a controlled comparison isolating the effect of weighting from the effect of the scoring rule or scale decomposition method?
- Section 4.2: What quantitative metric is used to assess whether a loss function 'improves' spectral fidelity? How is 'improvement' defined relative to ERA5? Are the differences in Figures 3–14 statistically significant?
- Section 4.2: The manuscript notes that 'some experiments show slight overcompensation for some variables, where early lead-time tendencies contain less small-scale variability than ERA5 reference tendency.' Which experiments show this, and is overcompensation considered desirable or undesirable?
- Section 3.2: What are the model size, embedding dimension, number of layers, and training duration for the experiments in Section 3.2? The text mentions 'embedding dimension of 256 and 12 processor layers' but does not specify the number of optimization steps or wall-clock training time.
- Section 4.1: The claim that graph energy score 'performs somewhat better' in the tropics (Abstract, Section 4.1) is based on visual inspection of Figure 2. Can you quantify this difference (e.g., mean CRPS, correlation with observations) and test for statistical significance?
- Section 4.2: Why are accumulated tendency spectra the appropriate diagnostic for forecast realism? How do they relate to forecast skill (e.g., CRPS, anomaly correlation) or physical plausibility of the forecast state?
- Section 5: The authors acknowledge that 'greater model capacity and longer training may improve performance of losses without scale awareness or reduce differences between experiments.' Can you provide evidence (e.g., learning curves, convergence diagnostics) that the models in Section 3.2 have converged?
