# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents a systematic comparison of different scoring rules for training probabilistic machine-learned weather forecast models, with a focus on scale-aware formulations. The core novelty claim—that multivariate scoring rules are a viable alternative to CRPS-based training for global machine-learned weather forecasting—is supported and appears new based on my searches. However, the positioning of the graph energy score as novel requires clarification relative to prior work on patched energy scores. The contribution is incremental but useful, with appropriate framing as a preliminary study.

## Strengths
- The systematic comparison of multiple scoring rule variants (CRPS, energy score, graph energy score, graph variogram score, edge scores) provides valuable empirical evidence for the field.
- The manuscript appropriately acknowledges key prior work, including Pacchiardi et al. (2021) on patched energy scores and the authors' own prior work on multi-scale loss formulations.
- The study addresses an important practical question about whether the success of state-of-the-art models is tied specifically to CRPS or extends to multivariate scoring rules.

## Weaknesses
- Load-bearing claim 1: The graph energy score is presented as a novel localization approach for multivariate scoring rules. The manuscript states "the motivation is similar to the patched energy score of [19]" and cites Pacchiardi et al. (2021) on patched energy scores. My search for "patched energy score weather forecasting" returned no direct hits, but the reference [19] is "Probabilistic forecasting with generative networks via scoring rule minimization" (2021), which indeed discusses patched energy scores. The manuscript's claim that graph-based localization "provides a more flexible way to localize multivariate scoring rules" compared to "fixed rectangular patches" is plausible, but the delta from patched energy scores needs clearer articulation: what specific advantages does the graph formulation offer over patch-based approaches beyond the stated flexibility for irregular grids? The evidence presented (similar forecast skill to CRPS) doesn't distinguish whether this is due to the graph formulation specifically or simply to any form of localization.
- Load-bearing claim 2: The study demonstrates that "multivariate scores are a viable alternative to CRPS-based training for global machine-learned weather forecasting." This claim is supported by the results showing "forecast skill is broadly similar" across experiments. However, the conclusion that multivariate scores are "viable" could be strengthened by addressing why the global energy score experiment showed degradation in the tropics while the graph energy score performed best—this differential outcome suggests that not all multivariate scores are equally viable, and the localization aspect may be crucial. The alternative explanation is that the degradation is due to implementation details rather than fundamental properties of the scoring rules.

## Questions
- For the graph energy score experiment that performed best in the tropics, what specific aspects of the graph formulation (k=16 neighbors, uniform weights) contributed to this result compared to what a patch-based approach would achieve?
- The manuscript states "the graph energy score, however, may fail to be strictly proper"—could you quantify how much this theoretical limitation affected practical performance compared to the strictly proper alternatives?
