# Debate Transcript

## Advocate — round 1

This manuscript makes a genuine, well-scoped contribution: it introduces graph-based localizations of multivariate scoring rules (graph energy score, graph variogram score, graph edge energy/CRPS scores) as new tools for training machine-learned weather models, and tests them in a controlled comparison against CRPS under matched architecture, data, and training schedule (Section 3.1). Every reviewer agrees this design isolates the scoring-rule variable fairly, and the novelty reviewer explicitly credits the graph-based localization as new for weather forecasting. The paper is explicitly framed as a 'preliminary study' in the abstract, which is honest rather than evasive—reviewers repeatedly praise this candour (methodology, rigor, data_analysis reviews all note transparent acknowledgment of limitations). The core findings—that multivariate scores achieve broadly similar skill to CRPS (Fig. 2) and that explicit scale-awareness improves spectral realism (Figs. 3-14)—are real, useful signals for a fast-moving field, consistent with the journal's apparent tolerance for preliminary, hypothesis-generating work. Many flagged weaknesses are squarely fixable in revision without new experiments: reporting exact ζ_i weights and kernel widths, adding a commit hash for Anemoi, stating random seeds, clarifying Figure 3 vs 4 captions, and quantifying 'somewhat better' with numbers already implicit in existing runs. These are presentation and disclosure fixes, not fatal design flaws. The one area requiring real caution—confounding of model size/weighting/schedule in Section 3.2—is itself openly disclosed by the authors and appropriately hedged in their conclusions ('differences...comparatively small'), so the paper does not overclaim beyond what a preliminary study can support.

**Key points:**
- Novel graph-based localized scoring rules (graph energy, variogram, edge scores) extend multivariate score-based training to irregular-grid settings, a genuine methodological contribution
- Section 3.1 comparison is well-controlled (same architecture/data/schedule), fairly isolating the scoring-rule variable per multiple reviewers
- Explicit 'preliminary study' framing and candid limitation disclosure throughout matches appropriate epistemic humility, not overclaiming
- Many reviewer-flagged issues (missing weights/seeds/commit hashes, figure caption clarity, quantifying differences) are revision-fixable disclosure gaps, not fundamental design failures
- Authors already hedge appropriately where evidence is weakest (e.g., 'comparatively small' differences in Section 4.2), showing claims are scaled to the evidence

## Skeptic — round 1

I concede Section 3.1's three-way CRPS/energy/graph-energy comparison is well-controlled — that's real evidence. But the advocate glosses over the paper's *second* major claim, which rests on a genuinely confounded design, not a disclosure gap. The methodology reviewer flags as HARD that the twelve Section 3.2 experiments simultaneously vary scoring rule, model size (256 vs 512 embedding), training schedule, and 'ad hoc' scale/waveband weights chosen only 'to be of the right order of magnitude' (Sec 3.2). The manuscript itself undercuts its own headline abstract claim — 'any form of explicit scale-awareness improves realism' — by admitting 'after introducing waveband weighting factors, the spectra look very similar' (Sec 4.2), which the methodology reviewer correctly reads as evidence that weighting, not scale-awareness per se, is doing the work. No ablation isolates scoring-rule effect from weighting effect. Layer onto this the reproducibility and data_analysis reviewers' HARD findings: no random seeds reported, apparently single-run experiments per configuration, and zero error bars across 84 initialization dates — so we cannot tell whether the 'edge-CRPS slightly more successful' claims (Sec 4.2) reflect signal or training noise. This isn't a caption-fix or a missing commit hash; it's the evidentiary basis for the paper's core spectral conclusion. Calling this 'appropriately hedged' is generous — the abstract states scale-awareness 'improves realism' as fact, not as a tentative, confound-laden observation. Disentangling weighting from scale-awareness requires new ablation experiments with seed replication, which exceeds ordinary revision. This is FIXABLE only via substantial new experimentation, not text editing, and I'd treat it as near-fatal for the strong form of the claim as currently written.

**Key points:**
- Section 3.1 comparison is genuinely well-controlled — conceded
- Section 3.2's twelve experiments confound scoring rule with model size, training schedule, and ad hoc weighting (methodology reviewer HARD flag)
- Manuscript's own text ('after introducing waveband weighting factors, the spectra look very similar') undermines the abstract's unhedged claim that scale-awareness itself drives realism
- No random seeds, apparently single runs, no error bars across 84 init dates (reproducibility/data_analysis HARD flags) — can't distinguish signal from training noise
- Resolving this requires new ablation experiments with replication, not mere disclosure fixes, making it near-fatal for the claim as stated
