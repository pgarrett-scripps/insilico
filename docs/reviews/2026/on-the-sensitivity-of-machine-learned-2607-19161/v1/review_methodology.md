# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript compares machine-learned weather forecast models trained with different proper scoring rules and scale-aware loss formulations. The experimental design is generally sound for its stated scope—comparing forecast skill across training objectives and examining spectral properties—but has notable limitations in scope, control, and generalizability that prevent it from fully supporting some of its broader claims. The first experiment (Section 3.1) uses appropriate matched conditions and fair comparators, but the second (Section 3.2) introduces multiple confounds (model size, training schedule, weighting schemes) that make it difficult to isolate the effect of the scoring rule itself. The authors acknowledge these limitations candidly, which is commendable, but the design does not fully support claims about which scoring mechanisms are superior—only that scale awareness matters and that weighting choices are consequential.

## Strengths
- Matched experimental conditions in Section 3.1: architecture, data, training schedule, and resolution are held constant across the three main comparisons (CRPS, global energy score, graph energy score), isolating the scoring rule as the variable of interest.
- Fair comparators: the paper compares against CRPS (the current standard in AIFS-CRPS) and includes two multivariate alternatives, rather than comparing only against weak baselines.
- Transparent acknowledgment of limitations: the authors explicitly state (Section 5) that experiments used 'smaller models, relatively low spatial resolution, and a shortened training schedule' and that 'greater model capacity and longer training may improve the performance of losses without scale awareness or reduce the differences between experiments.' This candour is appropriate for a preliminary study.
- Consistent evaluation metrics across experiments: all three Section 3.1 experiments are verified using the same metrics (fair CRPS, energy score, graph energy score), avoiding metric-switching bias.
- Multiple diagnostic approaches: the paper uses both forecast skill metrics and spectral analysis to assess model behaviour, providing complementary views of performance.

## Weaknesses
-  HARD — Confounding of scoring rule and model configuration in Section 3.2: the twelve experiments in Table 2 differ not only in the scoring rule but also in model size (256 embedding vs. 512 in Section 3.1), training schedule (two phases vs. three), and ad hoc weighting schemes ('chosen only to be of the right order of magnitude - estimated from the data - rather than tuned'). This makes it impossible to isolate whether differences in spectral fidelity arise from the scoring rule, the weighting scheme, or the reduced model capacity. The authors acknowledge weighting matters ('as much as, or more than, the specific mechanism'), but the design does not cleanly separate these factors.
- HARD — Lack of ablation for scale-aware weighting in Section 3.2: the paper shows that scale-aware losses improve spectra and that weighting matters, but does not systematically ablate the weighting scheme itself (e.g., by comparing the same scoring rule with and without ad hoc variable/scale weighting). This is a critical confound because the authors note that 'after introducing waveband weighting factors, the spectra look very similar' (Section 4.2), suggesting weighting dominates the choice of score. Without isolating weighting from scoring rule, the claim that 'multivariate scoring rules are a promising direction' is not fully supported by the spectral results.
- HARD — Single-run experiments without replication: each of the twelve Section 3.2 experiments appears to be run once (no mention of multiple seeds or replicates). For a machine-learning study, this is a significant limitation. Variance in training (initialization, data shuffling) could be comparable to or larger than differences between loss functions, especially given the shortened training schedule. The paper does not report error bars, confidence intervals, or multiple runs.
- SOFT — Limited resolution and model scale: the O96 (~1°) resolution in Section 3.1 and the smaller model in Section 3.2 are acknowledged limitations, but they raise questions about whether conclusions generalize to operational scales. The authors note (Section 5) that 'experiments at higher resolution may yield different conclusions,' which suggests the design may not capture the phenomena of interest at scales where the method will be deployed.
- SOFT — Incomplete specification of graph construction: the paper states that the graph connectivity for graph-based scores uses 'a k-nearest-neighbour graph with k=16 on the O96 reduced Gaussian grid' (Section 3.1), but does not justify this choice or test sensitivity to it. For a method that claims to be 'flexible' and 'not tied to fixed rectangular windows,' the choice of k and its impact on results is underexplored.
- SOFT — Spectral analysis limited to one lead time and aggregation: accumulated tendency spectra are shown for 2022 forecasts, but the paper does not report how results vary across lead times (1–12 steps) or seasons. Spectral properties may degrade differently across lead times for different loss functions, and this variation is not characterized.
- SOFT — No comparison to other scale-aware approaches: the paper compares different scoring rules and scale-aware formulations, but does not compare to other methods for improving spectral fidelity mentioned in Section 5 (e.g., conditional layer norms, reference field truncation, spatially correlated noise). This limits the ability to position the contribution relative to existing practice.

## Questions
- In Section 3.2, were the twelve experiments each run once, or were multiple random seeds used? If single runs, what is the estimated variance in the metrics due to training stochasticity?
- Can the authors provide an ablation in which the same scoring rule (e.g., multi-scale CRPS) is trained with and without the ad hoc variable/scale weighting, to isolate the effect of weighting from the effect of the scoring rule itself?
- The choice of k=16 for the k-nearest-neighbour graph in Section 3.1 is not justified. How sensitive are the results to this choice? Does the graph energy score remain superior to CRPS across a range of k values?
- In Section 3.2, how do spectral properties vary across lead times (1–12 steps)? Do all loss functions degrade similarly, or do some maintain spectral fidelity longer?
- The paper states that the global energy score 'does not appear to offer an advantage over CRPS' in Section 4.2, but Figure 2 shows it performs worse in the tropics (Section 4.1). Is this a genuine degradation, or is it within the noise of the experiment? What is the statistical significance of the tropical difference?
- Can the authors clarify whether the 'weak global energy score anchor' (0.1 × fES) in the graph energy score experiment was tuned, or was this weight chosen a priori? If tuned, on what data?
- The paper claims 'multivariate scoring rules are a promising direction for generative modelling' (Section 5), but the spectral results show that weighting matters more than the choice of score. Does this conclusion still hold given the confounding of weighting and scoring rule in Section 3.2?
- How do the results change if the model capacity in Section 3.2 is increased to match Section 3.1? Would the differences between scoring rules persist, or would they be washed out by improved model capacity?
