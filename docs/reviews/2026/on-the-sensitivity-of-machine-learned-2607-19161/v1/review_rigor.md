# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript compares different probabilistic scoring rules as training objectives for machine-learned weather forecasting, with a focus on scale-aware variants. The work is technically sound in its presentation of scoring rules and experimental setup, but the claims in the abstract and title outrun the evidence presented. The core issue is that the paper presents preliminary results from limited experiments (low resolution, short training schedules, small models) yet frames conclusions as general findings about the viability of multivariate scores for weather forecasting. The forecast skill comparison (Section 4.1) shows only marginal differences across methods with no statistical significance testing, and the spectral analysis (Section 4.2) is largely descriptive. The authors acknowledge limitations but do not adequately hedge their main claims to match the evidence.

## Strengths
- The mathematical presentation of scoring rules (Section 2) is clear and rigorous, with fair variants properly defined and distinguished.
- The experimental design comparing three loss objectives while holding architecture and data constant (Section 3.1) is methodologically sound for isolating the effect of the scoring rule.
- The authors transparently acknowledge key limitations: smaller models, low resolution (O96 ≈ 1°), shortened training schedules, and that 'greater model capacity and longer training may improve performance of losses without scale awareness or reduce differences between experiments' (Discussion).
- The use of spectral analysis as a diagnostic of physical realism is well-motivated and provides interpretable output beyond standard verification metrics.
- Implementation details are provided (Anemoi framework, graph connectivity, training schedule, learning rates), supporting reproducibility.

## Weaknesses
- The abstract claims 'multivariate scores [are] viable alternative to CRPS-based training for global machine-learned weather forecasting' but the evidence is limited to one low-resolution experiment (O96) with a small model and short training schedule. The phrase 'viable alternative' implies broad applicability not demonstrated here. This is a HARD overclaim: the results show multivariate scores perform comparably in extratropics and slightly better in tropics at one resolution, not that they are a proven alternative for operational forecasting.
- The title 'On the sensitivity of machine-learned probabilistic weather forecast models to scale-aware scoring rules' promises a sensitivity analysis but the paper does not quantify sensitivity (e.g., no error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical tests on the differences shown in Figure 2). The visual differences in Figure 2 are described qualitatively ('small differences', 'somewhat better', 'some degradation') without statistical support. This is HARD: a sensitivity analysis requires quantified measures of how much the output changes with input variation.
- The abstract states 'forecast skill broadly similar' across methods but provides no statistical test or effect-size estimate to support this claim. Figure 2 shows spatial maps but no summary statistics, p-values, or confidence intervals. The claim 'broadly similar' is defensible visually but unsupported quantitatively. This is SOFT: the claim is hedged ('broadly') but lacks the rigor expected for a primary result.
- Section 4.2 (spectra analysis) is largely descriptive. The paper shows that scale-aware losses improve small-scale variability (Figures 3–14) but does not quantify the magnitude of improvement or test whether differences are statistically significant. The statement 'making forecast scores scale-aware substantially improves small-scale variability' (Discussion) is not backed by a metric or test. This is SOFT: the conclusion is reasonable from the figures but overstated without quantification.
- The paper states 'differences between scale-aware loss objectives comparatively small' (Section 4.2) but does not define 'small' or provide a metric. This is SOFT: the claim is vague and would benefit from a quantitative threshold or comparison to a baseline.
- The claim that 'weighting of different fields, scales, and variables can matter as much as, or more than, specific mechanism used to inject spatial awareness' (Discussion) is based on visual inspection of Figures 3–14 and is not quantified. No ablation study or statistical test isolates the contribution of weighting vs. mechanism. This is SOFT: the observation is plausible but presented as a conclusion without evidence.
- The paper does not report how many initialization dates or forecast lead times were used to compute the spectra in Section 4.2. It states '84 initialization dates in total' but does not specify the lead time range over which spectra were averaged. This affects the interpretability of the results. This is SOFT: procedural clarity is needed.
- The abstract claims the study 'analyse[s] how different scoring rules and scale-aware loss constraints shape spectra of forecast fields' but the analysis is observational (showing spectra) rather than mechanistic (explaining why). The paper does not isolate which component of the loss (e.g., multivariate structure vs. scale weighting) drives the spectral changes. This is SOFT: the framing overstates the depth of analysis.
- The paper uses ad hoc weighting factors for multi-scale experiments ('chosen only to be of right order of magnitude - estimated from data - rather than tuned', Section 3.2). This introduces an uncontrolled variable that could confound comparisons. The authors acknowledge this but do not test sensitivity to these choices. This is SOFT: a sensitivity analysis on weighting factors would strengthen the claims.

## Questions
- Figure 2 shows spatial maps but no global summary statistics. What are the mean CRPS differences (and confidence intervals or standard errors) between the three experiments across the full domain and at each lead time? Without this, the claim 'forecast skill broadly similar' cannot be verified.
- In Section 4.2, over what lead-time range were the accumulated tendency spectra computed? The paper states spectra were computed from 'forecasts initialized every four days' but does not specify whether all lead times (1–8 steps) were pooled or analyzed separately. This affects interpretation.
- How were the ad hoc weighting factors in Section 3.2 chosen? The paper states they were 'estimated from data' but does not specify the procedure. Were they tuned on the training set, validation set, or test set? A sensitivity analysis varying these factors by ±50% would clarify their impact.
- The paper claims graph energy score 'performs somewhat better' in the tropics (Abstract) but Figure 2 (right column) shows only visual differences. What is the magnitude of the difference in CRPS or another metric, and is it statistically significant at the 95% confidence level?
- In Section 2, the paper states that graph energy score 'may fail to be strictly proper' but then combines it with a weak global anchor to recover propriety. How sensitive are the results to the weight of this anchor (0.1 in the experiments)? Was this weight tuned or chosen a priori?
- The paper compares twelve loss configurations in Section 3.2 but uses a smaller model and shorter training schedule than Section 3.1 to 'reduce computational cost'. How do the results in Section 3.1 (full model) compare to the corresponding experiments in Section 3.2 (reduced model)? Without this, it is unclear whether the spectral conclusions generalize.
- The Discussion states 'greater model capacity and longer training may improve performance of losses without scale awareness or reduce differences between experiments'. This is a major caveat. What is the minimum model capacity or training duration needed to draw robust conclusions? Can the authors provide a brief experiment at intermediate scale to bound this uncertainty?
