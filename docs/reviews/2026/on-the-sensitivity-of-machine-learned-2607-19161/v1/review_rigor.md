# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This is a methodologically sound empirical study comparing scoring rules for training machine-learned weather forecasts. The core claims are appropriately hedged and supported by the experiments conducted. However, the abstract and title make broader claims about 'sensitivity' than the evidence strictly warrants, and several load-bearing claims about scale-awareness benefits rest on visual inspection of spectra rather than quantitative metrics. The work is honest about limitations and preliminary scope, but some claims about what the results 'suggest' or 'show' outrun the statistical evidence presented. The paper is suitable for publication with clarifications on claim scope and quantification of key spectral findings.

## Strengths
- The authors explicitly acknowledge limitations (smaller models, low resolution, shortened training) and frame the work as preliminary, which appropriately scales claims.
- Forecast skill comparison (Section 4.1) is well-controlled: architecture, data, and training are held constant across three experiments, isolating the effect of the scoring rule.
- The paper is transparent about the lack of strict propriety in the graph energy score and the mitigation strategy (adding a global anchor), which is honest about a potential weakness.
- Multiple scoring rules are tested systematically, and the authors report results across multiple variables and regions rather than cherry-picking.
- The spectral analysis uses a consistent methodology (accumulated tendency spectra, 84 initialization dates, T191 truncation) and compares against ERA5 reference, providing a quantitative reference frame.

## Weaknesses
- The title claims 'sensitivity of machine-learned probabilistic weather forecast models to scale-aware scoring rules,' but the experiments test only three univariate/multivariate scoring rules in Section 3.1 (CRPS, global energy, graph energy). The word 'sensitivity' implies a systematic exploration of how forecast properties vary with scoring rule choice; instead, the paper shows that three specific rules produce 'broadly similar' skill (Section 4.1, Figure 2). This is a null result for sensitivity, not a demonstration of it. The title should be reworded to reflect that the paper compares specific rules, not explores sensitivity across a range.
- The abstract states 'multivariate scores are a viable alternative to CRPS-based training' based on Section 4.1, but Figure 2 shows the graph energy score performs 'somewhat better' only in the tropics, while the global energy score 'shows some degradation.' The claim of viability is supported, but the abstract does not mention the tropical degradation of the global energy score, which is a material qualifier.
- Section 4.2 (spectra results) relies heavily on visual inspection of Figures 3–14 without quantitative metrics. The claim that 'scale-aware losses improve spectral fidelity' (Discussion, line 1) is supported by visual comparison, but no numerical summary (e.g., RMS error vs. ERA5 spectra, correlation of spectral slopes) is provided. This makes it difficult to assess the magnitude of improvement or whether differences are meaningful. The statement 'differences between scale-aware loss objectives are comparatively small' (Section 4.2) is qualitative; a quantitative threshold would strengthen this.
- The paper states that 'the weighting of different fields, scales, and variables can matter as much as, or more than, the specific mechanism' (Discussion), but this is inferred from visual comparison of Figures 3–14 (e.g., spectral energy score vs. spectral energy score weighted). No ablation or statistical test isolates the contribution of weighting vs. mechanism, so this claim is suggestive but not rigorously supported.
- The claim that the graph energy score 'does not rely on fixed rectangular patches on a regular grid' and 'can therefore be applied to irregular grids or sparse observation networks' (Discussion) is stated as a property of the method but is not demonstrated experimentally. The paper tests only on a regular O96 Gaussian grid. This is a theoretical advantage, not an empirical finding.
- Section 3.2 states that 'weighting factors are ad hoc and were chosen only to be of the right order of magnitude - estimated from the data - rather than tuned.' This is transparent but raises the question: how sensitive are the spectral results to these choices? No sensitivity analysis is provided, so the robustness of the scale-aware loss conclusions is unclear.
- The abstract claims the results 'suggest that multivariate scores are a viable alternative to CRPS-based training for global machine-learned weather forecasting,' but the experiments use a smaller model (O96 ≈ 1°) and shorter training schedule than operational AIFS-CRPS (which the paper cites as using higher resolution and longer training). The generalizability to full-scale global forecasting is not tested, and this scope limitation should be stated in the abstract or early in the paper, not buried in Section 5.

## Questions
- Can the authors provide quantitative metrics (e.g., spectral slope error, RMS difference from ERA5 spectra, or a summary statistic) for the spectral comparisons in Section 4.2, rather than relying on visual inspection? This would allow readers to assess the magnitude of improvement claimed for scale-aware losses.
- The title uses the word 'sensitivity,' which typically implies a systematic study of how a quantity varies with a parameter. The paper compares three specific scoring rules and finds 'broadly similar' skill. Is the title intended to convey that forecast skill is insensitive to the choice of scoring rule (a null result), or is it meant to highlight differences in tropical regions? A reworded title would clarify the main finding.
- In Section 3.2, the authors state that weighting factors were 'chosen only to be of the right order of magnitude.' How much do the spectral results in Section 4.2 depend on these choices? A brief sensitivity analysis (e.g., varying the scale weights by ±50%) would strengthen confidence in the conclusion that scale-aware losses improve spectral fidelity.
- The paper claims the graph energy score 'can in principle be applied on irregular grids, sparse spatial meshes, or sparse observation networks' (Section 2). Is this a theoretical property, or have the authors tested it? If untested, the claim should be qualified as 'in principle' or 'potentially.'
- Figure 2 shows that the global energy score experiment exhibits 'some degradation' in the tropics relative to CRPS and graph energy. Can the authors provide a hypothesis for why the global energy score underperforms in the tropics? This would help readers understand whether the result is specific to the tropical regime or reflects a general limitation of global multivariate scores.
