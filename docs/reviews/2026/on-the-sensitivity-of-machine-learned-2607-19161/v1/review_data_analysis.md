# Statistics & Data-Analysis Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript presents a preliminary study comparing different probabilistic scoring rules as training objectives for machine-learned weather forecasting models. The statistical analysis is sparse and largely descriptive. The main empirical claims rest on visual inspection of spectral plots and qualitative comparisons of forecast skill across three training objectives, with no formal hypothesis tests, confidence intervals, or quantified effect sizes. Critical statistical details are missing: no sample sizes for key comparisons, no definition of what constitutes 'similar' or 'some degradation' in forecast skill, no correction for multiple comparisons across the twelve loss configurations tested in Section 3.2, and no uncertainty quantification around the spectral estimates. The paper reads as a preliminary technical report rather than a complete statistical analysis, which may be appropriate for its stated scope, but the lack of rigor in quantifying differences and uncertainty substantially limits the strength of the conclusions.

## Strengths
- The experimental design is reasonable: three well-matched experiments in Section 3.1 with architecture and training held constant across loss objectives, allowing fair comparison of the scoring rules themselves.
- The paper is transparent about limitations: authors acknowledge the experiments use 'smaller models, relatively low spatial resolution, and shortened training schedule' and note that 'greater model capacity and longer training may improve performance', which is candid about generalizability.
- The spectral analysis approach (accumulated tendency spectra) is a sensible diagnostic for assessing physical realism across scales, and the use of ERA5 as a reference is appropriate.
- All scores are implemented in a shared framework (Anemoi), supporting reproducibility and comparability.

## Weaknesses
- No formal statistical test or confidence interval is provided for the main claim in Section 4.1 that 'forecast skill broadly similar across experiments'. The statement 'In extratropics, we do not find noticeable difference' is qualitative and unsupported by a hypothesis test, effect size, or CI. What threshold defines 'noticeable'?
- Figure 2 shows CRPS values across regions but provides no error bars, confidence intervals, or sample sizes. The figure caption does not state how many forecast initializations or lead times are included in each panel, making it impossible to assess whether visual differences are within sampling noise.
- Section 4.2 states 'Spectra computed from 2022 forecasts initialized every four days from 1 January to 1 December at 00 UTC, 84 initialization dates in total.' This is the only sample-size statement in the results, and it applies only to the spectral analysis. No n is given for the skill comparison in Section 4.1.
- The claim that 'scale-aware losses improve spectral fidelity' (Discussion) is not quantified. No metric is defined for 'improvement', no statistical test compares spectral shapes across loss configurations, and no correction for multiple comparisons is applied across the twelve experiments in Table 2.
- Figures 3–14 show spectral ratios (forecast/ERA5) but no confidence bands, bootstrap intervals, or uncertainty estimates. With only 84 initialization dates, sampling variability in spectral estimates could be substantial, especially at high wavenumbers, but this is not addressed.
- The paper states 'weighting of different fields, scales, and variables can matter as much as, or more than, specific mechanism used to inject spatial awareness into loss' but provides no quantitative support (e.g., variance decomposition, effect sizes) for this claim.
- No multiple-comparison correction is applied or discussed despite twelve loss configurations being tested in Section 3.2. If visual differences in spectral plots are being used to rank loss configurations, the family-wise error rate or false discovery rate should be controlled.
- The 'ad hoc' weighting factors for small scales (mentioned in Section 3.2: 'These weighting factors ad hoc and chosen only to of right order of magnitude - estimated from data - rather than tuned') introduce an unquantified source of bias. How sensitive are the spectral results to these choices?
- No ablation study or sensitivity analysis is provided to isolate the contribution of different components (e.g., graph connectivity, smoothing kernel widths, spectral truncation) to the observed differences in spectra.

## Questions
- In Section 4.1, what is the sample size (number of forecast initializations, lead times, or grid points) underlying each CRPS value in Figure 2? How are error bars or confidence intervals computed, and why are they not shown?
- For the claim that 'graph energy score experiment appears to perform best' in the tropics (Section 4.1), what is the magnitude of the difference in CRPS between graph energy and CRPS, and is it statistically significant? Please provide a test statistic, p-value, and 95% CI for the difference.
- In Section 4.2, were the twelve loss configurations pre-specified before observing the spectral results, or were they selected post-hoc based on preliminary findings? If post-hoc, a multiple-comparison correction (e.g., Bonferroni or FDR) should be applied to any claims about which configurations perform best.
- How were the 'ad hoc' weighting factors for small scales (Section 3.2) chosen? Please provide the estimated values and a sensitivity analysis showing how spectral results change if these weights are perturbed by ±50%.
- Figures 3–14 show spectral ratios but no confidence intervals or uncertainty bands. With n=84 initializations, what is the approximate 95% CI width for spectral estimates at high wavenumbers (e.g., ℓ=180)? Do the visual differences between loss configurations exceed this uncertainty?
- The paper claims 'making forecast scores scale-aware substantially improves small-scale variability' (Section 4.2). Please quantify 'substantially' with an effect size (e.g., percent change in spectral power at small scales) and a statistical test comparing scale-aware vs. scale-unaware losses.
- In Section 3.1, were the three experiments (CRPS, global energy, graph energy) the only ones planned, or were other loss configurations tested and not reported? If the latter, please disclose the full set of experiments conducted to avoid selective reporting.
