# Statistics & Data-Analysis Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript compares machine-learned weather forecast models trained with different proper scoring rules and scale-aware loss formulations. From a statistics and data-analysis perspective, the work is generally sound: the experimental design is clear, the comparison is fair (same architecture and data across conditions), and the results are presented with appropriate visual diagnostics. However, the statistical reporting lacks rigor in several places. The forecast skill comparison (Section 4.1) presents no formal statistical test, confidence intervals, or sample size justification; differences are described qualitatively ('broadly similar', 'somewhat better') without quantification. The spectral analysis (Section 4.2) similarly lacks error bars, significance tests, or a clear definition of the independent unit of replication (84 initialization dates, but this is not stated as n). The weighting factors for multi-scale experiments are acknowledged as 'ad hoc' and 'not tuned', which is honest but leaves the spectral results vulnerable to confounding by arbitrary hyperparameter choice. For a preliminary study, the work is acceptable, but the claims about performance differences would benefit from formal statistical comparison and explicit uncertainty quantification.

## Strengths
- The experimental design is well-controlled: same architecture, training data, and inference setup across all three main experiments (Section 3.1), enabling fair comparison of scoring rules.
- The manuscript is transparent about limitations: acknowledged as 'preliminary', smaller models and lower resolution than operational systems, shortened training schedule (Section 5), and ad hoc weighting choices (Section 3.2).
- Spectral diagnostics are appropriate for the question: accumulated tendency spectra and ratios to ERA5 are a sensible way to assess whether different loss objectives preserve physical realism across scales.
- The graph energy score formulation is novel and well-motivated: the use of local graph neighbourhoods instead of fixed rectangular patches is flexible and generalizable to irregular grids.
- Multiple loss objectives are tested systematically (12 experiments in Section 3.2), allowing exploration of how scale awareness, multivariate structure, and weighting affect forecast fields.

## Weaknesses
- The forecast skill comparison (Figure 2, Section 4.1) presents no statistical test, confidence intervals, or sample size. The claims 'broadly similar' and 'somewhat better' are qualitative. No n is stated; it is unclear whether differences are computed over grid points, time steps, or initialization dates, and whether these are independent replicates.
- No error bars or uncertainty quantification appear in Figure 2 or Figure 3–14. For spectral plots, it is unclear whether bands represent standard deviation, standard error, confidence intervals, or something else. The number of independent samples (84 initialization dates in 2022) is stated only in passing (Section 4.2) and not used to compute or justify any uncertainty measure.
- The weighting factors for multi-scale and spectral experiments are described as 'ad hoc' and 'chosen only to be of the right order of magnitude' (Section 3.2). This introduces an uncontrolled variable that could confound the comparison between loss objectives. No sensitivity analysis is provided to show results are robust to these choices.
- No multiple-comparison correction is applied. Twelve experiments are compared in Section 3.2 (Table 2), and statements like 'edge-CRPS experiment and spectral magnitude CRPS experiment seem to be slightly more successful' (Section 4.2) are made without adjustment for multiple testing or a pre-registered hypothesis.
- The definition of 'accumulated tendency' spectra is stated only once in passing ('differences between the state at a given lead time and the initial state', Section 4.2) and not formally defined. It is unclear whether this is computed per variable, per ensemble member, or averaged; whether it is differenced in space or time; or how the spectral transform is applied.
- Figure 2 uses a single verification metric (fair CRPS at 1.5° resolution) without justification for this choice. The text states 'conclusions are also consistent when the fair energy score or fair graph energy score is used for verification' but provides no figure or table to support this claim.
- The training schedule differs between Section 3.1 (150k + 30k + 1k per rollout) and Section 3.2 (150k one-step + 1k per rollout up to 8 steps), but it is not stated whether this difference could affect the comparison. No ablation or sensitivity analysis justifies these choices.

## Questions
- In Figure 2, what is n? Are differences computed over grid points, time steps, initialization dates, or ensemble members? Which of these are independent replicates? Are error bars or confidence intervals available, and if so, what do they represent?
- For the spectral plots (Figures 3–14), what is the definition of the error band or shaded region? Is it standard deviation, standard error, 95% confidence interval, or something else? Over how many samples (initialization dates, ensemble members, grid points) is it computed?
- The manuscript states 'conclusions are also consistent when the fair energy score or fair graph energy score is used for verification instead of the fair CRPS' (Section 4.1). Can the authors provide a table or figure showing these alternative verification metrics for Figure 2?
- In Section 3.2, the weighting factors for multi-scale and spectral experiments are 'ad hoc' and 'not tuned'. How sensitive are the spectral results (Figures 3–14) to these choices? A sensitivity analysis varying the weights by ±50% would strengthen the claim that scale awareness, not arbitrary weighting, drives the improvement.
- The manuscript compares 12 loss configurations in Section 3.2 without a multiple-comparison correction. Were these comparisons pre-registered, or should a correction (e.g., Bonferroni or FDR) be applied to statements like 'edge-CRPS experiment and spectral magnitude CRPS experiment seem to be slightly more successful'?
- How are the accumulated tendency spectra computed? Specifically: (i) are they computed per variable, per ensemble member, or averaged over ensemble members? (ii) is the spectral transform applied to the full field or to differences? (iii) are spectra then averaged over the 84 initialization dates, and if so, is the variance across dates reported?
- The training schedule differs between the two experimental setups (Section 3.1 vs. 3.2). Could this difference affect the comparison? An ablation showing that the choice of training schedule does not confound the results would be helpful.
- In Section 4.2, the statement 'achieving realistic spectra for geopotential appears more challenging' is qualitative. Can the authors quantify this, e.g., by computing a metric of spectral realism (e.g., integrated squared error relative to ERA5) and testing whether differences are statistically significant?
