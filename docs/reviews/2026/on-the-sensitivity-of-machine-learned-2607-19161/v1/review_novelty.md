# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript presents a preliminary empirical study comparing scoring rules and scale-aware loss formulations for machine-learned probabilistic weather forecasts. The core contribution is a systematic comparison of AIFS-CRPS variants trained with univariate versus multivariate scoring rules (almost-fair CRPS, fair global energy score, fair graph energy score) and assessment of scale-awareness mechanisms (multi-scale decomposition, spectral losses). The novelty is real but incremental: graph energy scores applied to weather forecasting are new, as are graph variogram and graph edge energy score variants, and the systematic comparison is useful. However, core methodological components (multi-scale losses, spectral losses, energy scores, variogram scores) are established in prior work. The contribution is primarily empirical—showing multivariate scores can match CRPS performance and scale-awareness matters—rather than methodologically novel. The work is appropriately framed as preliminary and transparent about limitations, fitting In Silico's scope. However, three hard issues require clarification: (1) the propriety claim for the combined graph energy score lacks formal justification, (2) spectral weighting choices are ad hoc and substantially affect results without detailed justification, and (3) hyperparameter reporting is incomplete for reproducibility. These are fixable without major restructuring.

## Strengths
- Honest framing as preliminary work with explicit acknowledgment of limitations (smaller models, low resolution, shortened training schedule), appropriately calibrating expectations and consistent with In Silico scope.
- Systematic controlled comparison of three scoring rules (almost-fair CRPS, fair global energy score, fair graph energy score) holding architecture and training constant, providing useful empirical evidence.
- Introduction of graph-based localization for multivariate scores (graph energy score, graph variogram score, graph edge energy score) as novel applications to weather forecasting, with graph-based approach motivated as more flexible than fixed rectangular patches.
- Dual investigation of both forecast skill (Section 4.1) and spectral fidelity (Section 4.2) providing complementary diagnostics, with finding that scale-awareness improves spectral realism.
- Reproducibility infrastructure with all scores implemented in Anemoi framework and sufficient detail on architecture, training schedules, and hyperparameters provided in Section 3.
- Explicit acknowledgment of limitations and transparent discussion of how findings may change at higher resolution or with larger models.

## Weaknesses
- Graph energy score propriety claim is understated and unverified: the manuscript states the graph energy score 'may fail to be strictly proper' and claims combining it with a weak global anchor (0.1 fES) recovers strict propriety, but provides no formal proof or citation of a theorem establishing this, making a core claim about score validity rest on unverified assertion.
- Spectral weighting choices are ad hoc and substantially affect results without justification: Section 3.2 explicitly states weighting factors are 'ad hoc and chosen only to be of the right order of magnitude' yet Figures 3-14 show these weights matter substantially, undermining the main result that scale-awareness improves spectra.
- Incomplete hyperparameter reporting prevents reproducibility: Table 2 does not specify the weighting coefficient for global energy score anchor in all experiments, exact smoothing kernel widths are given as 'approximately' rather than precisely, and exact scale weights ζ_i in multi-scale loss are not reported.
- Novelty positioning could be sharper: the manuscript does not clearly distinguish which graph-based scores are entirely new versus adaptations of existing approaches (e.g., graph energy score as localization of standard energy score using graph neighborhoods versus rectangular patches).
- Missing direct comparison to patched energy scores from reference [19] (Pacchiardi et al., 2024), which introduced patched energy scores and found them to perform best among multivariate scores in weather forecasting, limiting evidence that graph-based localization is a viable alternative.
- Tropical performance difference is reported but not explained: Section 4.1 notes graph energy score performs better and global energy score degrades in tropics but provides no mechanistic insight into why or what the graph energy score captures that global energy score misses.
- Scale-awareness mechanism comparison is incomplete: multi-scale decomposition and spectral losses are compared separately but not directly against each other, with no quantitative assessment of which mechanism is more effective or under what conditions.
- Limited experimental scope with O96 (~1°) resolution coarse by modern standards, 8-member ensemble small, and shortened training schedule, with no testing of whether findings generalize to higher resolution or larger ensembles.

## Questions
- Can you provide a formal proof or cite a theorem showing that fGESgraph + 0.1 fES is strictly proper? If not, what is the justification for using this combination, and should the manuscript state that strict propriety is not guaranteed?
- What are the exact scale weights ζ_i used in the multi-scale experiments, and the exact per-waveband weights used in spectral experiments? Can you justify these choices a priori rather than post hoc?
- Is the 0.1 coefficient for the global energy score anchor used in the 'Graph energy' experiment (Section 3.1) also applied in the multi-scale graph energy and multi-scale graph edge energy experiments (Table 2)? If not, what values are used?
- What are the exact kernel widths in grid points or kilometers for the Gaussian smoothing operators, rather than 'approximately 100, 200, 400, and 800 km'?
- Why was a direct comparison to the patched energy scores of reference [19] not included? Would the graph-based approach outperform or underperform rectangular patches?
- What causes the global energy score to degrade in the tropics relative to CRPS and graph energy? Is this a known limitation of global energy scores or specific to your setup?
- Do you expect the relative performance of multivariate scores to change at higher resolution (e.g., O320 or higher), given that maintaining spatial coherence is particularly challenging at high resolution?
