# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents a plausible and interesting case that the ESCRT-0 protein Hrs localises to synapses, is bidirectionally regulated by neuronal activity, and influences AMPAR currents and CaMKII phosphorylation. The central claim — that Hrs 'regulates' AMPAR currents and Ca2+-dependent signalling — is moderately supported but weakened by three gaps: the direction of the AMPAR change (faster kinetics and reduced AMPA/NMDA ratio in Hrs KD) is reported as a loss-of-function effect only, without the complementary overexpression electrophysiology to establish bidirectionality; the Hrs-KD effect on AMPA/NMDA ratio is modest and the fast/slow AMPAR current classification is not fully explained; and the phosphoproteomics hits are reported at an uncorrected p<0.05 threshold with no multiple-testing adjustment, which risks over-interpreting the 16 nominally significant sites out of 924. The behavioural or in vivo functional significance of the observed changes is not established, and several claims in the abstract outrun the evidence. For these reasons I recommend major revision.

## Strengths
- The activity-dependent regulation of Hrs protein levels (Fig. 2A–D) is demonstrated with bidirectional pharmacological manipulation (Bic and TTX), providing both an increase and a decrease, which is a clean design.
- The phosphoproteomics dataset (Fig. 4) is a hypothesis-generating resource that will be useful to the field if properly deposited and corrected for multiple testing.
- The use of multiple complementary methods — biochemistry, confocal and STED imaging, electrophysiology, and proteomics — permits cross-validation of the synaptic localization of Hrs.

## Weaknesses
- LOAD-BEARING 1 — Causal direction and bidirectionality of the Hrs–AMPAR relationship. The title and abstract state that ESCRT-0 'regulates AMPA receptor currents' and 'impacts' AMPAR function. The electrophysiological evidence is confined to Hrs knockdown (Figs. 3B–L), which shows faster rise time, steeper decay slope, and a reduced AMPA/NMDA ratio. This is a single perturbation; a true regulatory role implies that increasing Hrs would produce the opposite effect. The overexpression experiments (Fig. 7) do measure AMPAR phosphorylation (pGluA1-S831) but do not include any electrophysiological measurement — the strongest claim about 'regulation' is in the dimension (current kinetics) where the complementary experiment is absent. Without the overexpression electrophysiology, the claim that Hrs 'regulates' AMPAR currents is over-strong: the data establish that knocking Hrs down alters AMPAR kinetics, not that Hrs is a bidirectional regulator of those currents. The word 'modulates' in the discussion and 'bidirectional' in the abstract should be restricted to the Hrs-protein-level data (Fig. 2), not extended to the functional consequences. To support the claim fully, the authors would need to record AMPAR EPSCs from Hrs-overexpressing CA1 neurons and show that rise time and AMPA/NMDA ratio shift in the opposite direction from the KD. Alternatively, the claim should be requalified to 'Hrs knockdown alters AMPAR kinetics'.
- LOAD-BEARING 2 — The fast vs slow AMPAR current classification is insufficiently explained and could drive the measured kinetic differences. The authors classify AMPAR EPSCs as 'slow' when >10% of sweeps show multiple peaks and decay >25 ms (Fig. 3B–D). The proportion of slow cells drops from 83% (38/46) in scrambled to 51% (21/41) in Hrs KD, and this is the basis for the reported effects on rise time and decay slope. However, the classification boundary (10% of sweeps) is arbitrary, and the authors do not report whether the 38 slow cells in scrambled and the 21 slow cells in Hrs KD have similar rise/decay parameters when considered only among themselves. If the Hrs KD group simply has fewer slow-classified cells (which tend to have longer rise times), the overall mean shift could be an artifact of reclassification rather than a true change in channel kinetics. The authors should report the kinetics in the fast-only subset of each genotype, and should show that the kinetic differences survive within the slow-only category. If they do not, the claim about 'faster AMPAR current kinetics' (abstract) is primarily a claim about prevalence of a multi-peak waveform, not about single-channel or unitary-synaptic behaviour. This needs clarification.
- LOAD-BEARING 3 — The phosphoproteomics (Fig. 4) is presented as evidence that Hrs depletion 'disrupts glutamatergic signaling at the post-synaptic membrane', but the analysis uses an uncorrected p<0.05 threshold on 924 phosphopeptides. At this threshold one expects ~46 false positives by chance; the authors report 16 hits, which is fewer than the expected false-positive count. Enrichment analyses (GO, KEGG) based on 16 hits are therefore fragile — the pathway hits (GABAergic and glutamatergic synapses, tight junctions) each depend on a small number of phosphopeptides (one or two per pathway) that may not survive correction. The paper does not report any multiple-testing correction (Bonferroni, Benjamini-Hochberg). Until the authors show which hits survive FDR correction, the phosphoproteomics should be described as 'candidates that did not reach corrected significance' rather than as evidence of pathway disruption. The claim that Hrs depletion 'disrupts glutamatergic signaling' should be softened if the phosphoproteomics pillar of that claim collapses.
- SOFT — Causal language for correlational data. The abstract says Hrs 'regulates AMPA receptor currents' and 'drives' an LTD-like state. The LTD-like interpretation (reduced pCaMKII, reduced PSD-95, reduced gephyrin) is based on correlational protein-level changes in whole cortical lysates from Hrs KO mice. The authors do not demonstrate that these changes are physiologically reversible, nor that they are accompanied by the expected functional hallmarks of LTD (e.g., a measured change in synaptic efficacy in the slice from Hrs KO mice). The LTD language should be hedged to 'consistent with an LTD-like depressive state' throughout.
- SOFT — Generalisation from organotypic slices. All electrophysiology was performed in organotypic hippocampal slices 5–12 days after AAV injection. This preparation has altered connectivity and glial reactivity relative to acute slices. The authors acknowledge this implicitly by using slices for shRNA delivery, but the abstract does not qualify 'CA1 pyramidal neurons' with 'in organotypic slice culture'. Given that the rise-time and AMPA/NMDA ratio effects are modest, replication in acute slices from Hrs KO mice would substantially strengthen the generalisability.

## Questions
- What proportion of the Hrs/Bassoon and Hrs/PSD-95 overlap in the STED data (Fig. 1L–M) is accounted for by the diffraction-limited resolution of the Bassoon and PSD-95 channels relative to the STED-resolution Hrs channel? The text reports 89–91% overlap, but this could be inflated if those channels were acquired at confocal rather than STED resolution — please clarify the acquisition mode for each channel and, if applicable, provide a cross-channel resolution control.
- In Fig. 3I–L, the rise-time and decay-slope differences between scrambled and Hrs KD are nominally significant but visually small. Please report the mean and SD for each group, not only the p-value, and specify the effect size (e.g., Cohen's d or a ratio) so a reader can judge biological magnitude.
- The abstract and discussion state that Hrs 'senses' neuronal activity. The experiments show that Bic increases, and TTX decreases, Hrs protein levels (Fig. 2). Does this bidirectional change require 72 h of treatment? If so, the claim that Hrs 'senses' activity should be qualified as a slow transcriptional or translational response, not a rapid sensor like Ca2+ influx — please clarify the timescale on which the Hrs protein change first becomes detectable.
- Fig. 3M–N: the basal surface GluA1 comparison (YFP vs Cre, no Bic) is a control for Cre toxicity. If these two groups differ even under basal conditions, the Bic interaction could reflect a baseline shift rather than a specific activity-dependent defect. Please report the raw (non-normalized) surface GluA1 intensities for all four groups, not only the normalized bar chart.
- The phosphoproteomics (Fig. 4) compares Cre+ vs Cre- PSM fractions. Which of the 16 differentially phosphorylated peptides survive a correction for multiple testing (e.g., Benjamini-Hochberg)? The volcano-plot threshold of p<0.05 uncorrected is permissive for a 924-peptide discovery set. Please report both the raw and adjusted p-values for the 16 hits.
