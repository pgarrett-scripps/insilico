# Clarity & Presentation Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript is clearly structured and the central claims are explicitly stated. The major presentation issues are HARD: several figure legends and text descriptions do not define all symbols, abbreviations, or color codings used in the figures (e.g., Figure 3E—what are the shaded areas? Figure 4B—what do the row colors represent?); a few pronoun references are ambiguous in key claim sentences; and the Methods section has a missing step (the order between hypoosmotic lysis and Triton X-100 extraction in the PSM protocol is unrecoverable from the text). These are specific and fixable. The SOFT issues (redundant figure references, overlong figure legends) are minor. The contribution is presented accessibly to a reader in adjacent fields, and the quantitative reporting is disciplined.

## Strengths
- The manuscript is generally well structured, with a clear narrative from Hrs localization through activity-dependent regulation to functional consequences.
- Each figure is explicitly referenced in the text and the figure legends provide sufficient detail to interpret the panels independently.
- The authors consistently state quantitative values (fold changes, p-values, exact Ns) in their results and figure legends, avoiding vague directional claims.

## Weaknesses
- [LOAD-BEARING] The central claim (title, opening, and end of the Introduction) is that Hrs 'regulates AMPA receptor currents and Ca2+-dependent signaling' at the post-synapse. The primary evidence is in Figure 3: Hrs knockdown alters AMPAR kinetics and reduces the AMPA/NMDA ratio. The text reports two distinct AMPAR current types ('fast' and 'slow') and finds Hrs KD shifts the population toward fast currents and reduces the ratio. However, the classification of 'fast' vs 'slow' currents is defined in the Methods as a binary threshold based on decay properties, yet none of the electrophysiology results panels (Figure 3D, 3E, 3I–L) report how many of the excluded sweeps per neuron contributed to this classification, nor is there any control for whether the slow currents arise from polysynaptic activation (which an Hrs-dependent change in circuit connectivity could produce) rather than from altered single-synapse AMPAR kinetics. A reader cannot distinguish between 'Hrs changes AMPAR subunit composition or trafficking at the synapse' (the authors' interpretation) and 'Hrs changes local circuit connectivity and therefore the evoked EPSC waveform.' The key distinguishing analysis — paired-pulse ratio, miniature EPSC frequency/amplitude, or input-output curves from the same recordings — is absent from the manuscript. Without that control, the claim that Hrs regulates AMPAR currents at the single-synapse level is stronger than the evidence supports. I flag this as a presentation and interpretive framing issue because the text presents only the synaptic interpretation; the alternative is not discussed. (HARD — the conclusion as stated may not follow from the data; at minimum the alternative should be acknowledged.)
- [LOAD-BEARING] The second major claim is that Hrs expression is bidirectionally regulated by neuronal activity (Figure 2A–B). This is supported by immunoblot quantification of Hrs in cortical neurons treated with bicuculline or TTX for 72 h. The text reports a 34% increase and 31% decrease. However, the western blots show a single band at the expected Hrs molecular weight, but there is no loading control figure panel shown for the 8 independent experiments — the Methods and figure legend state α-tubulin was used, but Figure 2A does not include a representative α-tubulin blot in the figure itself (it is described in the legend but not shown in the panel). If the α-tubulin loading control is embedded within the same blot and simply unlabeled, the reader cannot verify that from the figure. This is a HARD transparency issue: a reader cannot determine whether the reported normalizations are valid from the figure as published.
- Figure 3E: The trace is described as 'the averaged normalized AMPAR EPSCs' but the shaded areas around the lines are not defined anywhere in the legend. A reader cannot determine whether these represent SEM, SD, confidence intervals, or individual sweeps.
- Figure 4A (volcano plot): The axes are labeled but the dashed horizontal line is described as 'the significance threshold at –log10 (0.05)' — this is not informative about what multiple-testing correction (if any) was applied. With 924 phosphopeptides tested, uncorrected p < 0.05 would yield ~46 false positives under the null, while the paper finds 16 differentially abundant. The text does not state whether any multiple-testing correction was applied, leaving a competent reader unable to interpret the significance of these findings.
- Phosphoproteomics Methods: the description of the PSM purification (lines 633–648) jumps between steps in a way that makes the order of operations unrecoverable. The text says 'subjected to hypoosmotic shock... followed by solubilization in Buffer F (0.32 M sucrose, 1% Triton X-100).' But it is unclear whether the Triton X-100 solubilization step precedes or follows the first centrifugation after the shock, and whether the 82,000 × g spin reported is the first or second spin. This is HARD: a reader attempting to reproduce the fraction would not know the sequence.
- Figure 1A–B: The blot is said to quantify 'Hrs in S3 relative to S1' and 'Hrs in PSD2 relative to S1,' but the text reports these as ratios (1.42, 0.33) without stating whether these are normalized to total protein loaded, to a housekeeping protein, or to something else. The Methods section on fractionation (lines 436–460) says 'Equal amounts of protein from each fraction were analyzed,' which would be the standard loading control, but the reader cannot tell whether the ratios reported are based on equal protein loading across fractions (which differ greatly in composition) or adjusted for the starting material. The statement that S3/S1 is >1 for Hrs is surprising if both are normalized to equal total protein, and the reader has no information to resolve this.
- Abstract: The final sentence of the abstract states that 'Hrs overexpression led to increased... AMPAR subunit GluA1-S831, a site which increases conductance.' This is a substantive claim about AMPAR function, but the functional consequence of increased pGluA1-S831 is asserted from the literature rather than demonstrated by these experiments. The manuscript does not include any electrophysiological measurements of conductance under Hrs overexpression. This should be caveated as an inference from an established phosphorylation–conductance relationship, not presented as a finding of the present study. (SOFT — does not block understanding but could mislead a casual reader.)
- Figure 1N–O reports cluster volume and object density for Bassoon, Hrs, and PSD95 from STED data. The statistical comparisons appear to use one-way ANOVA, but the number of independent preparations (3) is mentioned only in the legend and the analysis treats individual images (10 per culture) as independent units. The text does not state whether clustering by culture was accounted for in the statistical model. (SOFT — a reader can infer the issue but the authors should clarify.)
