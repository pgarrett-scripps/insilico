# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This is a well-executed structural and immunological study of EtpA, an ETEC adhesin, with high-resolution cryo-EM structures, comprehensive glycosylation mapping, and functional validation. The core claims—that C-terminal repeats direct blood group A binding and that extensive N-linked glycosylation is a feature of the protein—are solidly supported by the data presented. The main weakness is overclaiming in the abstract and discussion about immune evasion and vaccine design implications that exceed what the experiments demonstrate. The work is technically sound and makes a genuine contribution to understanding ETEC pathogenesis, but some headline statements require hedging.

## Strengths
- The cryo-EM structures at 3.3–4 Å resolution are of high quality and directly visualize epitope-paratope interactions and glycan positioning, providing atomic-level detail for the claims about antibody binding.

## Weaknesses
- 
**Load-bearing claim 1: "EtpC is a promiscuous low-fidelity N-linked glycosyltransferase" and glycosylation heterogeneity reflects immune evasion.** The MS data show extensive and heterogeneous glycosylation (61 glycans per molecule on average, with occupancy ranging from 0–100% across 133 sites), and the authors characterize EtpC by structural homology to HMW1C and note the lack of apparent sequon selectivity. However, 'low-fidelity' is inferred from the heterogeneity itself, not from direct biochemical assays of EtpC specificity, kinetics, or substrate preference. The claim that this heterogeneity is "exploited as a mechanism of immune evasion" (line 393–397) is speculative: the data show that mAbs 1C08 and 1G05 target epitopes containing single glycans with high occupancy (N349 ~96%, N849 in top 17%), which is consistent with either affinity maturation toward conserved sites *or* immune evasion through glycan shielding. The authors acknowledge this ambiguity ("could be taken as evidence that antibodies targeting PNGS with higher occupancy are enriched during affinity maturation, or conversely, that heterogeneity in glycosylation is being exploited as a mechanism of immune evasion") but then assert the evasion hypothesis in the discussion without additional evidence. A direct test—e.g., comparing antibody responses to wild-type vs. glycosylation-deficient EtpA, or measuring whether glycan heterogeneity reduces neutralization breadth—would be needed to support the evasion claim. As written, the discussion overstates the mechanistic conclusion.

**Load-bearing claim 2: "Antibodies targeting the C-terminal repeat region can effectively inhibit interactions with the host" and this region is "crucial for ETEC pathogen-host interaction."** The evidence is strong for the first part: mAbs 1G05 (CTR-binding) and 1F09 (from human volunteers, CTR-binding) both inhibit EtpA-bgA binding and ETEC adhesion (Fig. 1E–G, 1I), while NTS-binding mAbs (1C08, human mAbs) do not. The truncation experiment (EtpA1-1086 lacking full repeats) shows loss of bgA binding. However, the claim that the CTR is "crucial for ETEC pathogen-host interaction" is broader than the evidence supports: the experiments show the CTR is necessary for *blood group A binding specifically*, but EtpA also binds mucins (MUC2) and other glycans (line 101–102). The manuscript does not test whether the CTR is required for mucin binding or whether NTS-binding antibodies might block other EtpA functions (e.g., flagella interaction, mentioned in line 48). The claim should be narrowed to "crucial for blood group A glycan recognition" or the mucin-binding data should be included in the functional assays.

**Load-bearing claim 3: "Polyclonal antibodies target a variety of epitopes on both the CTR and NTS domains" with "5–6 and 3–4 unique epitopes" respectively, suggesting "both neutralizing and decoy epitopes."** The EMPEM analysis (Fig. 5, Supplemental Fig. 10) identifies distinct Fab-binding positions by visual inspection of 2D class averages. The authors state they "confidently identify antibodies bound at either the N- or C-terminal domains and estimate the total number of unique epitopes on each." However, the resolution and specificity of negative-stain EM are substantially lower than cryo-EM, and the epitope count is an estimate based on visual clustering of Fab density in 2D classes, not a rigorous quantification. The claim that some epitopes are "neutralizing" and others "decoy" is not directly tested: the polyclonal sera were not functionally assayed for their ability to inhibit bgA binding or bacterial adhesion. The authors infer the neutralizing/decoy distinction from the location of epitopes (CTR vs. NTS) based on the mAb data, but this is indirect. The statement should be hedged to "suggests the presence of multiple epitopes, some of which may correspond to neutralizing sites based on the CTR-binding mAbs" or functional assays of the polyclonal sera should be added.

- The abstract states that "mAbs directly engage these unique glycan containing epitopes" (line 62–63), but only 2 of the 7 mAbs isolated are structurally characterized (1C08 and 1G05), and only these two are shown to contact glycans; the other 5 mAbs are mentioned only in the context of human volunteer-derived antibodies (line 1H–I) without structural data, so the claim overgeneralizes from the two characterized mAbs to the broader set.
- The title claims EtpA "repeat modules and N-linked glycans define structure and antigenicity," but "define" is too strong: the repeats define the overall elongated β-helix fold (shared with other TpsA proteins), and glycans are a surface feature that modulates antibody binding; neither "defines" the structure in the sense of determining the core architecture, which is conserved across the TpsA family.
- The claim that "EtpA vaccination generates responses against neutralizing and decoy regions" (line 68–69) is presented as a conclusion from the EMPEM data, but EMPEM alone cannot distinguish neutralizing from decoy epitopes without functional validation of the polyclonal sera.
- The discussion states that "the EtpA adhesin may have evolved with its own low-fidelity glycosyltransferase under selective pressure to shield important and highly conserved functional regions" (line 393–397), but this is evolutionary speculation not supported by comparative genomics, phylogenetic analysis, or functional data showing that glycosylation shields functional sites from antibodies more effectively than alternative mechanisms.
- The claim that "native full-length EtpA from ETEC strain H10407" exhibits "close agreement" with rEtpA glycosylation (line 305–307) is based on mapping only 48 of 166 potential N-glycosylation sites due to "low peptide detection," which limits the strength of the validation; the authors should quantify the agreement more precisely or acknowledge the limitation more prominently in the main text.
