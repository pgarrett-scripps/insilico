# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript makes genuine and significant contributions to understanding EtpA structure, glycosylation, and antigenicity through novel cryo-EM structures (3.3–4.0 Å resolution), comprehensive mass-spectrometry-based glycosylation mapping of 133 sites with quantified occupancy, and functional data linking the C-terminal repeat domain to blood group A binding and antibody-mediated protection. The work will be valuable for ETEC vaccine design and bacterial lectin–glycan interactions broadly. However, three load-bearing claims are either speculative or incompletely supported: (1) immune evasion via glycosylation heterogeneity is not demonstrated mechanistically—the data show glycans are present at epitopes and affect antibody binding but do not prove heterogeneity impairs protective immunity or is under selection for evasion; (2) the CTR's role in blood group A binding is established functionally but lacks mechanistic detail (no direct visualization of bgA binding, no mutagenesis of putative binding residues); and (3) glycosylation's requirement for folding and secretion is inferred from structural data but not experimentally tested. These are not fatal flaws—the core structural and glycosylation findings are robust—but the immune evasion hypothesis in particular overstates the evidence and should be reframed as speculative.

## Strengths
- The cryo-EM structures of EtpA–Fab complexes at 3.3–4.0 Å resolution provide the first complete atomic model of full-length EtpA and directly visualize how protective (1G05) and non-protective (1C08) antibodies engage distinct domains, enabling structure-guided identification of neutralizing epitopes.
- Site-specific mass-spectrometry analysis of 166 asparagine residues identifies 133 glycosylation sites with quantified occupancy, revealing ~61 glycans per molecule (~4% of residues) with unprecedented density and heterogeneity for bacterial adhesins, directly validated against native EtpA from H10407.
- The integration of cryo-EM, MS, monoclonal antibody binding, and negative-stain polyclonal epitope mapping provides complementary evidence that the C-terminal repeat domain is essential for blood group A binding and that antibodies targeting this region inhibit both EtpA–glycan interactions and bacterial adhesion.
- Structural evidence for glycan–glycan and glycan–amino acid interactions (556 Å of buried glycan surface area, 63 hydrogen bonds) supports a role for glycosylation in protein stability.

## Weaknesses
- The immune evasion claim (lines 390–401) rests on three observations—sequence conservation, glycans at epitopes, and heterogeneous occupancy—but does not establish that glycosylation heterogeneity causes immune evasion; the data show glycans contribute to antibody binding affinity but not that heterogeneity shields conserved regions or impairs polyclonal responses.
- The alternative explanation that glycans stabilize protein fold and are retained for functional reasons (secretion, folding) rather than immune evasion is equally consistent with the data, yet the manuscript does not demonstrate that glycosylation heterogeneity reduces breadth or potency of polyclonal responses or that variants with altered glycosylation escape antibody recognition.
- The truncation experiment (EtpA1-1086) does not isolate the CTR's contribution: failure to bind blood group A could result from global misfolding, loss of avidity from missing repeat modules, or combination thereof; the manuscript does not quantify whether the CTR alone binds bgA in isolation.
- The structural basis for blood group A recognition is not explicitly identified—the manuscript states bgA "must interact directly with the β-helix" (line 220) but provides no direct evidence such as docking, mutagenesis of putative binding residues, or structural visualization of bgA bound to EtpA.
- Glycosylation's requirement for proper folding and secretion is inferred from structural data (glycan–glycan interactions) and cited from prior work but not experimentally tested in this manuscript; no glycosylation-deficient mutants (N-to-Q mutations or EtpC knockout) are expressed and analyzed for yield, solubility, or secondary structure.
- Polyclonal epitope mapping by negative-stain EM identifies 5–6 epitopes on NTS and 3–4 on CTR (line 343) based on visual inspection of 2D class averages without quantitative clustering analysis or statistical validation of epitope distinctness.
- Native EtpA glycosylation validation (Supplemental Fig. 7) shows substantially lower peptide coverage (48 of 166 PNGS) than recombinant protein, limiting confidence in the comparison.
- Enrichment analysis for aromatic residues around PNGS (Supplemental Fig. 6A–C) shows no statistical enrichment, contradicting the common mechanism of glycan stabilization; the manuscript does not discuss why EtpA may differ from other glycoproteins.
- The work focuses on a single ETEC strain (H10407) and does not address whether glycosylation profile, epitope landscape, or protective antibody responses vary across geographically diverse ETEC strains important for vaccine development.

## Questions
- Does the CTR domain alone, in the absence of the NTS, bind blood group A glycans, or is the NTS required structurally for CTR function?
- What is the molecular basis for blood group A recognition by the CTR—are specific residues or the glycan coat itself responsible for GalNAc binding?
- Have glycosylation-deficient EtpA mutants (e.g., N-to-Q at multiple sites or etpC knockout) been expressed and tested for yield, folding, and secretion to directly test the stabilization hypothesis?
- Does the heterogeneity in glycosylation occupancy across sites correlate with escape from polyclonal antibody recognition, or is it simply a consequence of the low-fidelity glycosyltransferase?
- How does glycosylation and epitope distribution vary across ETEC strains from different geographic origins, and does this affect vaccine efficacy predictions?
- The cryo-EM structure shows that 1G05 can bind up to 3 Fabs per EtpA molecule (line 310)—does this reflect multivalent avidity effects, and how does this influence the interpretation of the CTR as the primary functional domain?
