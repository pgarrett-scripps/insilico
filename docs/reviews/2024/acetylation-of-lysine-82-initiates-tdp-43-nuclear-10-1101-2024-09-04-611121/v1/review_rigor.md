## Load-bearing weaknesses

**1. The K82-acetylation antibody is validated only against synthetic peptides, not against a biological negative control.** The three polyclonal antibodies are tested by ELISA against the acetylated versus unacetylated peptide (Fig. 5A) and by immunoblot of BTZ-treated neurons (Fig. S5). What is missing is a specificity control in the actual assay system: does the antibody signal disappear when TDP-43 is knocked down, or when the K82 residue is mutated to a non-acetylatable arginine (K82R) in cells? The BTZ-treated neuron blot (Fig. S5) shows increased signal, but this could reflect increased total TDP-43 in the insoluble fraction or cross-reactivity with another acetylated protein that accumulates under proteasome inhibition. The sALS versus control difference (Fig. 5B) is the paper’s translational claim, and it rests entirely on this antibody. Without a K82R or knockdown control, the possibility that the antibody recognizes a co-migrating acetylated species — or that the signal tracks total TDP-43 load rather than K82 acetylation specifically — is not excluded. The authors should show the same blot probed with the antibody after TDP-43 immunodepletion, or on lysates from cells expressing K82R TDP-43.

**2. The claim that proteasome inhibition “induces” K82 acetylation is not supported by the mass spectrometry data as presented.** The PTM analysis (Fig. 3A–B) identifies acetylation/ubiquitination at K79, K82, K84 and phosphorylation at S91/S92 in TDP-43 purified from BTZ-treated cells, but the text does not report whether these modifications are absent or reduced in untreated cells. The scheme (Fig. 3A) shows both conditions were analyzed, yet no quantitative comparison of modification occupancy between BTZ and control is given. Without this, the data show that these modifications exist under proteasome inhibition, not that proteasome inhibition causes them. The causal claim in the Results (“reduced proteasome activity induces acetylation…”) outruns the evidence. The authors should report the relative abundance of each modified peptide in treated versus untreated samples — this is a standard label-free or TMT comparison and should be straightforward from the data they already have.

**3. The “initiates” language in the title and abstract implies a temporal sequence in disease that the human data cannot establish.** The sALS samples show increased K82 acetylation (Fig. 5B), and the authors argue it is “an earlier event than phosphorylation” because ac-K82 appears in the soluble fraction while phospho-TDP-43 is only in the insoluble fraction (Fig. 5C). But this is a single sALS case, and soluble-versus-insoluble fractionation is not a temporal marker — a modification can be present in the soluble pool and still occur late in the pathogenic cascade. The in vitro data show K82 acetylation is sufficient to drive mislocalization, but sufficiency in a cell model is not evidence that acetylation initiates the human disease. The title claim should be hedged to “can initiate” or “is sufficient to initiate,” which the body largely does but the headline does not.

## Sweep

- The claim that TDP-43-PY-NLS “prevented” mislocalization of co-expressed cNLS-containing TDP-43 (Fig. 2G–I) is presented as rescue through oligomerization, but the alternative — that the PY-NLS construct simply dilutes the pool of importin-α1-competent TDP-43 or that the mRuby signal is dominated by the Clover-PY-NLS species through FRET or spectral bleed-through — is not excluded; the co-IP in Fig. 2F does not directly test heterodimer formation.
- The statement that “TDP-43 was the protein whose nuclear localization was the most sensitive” (Fig. 1E) is based on a single time point (24 h) and a single inhibitor dose; the claim would be stronger if the ranking were stable across doses or time points.
- The K82R single-point mutant causing mislocalization (Fig. 4D–E) is used to argue that K82 is “critical,” but the authors do not report whether K82R also disrupts importin-α1 binding in the co-IP (Fig. 4C shows 6KR and K82R, but the quantification is not shown for the single mutant); the binding data for K82R specifically would strengthen the mechanistic link.
- The claim that “acetylation at K82 alone was sufficient to drive complete TDP-43 nuclear clearance” (Results) is based on the K82Q mimic; the authors should acknowledge that glutamine is an imperfect mimic of acetylation (it does not carry the same charge distribution) and that the peptide binding assay (Fig. 3F) is the more direct evidence.
- The stathmin-2 cryptic splicing result (Fig. 1G) is presented as evidence of TDP-43 loss of function, but the authors do not report whether the effect size is comparable to that seen with TDP-43 knockdown — a quantitative comparison would calibrate how much dysfunction the observed mislocalization actually produces.
- The statement that “proteasome activity has been claimed to decline during aging” (Results) is appropriately hedged, but the mouse data (Fig. S1A) show a decline by 12 months that is not clearly progressive — the authors should not imply a monotonic age-dependent decline from these data.
- The claim that “acetylation initiates and drives TDP-43 proteinopathy” (Discussion) exceeds the evidence, which shows acetylation is sufficient to disrupt import in vitro and is present in sALS tissue; the Discussion should distinguish “initiates” from “contributes to” given the cross-sectional human data.
- The antibody validation in Fig. 5A shows specificity against peptides, but the authors do not report whether the antibody recognizes full-length TDP-43 acetylated at K82 by an orthogonal method (e.g., recombinant acetylated protein or in vitro acetylation reaction) — this is a gap that a reader cannot fill from the text.

## Questions

1. Can you show the mass spectrometry quantification of K82 acetylation occupancy in BTZ-treated versus untreated cells, rather than only the identification of the modification?
2. Does the ac-K82 antibody signal disappear in cells expressing K82R TDP-43 or after TDP-43 knockdown, in the same immunoblot format used for Fig. 5B?
3. In Fig. 2G–I, is the mRuby-TDP-43 signal quantified in cells that also express Clover-PY-NLS verified to be free of spectral bleed-through from the Clover channel?

## Overall

The core finding — that K82 acetylation disrupts importin-α1 binding and nuclear import — is well-supported by convergent biochemical and cell-based evidence, and the identification of K82 acetylation in sALS tissue is potentially important. The paper’s main weaknesses are in the strength of its causal and temporal language: “induces” and “initiates” outrun what the mass spec and cross-sectional human data can establish. The antibody specificity issue is the most serious gap, since the translational claim rests on it. With quantitative PTM comparison and a biological antibody control, this would be a strong paper; as written, the headline claims need requalification.
