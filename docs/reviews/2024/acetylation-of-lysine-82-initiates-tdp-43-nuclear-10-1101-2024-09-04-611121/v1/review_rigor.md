# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript identifies a plausible mechanism — K82 acetylation within the TDP-43 bipartite NLS disrupts importin-α1 binding and thus nuclear import — and provides correlative evidence from human tissue that this modification is elevated in sporadic ALS. The core experiments are well conceived, but the causal claims in the title and abstract ('initiates', 'drives', 'is sufficient to initiate') outrun the evidence. The design establishes a correlation between proteasome inhibition, K82 acetylation, and mislocalization, but does not show that K82 acetylation itself is the initiator in a physiological cascade; it demonstrates sufficiency of an acetylation-mimicking point mutant, not of the endogenous modification, and the human tissue data are a single-timepoint, cross-sectional observation that cannot distinguish initiation from consequence or co-occurrence. The manuscript needs a rebalancing of its interpretive language and additional controls before the central claims are adequately supported.

## Strengths
- The combination of unbiased quantitative proteomics to identify TDP-43 as the most sensitive nuclear cargo, followed by targeted mutagenesis to pinpoint K82, is a sound experimental strategy.
- The rescue experiment replacing the cNLS with the PY-NLS of FUS (Fig. 2E–I) is a well-designed test of mechanism that shows the mislocalization is specific to the import route, not a general nuclear-import failure.
- The use of newly generated, affinity-purified polyclonal antibodies to detect endogenous K82 acetylation in human postmortem tissue provides translational evidence for the proposed mechanism.

## Weaknesses
- **LOAD-BEARING: Causal claims in title and abstract outrun the evidence.** The authors state that K82 acetylation 'initiates TDP-43 nuclear loss of function' (title), 'drives disruption of its importin-α1-mediated nuclear import' and 'is sufficient to initiate TDP-43 nuclear loss of function and cytoplasmic accumulation' (abstract). The evidence for a causal, initiating role comprises: (i) partial proteasome inhibition leads to TDP-43 mislocalization and K82 acetylation (detected by mass spec and by acetylation-specific antibodies); (ii) an acetylation-mimicking K82Q point mutant abolishes importin-α1 binding and causes mislocalization. A K82Q substitution is not acetylation — it removes the positive charge and replaces it with a neutral, sterically distinct residue that may also disrupt hydrogen bonding differently from an acetyl group. Acetylation is reversible and chemically specific; a glutamine substitution provides a sufficiency argument for *charge neutralisation at that position* but does not demonstrate that acetylation per se is the initiator. Moreover, the mass spectrometry identifies ubiquitination and acetylation of K79, K82, and *and* phosphorylation of S91/92 — several modifications co-occur. The claim that K82 acetylation 'initiates' the cascade, rather than being one of several modifications whose combined effect produces the phenotype, is not established. There is no experiment that manipulates acetylation specifically (e.g., by inhibiting the relevant acetyltransferase or activating a deacetylase) and shows that this alone blocks the mislocalisation upon proteasome inhibition. The title and abstract should be rephrased to reflect what the data actually show: that K82 acetylation (or charge neutralisation) is sufficient to block nuclear import, and correlates with mislocalization in cells and disease tissue.
- **LOAD-BEARING: The human tissue data are cross-sectional and do not demonstrate initiation.** The abstract states that 'acetylation of lysine 82 in the motor cortex of sporadic ALS patients' was detected and that this 'evidence supporting acetylation as a plausible initiator of TDP-43 proteinopathies'. However, the data in Fig. 5B and 5C are immunoblots of postmortem tissue at a single timepoint (end-stage disease), showing that K82-acetylated TDP-43 is present in ALS samples (six of six) but not in controls (0/4). This is a cross-sectional, correlative observation. Acetylation could be a consequence of cellular stress or proteasome dysfunction that is itself secondary to the disease process, rather than an initiator. The claim that it is 'plausibly an initiator' is not contradicted by the data, but neither is it supported by them — any bystander modification that accumulates in sick neurons would show the same pattern. To distinguish initiation from consequence, one would need longitudinal data, or an experimental system where acetylation is manipulated independently of proteasome inhibition. The authors should explicitly acknowledge that the tissue data are correlative and consistent with, but do not establish, an initiating role.
- **LOAD-BEARING: The mechanistic claim rests on a correlational chain, not a direct test of the proposed sequence.** The proposed sequence is: reduced proteasome activity → K82 acetylation → disrupted importin-α1 binding → nuclear import failure → mislocalization → loss of function. The only direct evidence for each step is: step 1 (proteasome inhibition → mislocalization) is solid (Fig. 1). Step 2 (proteasome inhibition → K82 acetylation) is supported by mass spec (Fig. 3A-B) and by the acetylation-specific antibodies (Fig. S5, Fig. 5B-C) — but note that the mass spec identifies K82 acetylation after 24 h of proteasome inhibition, at which point TDP-43 is already mislocalized. The authors have not shown whether acetylation precedes mislocalization or is a consequence of it (e.g., because cytoplasmic TDP-43 encounters different acetyltransferases). Step 3 (K82 acetylation → disrupted importin-α1 binding) is demonstrated only for the K82Q mimic, not for endogenous acetylated TDP-43. The peptide-binding assay (Fig. 3F) shows that an acetylated K82 peptide cannot bind importin-α1, which is a strong argument for sufficiency — but even this is not a direct demonstration that the endogenous, full-length, acetylated protein cannot bind importin-α1. The entire chain from proteasome inhibition to mislocalization via K82 acetylation is inferred from correlations and from substitution mutants, not from an experiment in which K82 acetylation is the only variable. An experiment that blocks K82 acetylation (e.g., by knocking down the relevant acetyltransferase or overexpressing a deacetylase) and tests whether proteasome inhibition still causes mislocalization would directly test the causal sequence. Without it, the claim that this is the *initiating* event remains an untested inference.

## Questions
- Can the authors provide the quantified group data (mean ± SEM, individual points) for the nucleocytoplasmic ratio of TDP-43 across all cells and conditions in Fig. 1D, alongside the representative images?
- For the TMT-MS nuclear proteome experiment (Fig. 1E, F): what is the number of biological replicates, the FDR threshold applied, and were the comparisons done with correction for multiple hypotheses (e.g., BH-adjusted q-values)?
- For the live-cell imaging in Fig. S2C and Fig. 2G–I: were the cells counted blinded? How many cells per condition were tracked? Was the 'partial proteasome inhibition' condition (2 nM BTZ) confirmed to produce ~50% inhibition in these specific experiments?
- For the acetylation-mimicking variants: do the K82Q, K79Q, and K84Q substitutions lead to the same loss of importin-α1 binding in a cell-free system with purified proteins, or could the effect be indirect (e.g., altered localization causing reduced association after lysis)?
- In Fig. 5B, is the signal for ac-TDP-43(K82) in the sALS lanes also present when the blot is probed with the same antibody pre-adsorbed with the acetylated peptide (to confirm specificity in tissue lysates, where background can differ from peptide ELISA)?
- What is the total TDP-43 protein level in the sALS vs. control motor cortices? The claim that K82 acetylation is 'increased' in sALS requires normalization to total TDP-43 protein, not just GAPDH.
