# Statistics & Data-Analysis Reviewer

## Summary
The paper identifies acetylation of TDP‑43 lysine 82 as a trigger for nuclear loss of function and cytoplasmic mislocalization, using a combination of proteasome inhibition, mass spectrometry, mutagenesis, and patient tissue analysis. The biological ideas are interesting and the experimental design is largely thoughtful. However, the quantitative evidence for the central claims is under‑supported by the statistics reported. Key load‑bearing analyses lack proper multiple‑testing correction, stated sample sizes, and defined statistical tests, and several comparisons are presented as qualitative images or blots without replicate quantification. These gaps must be closed before the conclusions can be evaluated rigorously.

## Strengths
- The use of iPSC-derived cortical neurons and endogenous knock‑in tags strengthens the relevance of the cell culture findings.
- The rescue experiment replacing the TDP‑43 cNLS with the FUS PY‑NLS is well‑conceived and cleanly distinguishes an import pathway dependence.
- The mass spectrometry coverage (98.3%) of TDP‑43, including the full cNLS, is thorough and gives confidence in the modification list.

## Weaknesses
- HARD – Uncorrected multiple testing in the nuclear proteome screen
The claim that TDP‑43 is the protein whose nuclear localization is most sensitive to reduced proteasome activity rests on a TMT mass‑spec experiment comparing nuclear fractions of BTZ‑treated vs. untreated neurons. The volcano plot (Fig. 1E) is plotted with *unadjusted p‑values* (stated in the legend), and the text reports that “unadjusted P values were calculated using a one‑sample two‑sided Student’s t‑test.” With 5,202 proteins tested, no multiple‑testing correction (e.g., FDR) is applied or mentioned. Consequently, the top‑ranked protein under raw p‑values is not distinguished from what could arise by chance. This is a **HARD** issue: the authors must report the number of proteins remaining significant after an appropriate correction (e.g., Benjamini‑Hochberg at q<0.05) and confirm that TDP‑43 remains the most strongly reduced nuclear protein under that threshold. If few or no proteins survive correction, the claim is unsupported.
- HARD – Missing statistical information for quantification of TDP‑43 localization
In Figures 3D, 4E, 4G, and the corresponding supplementary panels, the authors quantify nuclear fluorescence of TDP‑43 variants and present bar plots with error bars. The figure legends do **not** specify: (a) the sample size (n = number of cells? independent differentiations?); (b) the statistical test used for pairwise comparisons (t‑test? ANOVA?); or (c) whether multiple comparisons were controlled when many mutants are compared against wild‑type. The Methods describe general statistical procedures, but each quantitative panel must state the test, the exact n, and the correction applied (if any). Without these, the reader cannot verify that the reported differences (e.g., K82Q abolishing nuclear import) are statistically significant rather than arising from biological or technical variability. Overlaying individual data points on the bars would further aid transparency.
- Soft – Insufficient qualification of the proteasome activity and viability curves
Figure 1A and Supplementary S1B,C show dose‑response curves for proteasome activity and cell viability. The text does not state the number of independent experiments or whether any statistical test was applied to define the 50% inhibition point (e.g., a comparison of the chosen dose to the control). Error bars are present but their definition and what they represent (SD or SEM) should be included in the legend.
- Soft – ac‑TDP‑43(K82) detection in patient samples lacks statistical comparison
Figure 5B presents immunoblots of motor cortex lysates from 4 controls and 6 sALS patients, with a qualitative claim that acetylation is increased in all sALS samples. A quantitative measure (e.g., band intensity normalized to total TDP‑43) and a comparison (e.g., Mann‑Whitney U test) would strengthen the claim. The sample size is small, and the conclusion would benefit from stating effect size and uncertainty.
- Soft – Peptide‑importin binding assay reported without statistical summary
Figure 3F shows concentration‑response curves for a series of modified TDP‑43 peptides binding to importin‑α1. The text states that “peptides acetylated at K82 did not bind to importin‑α1,” but no statistical comparison is provided (e.g., area under the curve, signal at a fixed concentration, or fitted binding parameters with confidence intervals). Replicate number is not given. A quantitative comparison, even a simple t‑test on endpoint binding at a high concentration, would make the claim testable.
- Soft – Representative immunoblots are the sole evidence for several interaction claims
Co‑immunoprecipitation experiments (Fig. 2A, 3E, 4C) and the stathmin‑2 rescue (Fig. S2E) are shown as single blots without quantification across biological replicates. While such blots are often supportive, the central claim that proteasome inhibition disrupts the TDP‑43–importin‑α1 interaction would be strengthened by replicate quantification (e.g., band intensity ratios) with an appropriate test.

## Questions
- For the mass spectrometry nuclear proteome (Fig. 1E), what was the FDR threshold used in DTASelect2 for peptide‑spectrum matches? Was any correction applied to the protein‑level TMT ratios? Please provide the number of proteins that remain significantly changed after FDR correction, and the specific rank and adjusted p‑value of TDP‑43.
- For all quantification of TDP‑43 nuclear fluorescence (Figs. 1D, 3D, 4E, 4G, and related supplementary panels), please state: the exact number of cells or fields imaged per condition, the number of independent differentiations, the statistical test used (and whether it was one‑ or two‑sided), and whether a multiple‑comparison correction was applied (if so, which). Overlay individual data points on the bar graphs.
- For the peptide binding assay (Fig. 3F), how many independent experiments were performed? Can the authors provide a statistical comparison of binding at a saturating concentration (e.g., 1 μM) across the peptides, or fitted dissociation constants with confidence intervals?
- For the antibody specificity validation (Fig. 5A), what metric and statistical criterion were used to define that the antibody recognizes only the acetylated peptide? Please show the raw data and number of replicates.
