CONFIDENCE: 4

**Overall take.** The central claim—that acetylation at K82 drives TDP-43 nuclear loss via disrupted importin-α1 binding—is plausible and worth pursuing, but the evidence presented for the load-bearing hypothesis is largely circumstantial or derived under conditions of *near-total* proteasome inhibition rather than the 50% reduction the authors say they modeled. The data do not rule out a two-step model where proteasome inhibition first triggers a distinct event that then licenses acetylation, nor do the peptide/recombinant experiments establish that K82 acetylation is upstream of (rather than coincident with) TDP-43 mislocalization in neurons. The sporadic ALS tissue staining is the closest to causally relevant evidence, but the antibody specificity is not established with adequate controls. Multiple HARD procedural gaps (below) currently prevent evaluation of the quantitative claims; once those are supplied the manuscript will be testable, but as presented the load-bearing conclusions are not independently reproducible from the text.

**Strengths.** (1) The time-course of TDP-43 mislocalization under sublethal proteasome inhibition is well controlled across three inhibitors and a dose-response curve. (2) The identification of K82 acetylation in human ALS motor cortex tissue is potentially important and appropriately compared to controls. (3) The stathmin-2 cryptic splicing assay provides a functional readout directly tied to TDP-43 loss-of-function.

**Weaknesses.**

1. **HARD — Acetylation at K82 is asserted without direct mass-spectrometric evidence.** The paper concludes that K82 acetylation drives the phenotype, yet I do not find MS data identifying an endogenous K82 acetylated peptide in treated neurons or ALS tissue. The evidence is limited to (a) an antibody whose specificity is not demonstrated against a defined panel of competing acetylated peptides, and (b) acetylation-mimetic mutants (K→Q). Neither establishes that endogenous TDP-43 is acetylated at K82, nor that this modification occurs before, rather than after, cytoplasmic mislocalization. If the antibody detects an in-vitro-acetylated peptide, show the peptide competition data and the MS spectrum for endogenous K82-acetylation.

2. **HARD — The cNLS-importin binding measurements are performed with peptides, not full-length TDP-43.** Peptide-based binding assays (presumably residues ~78–98) do not recapitulate the context of the folded NLS in the intact protein, where adjacent residues and RNA-binding domains could modulate accessibility. The authors must show that K82 acetylation (or the K82Q mutation) disrupts importin-α1 binding in the context of recombinant full-length TDP-43, or state explicitly that the claim is restricted to the isolated peptide.

3. **HARD — Proteasome inhibition levels and off-target effects are not adequately controlled.** BTZ and MG132 are used at concentrations set to reduce proteasome activity by ~50%, but the paper does not report whether this partially inhibits other proteases (e.g., calpains, cathepsins) that cleave TDP-43 and could generate the ~35-kDa fragments central to proteinopathy. A 50% inhibition is purposefully mild, but the specificity of BTZ at 2 nM for chymotrypsin-like activity (vs. trypsin-like, vs. calpain) is not established in the neuronal context. Without a second mechanistically distinct proteasome inhibitor or knockdown of a specific proteasome subunit, the attribution of the effect to the proteasome — rather than off-target kinase/phosphatase modulation — is not secured.

4. **HARD — No rescue or epistasis experiment.** If K82 acetylation is sufficient to initiate TDP-43 mislocalization, then preventing acetylation — by K82R mutation of the endogenous locus (CRISPR) or by expressing a deacetylase fused near the NLS — should rescue nuclear localization and stathmin-2 splicing under proteasome inhibition. The absence of any such experiment leaves the causal claim correlational. The time-course is also weak: the earliest timepoint after BTZ addition is not resolved, so causality (acetylation before mislocalization) is not demonstrated.

5. **HARD — The bortezomib-treated cells show cryptic splicing but the concentration-response is not shown.** At 2nM BTZ (in neurons) and presumably low nM in SH-SY5Y, does TDP-43 acetylation appear at an earlier time or lower dose than the cryptic-splicing phenotype? If acetylation is upstream, it must precede the splicing change. The authors show both at 24h, but the sequential ordering required by their model demands a time-course where acetylation is detected before or without splicing changes.

6. **HARD — Transfection-associated artifacts.** For the immunofluorescence assessment of cytoplasmic TDP-43 (Fig 1B-D), the authors used plasmid transfection of TDP-43-GFP at 24h. Overexpression itself can saturate nuclear import, and transfection reagents can cause stress-granule-like TDP-43 aggregates. The claim that cytoplasmic accumulation follows proteasome inhibition in endogenous settings needs a time-course with the endogenous protein (not overexpressed) at matched BTZ concentrations.

7. **HARD — Code/data availability for the proteomics and image quantification.** For the mass-spec analysis (if performed to identify acetylated residues in BTZ-treated lysates), no accession, software version, or search parameters (e.g., MaxQuant version, FDR thresholds) are given. For image quantification, no statement of cells per condition, number of independent differentiations, or analysis pipeline. These are required for replication. Under data deposition: no GEO/SRA identifier for the affected-neuron RNA-seq panel is provided; the stathmin-2 experiment has no accession.

8. **HARD — No evidence excluding proteasome-inhibitor off-target effects on transcription/protein quality control as drivers of the observed nuclear depletion.** Proteasome inhibition globally alters protein homeostasis. The claim that TDP-43 mislocalization is *specific* to its acetylation requires demonstrating that other shuttling proteins or general nuclear-import clients (e.g., a nuclear reporter without an acetylated cNLS) remain import-competent under identical BTZ doses.

9. **SOFT — The ALS tissue staining states “motor cortex” without quantifying severity or stage**, and the controls are described only as “controls.” Cortical TDP-43 pathology is regional and burden-dependent; the authors should report the number of neurons with cytoplasmic TDP-43 in the same sections and whether K82 acetylation co-localizes with cytoplasmic aggregates or appears in neurons with morphologically normal nuclei. Without this, the correlation could reflect secondary modification of aggregated protein.

10. **HARD — Antibody validation is incomplete for the K82-acK IHC.** The single-acetylation-site antibody requires (a) dot-blot or peptide-competition against K82-unmodified and K79 or K83 acetylated peptides, (b) confirmation that the IHC signal is absorbed by the acetylated peptide, and (c) demonstration on TDP-43-knockout or knockdown neurons that staining is specific. None are shown remotely.

11. **HARD — Software/statistical detail missing.** The cryptic splicing analysis uses a custom pipeline (new exon junction detection in stathmin-2) whose parameters (read-depth thresholds, junction filter criteria, alignment tool version) are not given. Similarly, the proteomics — if MS of TDP-43 peptides was performed — lacks instrument, search engine, and FDR thresholds. I also do not find the code or data accession for the transcriptomic analysis; without it, the stathmin-2 result cannot be independently reproduced.

12. **SOFT — No consolidated methods or data-availability statement.** A reader cannot tell where to find the raw RNA-seq, the custom scripts for cryptic-exon detection, or the IP-MS data. Given the central reliance on the K82-acetylation antibody, the peptide-competition control for its specificity should be in the main figures, not relegated to "data not shown" or a supplement without a figure number.

**Questions (one line each, only those not already in weaknesses):**  
- Is there evidence that the anti-K82ac antibody is specific against K84-acetylated or dimethylated TDP-43 peptides?  
- Does total (non-acetylated) K82 become ubiquitinated or otherwise modified in the 12–48h window before cytoplasmic accumulation?  
- Is the 2nM BTZ dose in human neurons confirmed to spare non-proteasomal proteases by activity assay, or only inferred from published IC50s?

**Strengths.** (i) The stathmin-2/STMN2 cryptic splicing and TDP-43 autoregulation readouts provide well-validated functional endpoints for nuclear TDP-43 loss. The IP-MS of TDP-43 interactors under proteasome inhibition is a sensible screen for NLS-perturbing PTMs ablating importin binding Hide  Name. (ii) Quantitative fractionation with nuclear/cytosolic markers supports the localization claims. (iii) Testing a panel of proteasome inhibitors at matched, viability-preserving doses is more rigorous than single-inhibitor studies.

**Data availability.** Underlying mass spectrometry data (proteomics for TDP-43 interactors; any PTM-mapping runs) are not deposited or referenced; if K82 acetylation is claimed, the raw files and search parameters are required (HARD). The imaging and immunoblot data are representative images; no quantified replicates are deposited established. Code for image quantification (if custom) is not described with versions.

**Verdict:** The claim that K82 acetylation initiates TDP-43 proteinopathy is biologically important but rests on a modification that is not directly evidenced, a specificity of detection that is not shown, and a causal order inferred from steady-state time points. I can provisionally recommend publication only if the load-bearing weaknesses above are addressed. I vote **major revision**, not rejection: the model is plausible and testablechers the tools exist.

**Questions I would put to the authors:**

1. What is the evidence that the acetyl-K82 antibody does not cross-react with acetyl-K84, K97, or other acetyl-lysines in the cNLS? Please provide the peptide array or competition data.
2. Can you detect endogenous K82-acetylation by targeted MS (AQUA or PRM) in BTZ-treated neurons before any observable TDP-43 mislocalization at 12 h?
3. Does the K82R mutant (acetylation-dead) rescue stathmin-2 cryptic splicing and TDP-43 nuclear retention under 50% proteasome inhibition?

**Bottom line.** The paper has identified a plausible post-translational modification hypothesis with a strong functional readout (stathmin-2), and the model is testable. But the central claim — acetylation at K82 as an *initiator* — is not experimentally supported with direct evidence (MS); the importin-α1 binding defect is shown only for a phospho- or acetyl-mimetic peptide of a different residue in another context, not for full-length TDP-43 with K82 acetylation; and the absence of any de-acetylase/rescue experiment leaves alternative explanations (ubiquitination, phosphorylation at nearby S/T) unaddressed. I need to see at least one of: (i) MS of endogenous TDP-43 identifying K82-acetylation in BTZ/MG132-treated neurons and ALS tissue; (ii) full-length recombinant TDP-43 acetylated at K82 showing reduced importin binding in vitro; (iii) CRISPR knock-in of K82R in iPSC-derived neurons protecting stathmin-2 from BTZ-induced misregulation. None of these appear in the manuscript as written.
