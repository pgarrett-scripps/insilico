# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The central novelty claim — that acetylation of K82 within the TDP-43 cNLS disrupts importin-α1 binding and thereby initiates TDP-43 nuclear loss of function, and that this acetylation is present in sALS motor cortex — is real and, on my searches, not preempted. The manuscript's own citations already acknowledge the nearest neighbors (Ko et al. 2024, Kametani et al. 2016, Doll et al. 2022), and the delta over them is substantive rather than cosmetic: Ko et al. showed the K82Q mimic disrupts importin-α1/β signaling, but this paper adds the mechanistic chain (proteasome decline → endogenous K82 acetylation → importin-α1 dissociation → nuclear depletion → stathmin-2 loss-of-function), the K82/K95-or-K97 bipartite requirement, and detection of endogenous K82 acetylation in sALS tissue. The contribution is framed at an appropriate altitude. The main novelty caveats are (i) the K82Q-mimic result is partly preempted by Ko et al., which the authors do acknowledge, and (ii) the claim that K82 acetylation is an initiator of proteinopathy is an inference that outruns the correlative human data.

## Strengths
- The paper is honest about its nearest neighbors: Ko et al. 2024 (K82Q mimic disrupting importin-α1/β), Kametani et al. 2016 (K82 acetylation in 1/2 ALS samples by MS), and Doll et al. 2022 (importin-α1/β recognition of the bipartite NLS) are all cited and the delta over each is stated.
- The mechanistic chain from proteasome decline to K82 acetylation to importin-α1 dissociation to nuclear loss-of-function is a genuinely new integration, not a reparameterization of known results.
- The K82/K95-or-K97 bipartite requirement (Fig. 4) is a novel, specific structural claim that goes beyond prior NLS characterization.

## Weaknesses
- The claim that K82 acetylation initiates TDP-43 proteinopathy ("sufficient to initiate TDP-43 nuclear loss of function," "acetylation as a plausible initiator") rests on the human data in Fig. 5, which are correlative: K82 acetylation is detected in sALS motor cortex alongside, and in the same samples as, phosphorylated TDP-43. The temporal ordering (acetylation "may be an earlier event than phosphorylation") is inferred from the presence of ac-K82 in the soluble fraction while phospho-TDP-43 is insoluble-only, but soluble-vs-insoluble partitioning is not a direct chronology. The alternative — that K82 acetylation is a consequence or correlate of an already-initiated proteinopathy rather than its initiator — is not excluded by the presented data. What would settle it: a time-course in a model where acetylation is shown to precede nuclear depletion and aggregation, or evidence that blocking K82 acetylation (e.g., deacetylase manipulation) prevents the downstream cascade.
- The novelty of the K82Q-mimic result specifically is partly preempted by Ko et al. 2024 ("Single acetylation-mimetic mutation in TDP-43 NLS disrupts importin α1/β signaling"), which the authors cite and which reported that a single acetylation-mimetic mutation disrupts importin-α1/β signaling. The authors' delta is the endogenous-acetylation detection and the full mechanistic chain, which is real — but the paper should state more explicitly that the K82Q-mimic observation itself is confirmatory of Ko et al., not novel, so the reader can see exactly what the new increment is.
- The claim that TDP-43 is "the protein whose nuclear localization is most sensitive to reduced proteasome activity" (Fig. 1E-F) is a strong, specific priority-adjacent claim. It rests on a single TMT experiment (nuclear proteome, one BTZ condition) whose statistical basis I cannot fully verify from the text (the volcano plot and P-value method are described but the underlying data are not deposited in a form I can inspect). If the nuclear-proteome dataset is not deposited, this "most sensitive" claim is not independently checkable. What would settle it: deposition of the TMT dataset (e.g., in a public repository) so the ranking and its significance can be audited.
- The "first to" framing is appropriately hedged ("plausible initiator," "evidence supporting"), which is good, but the abstract's "sufficient to initiate" is stronger than the correlative human evidence supports — a wording mismatch between abstract and discussion.
- The claim that K82 acetylation is "found in all sALS postmortem motor cortices examined" (6/6) is a small-n observation; the authors do flag variability, but the n=6/4 sample sizes should be stated as a limitation in the novelty framing rather than implied as general.
- The paper does not discuss whether the K82 acetylation it detects is the same modification previously reported by Kametani et al. 2016 (1/2 ALS samples) or a distinct finding — a head-to-head comparison of detection methods would sharpen the delta.
- The therapeutic-implication claim (deacetylase targeting, ref 32) is asserted rather than demonstrated; the paper shows no experiment where inhibiting deacetylation rescues TDP-43 nuclear import, so the "so what" for therapy outruns the demonstrated use case.
- No preempting preprint was found in my searches (bioRxiv/medRxiv/arXiv); the only matching preprint is the manuscript itself, so the priority claim survives a genuine search.

## Questions
- Is the TMT nuclear-proteome dataset (Fig. 1E-F) deposited in a public repository, and if so where, so the "most sensitive" ranking can be independently verified?
- Can the authors state explicitly which of their results are confirmatory of Ko et al. 2024 (K82Q-mimic) versus novel, so the increment is unambiguous?
- Is there any experiment (e.g., deacetylase inhibition or K82-acetylation blockade) that would test whether K82 acetylation precedes, rather than accompanies, nuclear depletion in a time-course?
