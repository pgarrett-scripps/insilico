# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The work identifies K82 acetylation within the TDP-43 cNLS as a modification that disrupts importin-α1 binding and nuclear import, and detects this modification in sALS motor cortex. The core finding that an acetylation-mimetic at K82 disrupts importin-α1 binding was recently reported by Ko et al. (2024, *J Mol Biol*), which the authors cite. The present manuscript extends this with acetylation-specific antibodies and patient tissue data, but the mechanistic sequence from proteasome inhibition to acetylation to nuclear depletion is incompletely supported. The contribution is real and incremental, but several novelty claims outrun the evidence provided.

## Weaknesses
- The causal sequence claim — that K82 acetylation "initiates" TDP-43 proteinopathy — is not established by the evidence presented. The title and abstract assert that acetylation of K82 "initiates TDP-43 nuclear loss of function" and "is sufficient to initiate TDP-43 proteinopathies." The supporting data are: (a) K82Q (acetylation-mimic) blocks import in cultured neurons; (b) ac-K82 signal is detectable in sALS motor cortex but not controls; (c) ac-K82 appears in both soluble and insoluble fractions whereas pS409/410 is only in the insoluble fraction, which the authors interpret as acetylation preceding phosphorylation. None of these establish initiation. The proteasome-inhibition time course (Fig. 1B, S1G) shows mislocalization beginning by 12 h of BTZ treatment, but no time-resolved measurement of K82 acetylation is reported. The sALS tissue data are cross-sectional and correlative. An equally plausible alternative is that acetylation is a downstream consequence of TDP-43 mislocalization, occurring on cytoplasmic TDP-43 after import failure from another cause. To distinguish these, the authors would need to show that acetylation precedes mislocalization (e.g., by blocking acetylation and showing nuclear retention is preserved under proteasome inhibition), or demonstrate that a non-acetylatable K82R mutant is resistant to BTZ-induced mislocalization.
- The specific link between partial proteasome inhibition and K82 acetylation is mechanistically undefined. The manuscript shows that 50% proteasome inhibition triggers TDP-43 mislocalization and that K82 acetylation is detectable by MS after BTZ treatment (Fig. 3A–B) and by immunoblot (Fig. S5). But it does not identify the acetyltransferase(s) or deacetylase(s) that regulate K82, nor does it demonstrate that proteasome inhibition directly elevates K82 acetylation levels (the S5 blot is not quantified and uses a single BTZ dose at one time point). The model in the Discussion invokes "competing activities of acetyltransferases and deacetylases" without testing whether inhibiting the relevant acetyltransferase blocks the mislocalization. Without this, reduced proteasome activity and K82 acetylation are co-occurring events in the same experiment, not a demonstrated causal chain. A minimal addition would be to measure K82 acetylation quantitatively over the BTZ time course and test whether the N-terminal acetyltransferase or HDAC inhibitors modify the phenotype.
- The prioritisation relative to Ko et al. (2024) is incompletely stated. The manuscript cites Ko et al. (*J Mol Biol*, 2024) as "consistent with a recent report" showing that "single acetylation-mimetic mutation in TDP-43 Nuclear Localization Signal disrupts importin α1/β signaling." That paper already demonstrated that K82Q (the same acetylation-mimic used here) disrupts importin binding and nuclear localisation. The present work clearly adds: (a) identification of the modification in the context of proteasome stress by MS; (b) peptide-binding assays with *bona fide* acetylated (not K82Q) peptides; (c) demonstration that K82 acetylation is present in sALS tissue. These are meaningful extensions, but the core finding — K82 acetylation-mimetic disrupts import — was in print before this preprint. The current framing ("acetylation of lysine 82 initiates TDP-43 nuclear loss of function") reads as a priority claim and should explicitly state what is added over Ko et al. The Discussion does not name or differentiate the Ko et al. findings; it only cites the paper in parentheses.
- The claim that "TDP-43 is the protein whose nuclear localization is most sensitive to reduced proteasome activity" (Fig. 1E–F) relies on a single time point (24 h) and a single BTZ dose (20 nM); different doses or durations might identify other sensitive proteins, and this should be noted as a limitation.
- The Kametani et al. (2016, *Sci Rep*) mass spectrometry study identified K82 acetylation in 1/2 ALS samples; the present manuscript cites this but does not discuss how the current 6/6 detection rate differs in method or cohort (different antibodies, different tissue fractionation, different disease stage).
- The evidence that PY-NLS swap "prevented" nuclear loss of function is based on stathmin-2 protein levels (Fig. S2E); the stathmin-2 mRNA splicing data shown in Fig. 1G are for the endogenous TDP-43 mislocalization, not for the rescue experiment — a side-by-side comparison would strengthen the functional rescue claim.
- The ac-K82 antibodies are validated only by ELISA against the cognate peptide (Fig. 5A); cross-reactivity against other acetyl-lysine TDP-43 peptides (e.g., K136ac, K145ac) is not reported, which is relevant since Cohen et al. showed K145ac is enriched in pathological TDP-43.
- The 4 control vs. 6 sALS sample size for human tissue (Fig. 5B–C) is small; the observation that ac-K82 is present in all 6 sALS and 0/4 controls is suggestive but should be expanded in larger cohorts, and the authors should report whether the sALS cases had confirmed TDP-43 pathology.
- The "initiator" model in the Discussion proposes that nuclear depletion precedes cytoplasmic aggregation, but the manuscript does not test whether K82 acetylation promotes aggregation or phase separation — the Garcia Morato et al. (2022) work on K136ac shows how such a test could be done.
