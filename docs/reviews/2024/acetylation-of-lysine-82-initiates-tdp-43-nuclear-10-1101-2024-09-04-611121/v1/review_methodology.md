# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The design mostly supports the conclusion that proteasome inhibition drives TDP-43 mislocalization via acetylation of K82 disrupting importin‑α1 binding, but two critical design mismatches weaken the core dose‑response claim, and the human tissue evidence is preliminary. The mutagenesis and peptide binding experiments are well controlled and convincing. The kinase/PTM‑mimic studies are sound, though the identification of acetylation in neurons relies on antibody signal rather than direct mass spectrometry. The manuscript would be strengthened by imaging at the exact 50% inhibition dose used for fractionation, by quantification and statistics for the sALS immunoblots, and by replication in a larger human cohort.

## Strengths
- The rescue experiment replacing the cNLS with a PY‑NLS (Fig 2E–I) provides a clean loss‑of‑function control: it changes which import receptor is used, and co‑expression of the PY‑NLS variant prevents mislocalisation of the wild‑type protein, supporting a model where disruption of importin‑α1 binding is causal.
- The peptide binding assay (Fig 3F) directly tests the effect of acetylation on importin‑α1 binding, avoiding the ambiguities of glutamine mimics and showing that K82 acetylation alone abolishes interaction.
- The comprehensive set of lysine‑to‑arginine mutants (Fig 4) systematically maps the bipartite NLS requirements and identifies K82 and either K95/K97 as necessary and sufficient for nuclear import.

## Weaknesses
- Dose mismatch between activity measurement and imaging/proteomics undermines the central claim. The activity curve (Fig 1A) shows that 2 nM BTZ gives ~50% inhibition, but the immunofluorescence images (Fig 1C,D) use 20 nM, and the nuclear proteome data (Fig 1E) also use 20 nM. The fractionation immunoblot (Fig 1B) uses 2 nM and supports the claim, but the imaging and proteomics – which are presented as the primary evidence for sensitivity – do not match the dose of the activity measurement. The conclusion that 50% inhibition triggers mislocalisation is therefore not supported by the images or the proteomics, which use a stronger insult. The authors should either repeat the imaging and proteomics at 2 nM BTZ or clearly state that higher doses were used and discuss the threshold.
- Human tissue data (Fig 5) lack quantification and statistical rigour. The claim that K82 acetylation is increased in sALS motor cortex rests on immunoblots of 6 sALS and 4 controls, with no densitometry, error bars, or statistical test. The blot shows variable signal among sALS samples, and the single sALS/control comparison for insoluble fractions (Fig 5C) cannot support the conclusion that acetylation precedes phosphorylation. The sample size is too small to generalise; at a minimum, quantify the signals, report a p‑value, and acknowledge the limited cohort. Additional cases would strengthen the claim.
- Post‑translational modification mapping was performed in a cancer cell line, not in neurons. The mass spectrometry identification of K79/K82/K84 acetylation (Fig 3A,B) used SH‑SY5Y cells with homozygous TDP‑43‑Clover knock‑in, not iPSC‑derived cortical neurons. While the acetyl‑K82 antibody detects increased signal in BTZ‑treated neurons (Fig S5), the exact modification sites and their stoichiometry in neurons remain unconfirmed by mass spectrometry. The authors should either provide mass spectrometry data from treated neurons or explicitly limit the claim to the cell line and note that the antibody data are supportive.
- Live‑cell competition experiment (Fig 2G–I) does not exclude a dominant‑negative or trapping mechanism. The observation that Clover‑TDP‑43‑PY‑NLS prevents mislocalisation of mRuby‑TDP‑43 is consistent with the model, but it could also arise if dimerisation with PY‑NLS‑containing molecules retains mRuby‑TDP‑43 in the nucleus irrespective of importin‑α1 function. The authors show that PY‑NLS binds importin‑β2, but they do not demonstrate that the mRuby‑TDP‑43 in co‑expressing cells is actually being imported through importin‑β2. An importin‑β2 knockdown or inhibitor would address this alternative.
- The stathmin‑2 cryptic splicing result (Fig 1G) is presented only as a gel image without quantification. The claim that TDP‑43 loss of function occurs after proteasome inhibition needs either qRT‑PCR data with statistics or a representative blot accompanied by a quantification panel. The immunoblot in Fig S2E provides protein‑level confirmation for the PY‑NLS rescue, but the key functional readout in Fig 1G requires quantification.
- Co‑immunoprecipitation experiments lack a negative control IP (e.g., IgG) to rule out non‑specific binding. While the signal for importin‑α1 is absent in the BTZ condition, the background binding to beads alone is not shown. This is a minor but standard control.
- The claim that acetylation at K82 is “sufficient to initiate TDP‑43 nuclear loss of function” (Abstract/Discussion) is not supported by a kinetic or dose‑response experiment. The K82Q mutant shows complete mislocalisation at a single time point, but whether acetylation is the *initiating* event (rather than an early one) is not directly tested. A time‑course of acetylation appearance relative to mislocalisation would strengthen this temporal claim.

## Questions
- For the imaging in Fig 1C, were the same doses (2 nM BTZ, 50 nM MG132, 10 nM MRZ) ever used? If so, are those data available? If not, why was a higher dose chosen for the imaging?
- Was the acetyl‑K82 antibody signal in the human tissue quantified and normalised to total TDP‑43? Can the authors provide the individual values and a statistical comparison?
- Could the authors provide mass spectrometry data identifying acetylation at K79, K82, or K84 in iPSC‑derived cortical neurons treated with 2 nM BTZ?
