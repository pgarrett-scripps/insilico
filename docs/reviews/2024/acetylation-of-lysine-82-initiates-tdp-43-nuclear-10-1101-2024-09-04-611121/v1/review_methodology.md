# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The core mechanistic claim — that lysine 82 within the TDP-43 cNLS is critical for importin-α1 binding and that its acetylation disrupts nuclear import — is supported by a well-designed set of experiments, including a charge-conserving K82R mutation, an in vitro peptide binding assay using genuinely acetylated peptides, and a PY-NLS swap rescue with a matched FUS control. However, three design gaps weaken the paper: the mass spectrometry cannot distinguish acetylation from ubiquitination at the identified lysines, the co-immunoprecipitation evidence for disrupted importin binding is confounded by compartmentalization, and the sALS tissue data lacks a competition control and stated blinding. The Discussion's claim that acetylation "initiates and drives" TDP-43 proteinopathy outruns the correlational evidence presented.

## Strengths
- The K82R charge-conserving mutation is an excellent control — it demonstrates that the specific residue matters beyond its positive charge, which is precisely the right way to test a post-translational modification hypothesis.
- The in vitro peptide binding assay with genuinely acetylated K82 directly tests the binding claim without cellular confounds, and the random-peptide negative control is appropriate.
- The PY-NLS swap rescue, paired with the FUS/importin-β2 control, provides a clean specificity test isolating the cNLS as the target of the proteasome-inhibition effect.

## Weaknesses
- Mass spectrometry cannot distinguish acetylation from ubiquitination at the identified lysines (HARD). The manuscript states that "acetylation and/or ubiquitination of lysines 79, 82, and 84 were identified." Acetylation (+42.0106 Da) and ubiquitination (di-glycine remnant, +114.0429 Da) are distinguishable by mass, yet the authors do not report which modification was present at each site. The paper's central claim is specifically about acetylation, but the mass spec evidence is ambiguous. The K82Q mimic and the acetylated-peptide binding assay support acetylation, and the ac-K82 antibody is the direct in-cell evidence — but the antibody's validation is incomplete (see weakness 3). The authors should report the specific modification identified at each lysine, or explicitly acknowledge the ambiguity and rest the acetylation claim on the antibody and peptide data alone.
- Co-immunoprecipitation evidence is confounded by compartmentalization (SOFT-to-HARD). The co-IP showing loss of importin-α1 binding after 24 hr of BTZ (Fig 2A) is interpreted as disruption of the binding interface. But at 24 hr, TDP-43 is already mislocalized to the cytoplasm. If acetylation occurs in the cytoplasm after mislocalization from another cause, the reduced co-IP would be a consequence, not a cause. The in vitro peptide assay (Fig 3F) is the direct test and supports the interface-disruption model, so the conclusion is probably right — but the co-IP alone cannot distinguish cause from consequence. Performing the co-IP at an earlier time point before mislocalization is detectable, or explicitly hedging the co-IP interpretation, would resolve this.
- sALS tissue data lacks a competition control and stated blinding (SOFT). The ac-K82 signal in sALS motor cortex (Fig 5B) is the key clinical evidence. The antibody is validated by ELISA against the peptide, but there is no competition control showing the tissue signal is competed by the acetylated peptide and not the unacetylated one. Without this, specificity of the tissue signal is not fully established. The comparison of 6 sALS vs 4 controls also does not state whether the immunoblot analysis was blinded. A competition control on a subset of sALS samples would settle both concerns.
- In vivo causal claim outruns the evidence (SOFT, overclaiming). The Discussion states acetylation "initiates and drives TDP-43 proteinopathy." The design shows acetylation can disrupt nuclear import in vitro and in cultured cells, and that ac-K82 is elevated in sALS tissue. It does not test whether acetylation initiates proteinopathy in vivo; the tissue data support association, not causation. The claim should be scaled to "acetylation can initiate" unless in vivo causal evidence is added.
- Replication not stated (SOFT). The number of independent differentiations for the iPSC-derived neuron experiments is not reported. For a paper whose key effects rest on cultured neuron phenotypes, this should be stated.
- K82Q is a mimic, not actual acetylation (SOFT). The cellular "sufficient" claim rests on the glutamine substitution. The peptide assay covers actual acetylation, but the cellular experiments use the mimic; this is standard practice but should be acknowledged as a limitation.

## Questions
- Can the mass spectrometry data distinguish acetylation from ubiquitination at K79/K82/K84, and what was the specific modification at each site?
- Was a competition control (pre-incubating the ac-K82 antibody with the acetylated peptide) performed on sALS tissue lysates?
- How many independent differentiations were used for the iPSC-derived neuron experiments?
- Was the sALS versus control immunoblot analysis blinded?
