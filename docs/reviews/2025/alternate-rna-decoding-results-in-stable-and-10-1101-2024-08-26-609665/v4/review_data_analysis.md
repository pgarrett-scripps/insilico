# Statistics & Data-Analysis Reviewer

## Summary
This large-scale proteomics study identifies thousands of amino acid substitutions in human and mouse proteins and quantifies their relative abundance (RAAS). The core finding—that some alternatively translated proteoforms are more abundant than their canonical counterparts—rests on solid MS/MS validation and multiple internal consistency checks. However, the statistical analysis has significant gaps: multiple-comparison corrections are inconsistently applied, the machine-learning model lacks proper validation reporting, and several key quantitative claims lack sufficient methodological detail to verify. The work is substantial and the validation strategy is thoughtful, but the statistical rigor does not match the scale of the claims.

## Strengths
- The six-part validation strategy for RAAS estimates (ionization efficiency, detectability, multiple digests, shared peptides, retention time, degradation rates) is well-designed and provides genuine triangulation across independent measurements rather than circular confirmation.

## Weaknesses
- **Multiple-comparison correction is applied inconsistently and incompletely.** The authors use Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for GO enrichment (Methods, 'Protein set enrichment analysis') and Pfam domain analysis, but do not disclose whether they corrected for the many statistical tests across the main results. Figure 4b,c and Extended Data Fig. 5 show RAAS dotplots comparing distributions across hundreds of substitution types and amino acid properties using Student's t-tests; the Methods state 'two-sided Student's t-tests' but do not state how many tests were run, whether a family-wise correction was applied, or what the corrected threshold is. The paper reports p-values as 'p≤10^−10' and 'p<10^−15' throughout (e.g., Fig. 5b, Extended Data Fig. 5h,i) without disclosing the family size or correction method. This is a HARD issue: without knowing the number of tests and the correction applied, the reader cannot assess whether these p-values are credible. The authors should state the total number of substitution-type comparisons, the correction method, and the corrected significance threshold.
- **The XGBoost RAAS prediction model (Fig. 5i) lacks proper validation reporting.** The Methods state 'The data was randomly split into a train fraction, 80%, and a test fraction, 20%' and 'The model was retrained 1000 times on random subsets of test and train data,' but do not disclose: (1) whether hyperparameters were tuned on the test set (leakage risk); (2) the distribution of performance across the 1000 retrainings (only a median is shown); (3) whether the reported Pearson correlation of 0.44 is the median, mean, or a single run; (4) the baseline model used for comparison. The caption states 'Each feature used increases the model's predictive power (bottom panel)' but does not define how feature importance was computed or whether it was derived from the same test set. A correlation of 0.44 on test data is modest; the paper should report 95% CIs on the correlation across folds and compare against a null model (e.g., predicting median RAAS for all proteins).
- **The gnomAD constraint analysis (Fig. 5j,k) lacks a formal statistical test and effect-size reporting.** The text states 'Missense variants are less constrained with increasing RAAS (p<10^−4)' but does not name the test (Spearman? linear regression?), the sample size (number of codons?), or the effect size (slope, Spearman ρ?). The Methods describe computing observed/expected ratios and comparing control groups but do not state the test statistic or degrees of freedom. The p-value alone does not establish the magnitude of the association; a correlation of 0.1 and 0.8 can both be 'significant' in large samples. Report the effect size (ρ or β) with 95% CI.
- **The degradation-rate analysis (Fig. 3c–f) does not disclose the sample size or statistical power.** The text states 'Many substituted peptides have lower degradation rates than their non-substituted counterparts' and 'strong inverse correlation (p<10^−10)' but does not report n (number of SAAP? number of proteins? number of cells?). The Methods state 'Degradation rates (α) were computed as the slope of ln(1+h/l)=αt' but do not specify how many time points were used per peptide, whether replicates were averaged before regression, or whether the regression was weighted. Figure 3d shows a scatter plot with apparent heteroscedasticity; were the data log-transformed? The correlation is reported for 'primary hepatocytes' and separately for 'B cells and natural killer cells' (Extended Data Fig. 3m,n) but the sample sizes and test statistics are not given. State n and the regression method for each cell type.
- **The ANOVA model (Fig. 4f) does not report effect sizes or confidence intervals.** The figure shows F-values and p-values but not the proportion of variance explained (η² or ω²) or 95% CIs on the effect sizes. The text states 'substitution type explains the most variance' but does not quantify 'most'—is it 30% or 70%? Report η² with 95% CI for each factor.
- **Tissue-type enrichment analysis (Extended Data Fig. 5h,i) applies FDR correction but does not disclose the family size or the corrected threshold.** The Methods state 'p-values were adjusted with a Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction to get q-values' and 'Substitution types were considered significantly enriched...if...q-value of ≤0.01' but do not state how many substitution-type × tissue comparisons were tested. If there are 255 unique substitutions (stated in Results) and 4 tissues, that is ~1000 tests; the corrected threshold should be much lower than 0.01 if Benjamini-Hochberg was applied at family-wise α=0.05. Clarify the family size and verify the corrected threshold.
- **The tumor vs. normal tissue comparison (Fig. 5b) does not disclose the sample size or multiple-comparison correction.** The volcano plot shows substitution types with significantly different RAAS in tumor vs. matched normal tissue, but the Methods state 'p-values were FDR-adjusted into q-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg method' without naming the family size (number of substitution types tested?). The text does not report how many patient pairs were included or whether the paired t-test was the appropriate test (are the RAAS values normally distributed within each substitution type?). Report n (number of substitution types and patient pairs) and the corrected significance threshold.
- **The RNA modification overlap test (Supplemental Fig. 8a) uses a hypergeometric test but does not disclose the multiple-comparison correction or the prior probability of overlap.** The Methods state 'we used a cumulative hypergeometric distribution test (p[X>179], in R: phyper...)' and report p<10^−10, but do not state whether this p-value was corrected for testing multiple RNA modifications or whether the test was one-sided or two-sided. The text states 'uracil modifications overlap significantly (p<10^−10) with substitution sites' but does not report the observed and expected counts or the odds ratio. Report the counts, the odds ratio with 95% CI, and whether a Bonferroni or other correction was applied if multiple modifications were tested.

## Questions
- How many total statistical tests were run across Figures 4b,c and Extended Data Fig. 5, and what was the family-wise correction applied?
- For the XGBoost model, were hyperparameters (tree depth, learning rate, etc.) tuned on the test set, and what is the 95% CI on the test-set correlation across the 1000 retrainings?
- For the gnomAD constraint analysis, what is the Spearman ρ or regression slope with 95% CI, and how many codons were included in each RAAS quartile?
- For the degradation-rate analysis, what is n (number of SAAP, proteins, or cell replicates) for each cell type, and were the data log-transformed before regression?
