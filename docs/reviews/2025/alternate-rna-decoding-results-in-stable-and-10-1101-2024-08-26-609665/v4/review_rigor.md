# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This is a large-scale proteomics study making a striking claim: that amino acid substitutions arising from alternate translation are not rare errors but stable, abundant proteins in mammalian tissues, sometimes exceeding their canonical counterparts. The work is technically ambitious and the validation pipeline is extensive, but the central claim—that high RAAS (substitution ratios >1) reflects genuine alternate translation rather than confounds—rests on indirect evidence. The authors acknowledge limitations honestly, but several load-bearing inferences remain underdetermined by the data presented.

## Strengths
- The validation pipeline is comprehensive: six independent lines of evidence for RAAS accuracy (ionization efficiency, detectability, multi-digest reproducibility, shared peptide consistency, degradation rates, and PepQuery confirmation) substantially reduce the risk of systematic artifact.
- The authors transparently acknowledge the tension between stringent filtering (which removes true positives) and false-positive risk, and provide both a conservative set (2,000 high-confidence sites) and a broader set (8,801 sites) for different use cases.
- The association of high-RAAS substitutions with protein disorder, lower conservation, and relaxed gnomAD constraint is consistent with a functional role and argues against these being random noise.

## Weaknesses
- **Load-bearing claim 1: High-RAAS substitutions reflect alternate translation, not alternative alleles or multiple modifications.** The authors exclude genetic variants by checking that LSCC patient genomes do not carry the relevant mutations (stated once, briefly). However, this is checked only for one cancer type, and the gnomAD analysis (Fig. 5j–k) shows that most alternate-translation codons have no observed variants—which is consistent with both the authors' claim and with the alternative that these sites are simply not polymorphic in the population, leaving the genetic origin hypothesis underdetermined. More critically, for ~half of all SAAP, the mass shift is not localized to a single position with high confidence (Methods: "for about half of all spectra matched to SAAP, the measured mass shift... cannot arise from multiple combinatorial modifications on different residues adding up to the mass shift; for the remaining SAAP, unknown combinatorial modifications cannot be rigorously disproved"). The authors argue that fragment ion intensities favor the proposed substitution over alternatives (Supplemental Fig. 4c–f), but this is a Bayesian argument: the predicted spectrum for Q→G is closer to observed than the predicted spectrum for cleavage, but both could be wrong, and the comparison does not rule out other modifications (e.g., simultaneous PTMs on different residues). The 2,000 high-confidence sites (positional probability >0.9) partially address this, but the main results (8,801 sites, 360 proteins with RAAS>1) include the lower-confidence set. The claim that these are "stable and abundant proteins" is thus supported for the high-confidence subset but overstated for the full dataset.
- **Load-bearing claim 2: Protein stability is a major mechanism driving high RAAS.** The authors measure degradation rates in hepatocytes (Fig. 3c–f) and find that SAAP with high RAAS have lower degradation rates than their base peptides (inverse correlation, p<10^−10). However, this is correlational: slower degradation of a SAAP could reflect either (a) the substitution stabilizing the protein, or (b) the SAAP being a minor proteoform that is only detected when it is stable enough to accumulate—a selection bias. The authors acknowledge this indirectly ("sites on lowly abundant proteins with low RAAS are less likely to be detected") but do not fully disentangle it. The degradation analysis is also limited to hepatocytes and three other cell types; generalization to the full dataset (which includes cancer tissues) is not established. Moreover, the effect size is modest: the log-ratio of degradation rates for SAAP vs. BP is small (Fig. 3d), and the authors note "the signal to noise in the degradation rates is smaller compared to the abundance measures." This is consistent with stability contributing to RAAS, but it does not establish it as a primary driver for the high-RAAS proteins that motivate the paper.
- **Load-bearing claim 3: These substitutions are conserved across species and functionally significant.** The authors identify 55 identical SAAP in human and mouse (Fig. 5l) and show their RAAS values correlate (p<10^−8). However, 55 shared sites out of 8,801 human sites is ~0.6% overlap, and the authors note this "likely underestimates the degree of conservation" due to sequence homology differences and technical factors. The correlation of RAAS for these 55 sites is real but does not establish that the broader set of human-specific substitutions are functional or conserved. The functional significance argument relies on GO enrichment (Fig. 5c) and association with cancer (Fig. 5a–b), but GO enrichment of high-RAAS proteins is not the same as showing those proteins have altered function due to the substitution—it shows they are in functional categories. The cancer-specific examples (lamin A, PP1-beta) are striking but anecdotal; no global test for cancer association is reported. The claim in the abstract that these results "demonstrate the contribution of alternate translation to diversifying mammalian proteomes" is supported for the identified substitutions but not for their functional impact.
- The title claims substitutions result in "stable and abundant proteins," but abundance is measured as RAAS (ratio to canonical form), not absolute copy number; high RAAS does not necessarily mean high absolute abundance if the canonical form is also rare.
- The abstract states "products of alternate translation are more abundant than their canonical counterparts for hundreds of proteins," but this is true only for ~360 proteins with RAAS>1 (Fig. 2h), and the confidence in this subset is lower for the broader dataset; the phrasing should specify the high-confidence subset or the RAAS threshold.
- The claim that "substitution ratios are positively associated with intrinsically disordered regions" (abstract) is supported (Fig. 5h), but causality is not established—disorder may be a consequence of the substitution, not a predictor of where substitutions occur.
- The paper does not report how many of the 8,801 sites would remain after applying the stringent filters used for the 2,000 high-confidence set; this would clarify how much of the main result depends on lower-confidence identifications.
- The ANOVA analysis (Fig. 4f) shows substitution type explains more variance than tissue type, but the effect sizes are not reported, making it difficult to assess the biological importance of tissue-specific variation.

## Questions
- For the ~4,000 SAAP with positional probability <0.9, what is the distribution of alternative hypotheses that could explain the observed mass shifts, and how often does the predicted spectrum for the proposed substitution rank first among all tested alternatives?
- In the hepatocyte degradation data, what fraction of the inverse correlation between RAAS and degradation rate remains after controlling for protein abundance (which the authors note is correlated with both RAAS and detectability)?
- How many of the 360 proteins with RAAS>1 in the full dataset would retain RAAS>1 if only high-confidence (positional probability >0.9) SAAP were included?
- For the 55 conserved SAAP between human and mouse, what is the range of RAAS values, and do they cluster in the high-RAAS or low-RAAS tail of the distribution?
