# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The paper makes a striking claim—that alternate translation produces thousands of stable, abundant proteins in mammals—supported by a large-scale proteomics analysis with extensive validation steps. The core methodology is sound and the validation efforts are commendable, but a critical design gap undermines the central causal claim: the paper demonstrates correlation between high RAAS and protein stability (Fig. 3d) but does not establish that stability *causes* high abundance rather than the reverse, nor does it rule out the possibility that both are consequences of a third variable. The quantification of RAAS itself is well-defended against technical artifacts, but the mechanistic interpretation of why certain substitutions are abundant rests on an incomplete causal argument.

## Strengths
- The dependent peptide search strategy with multiple independent validation layers (database search, PepQuery, Oktoberfest rescoring, multi-digest confirmation, shared peptide triangulation) substantially reduces false-positive risk and is well-justified for a discovery claim of this magnitude.
- The paper directly measures protein degradation rates using metabolic pulse-chase data (Fig. 3c–f), which is the appropriate method to test whether stability contributes to RAAS, rather than relying on indirect proxies.
- Extensive controls for technical confounds—ionization efficiency, peptide detectability, missed cleavages, retention time prediction—systematically rule out MS-specific artifacts as explanations for high RAAS values.

## Weaknesses
- **Causality from stability to abundance is not established.** The paper shows that high-RAAS substitutions have lower degradation rates (Fig. 3d, r < 10^−10), but this correlation does not prove that stability *causes* high abundance. The inverse relationship could equally reflect: (1) selection bias—only substitutions that happen to be stable enough to detect are observed, while destabilizing substitutions are degraded below detection limit; (2) reverse causality—high-abundance proteins may be more stable because they are more likely to fold correctly or be protected by binding partners; or (3) confounding—both stability and abundance may be driven by a third variable, such as codon optimality or mRNA structure, which affects both synthesis rate and degradation rate independently. The paper acknowledges detection bias (Discussion, 'lowly abundant substitutions are less likely to be identified') but does not address how this bias affects the causal interpretation of the stability–abundance relationship. To establish causality, the authors would need either: (a) a perturbation experiment (e.g., mutagenesis to destabilize a high-RAAS substitution and measure whether RAAS decreases), or (b) a more rigorous causal model that accounts for selection bias and competing mechanisms. As written, the claim that 'protein stability contributes to substitution abundance' (Abstract, Results heading) overstates what the correlation demonstrates.
- **The mechanistic explanation for high RAAS relies on codon frequency and tRNA pairing, but alternative mechanisms are not adequately ruled out.** Figure 3a–b and Extended Data Fig. 4 show that RAAS correlates with codon-anticodon mismatches and codon frequency, consistent with a 'ribosomal misreading' model. However, the paper also identifies a strong association with RNA modifications (U modifications, p < 10^−10, Supplemental Fig. 8) and with sequence motifs (KRAQ, CC, MM, WW), yet does not test whether these motifs or modifications are sufficient to predict RAAS independently of codon frequency. If sequence context or RNA modifications are the primary drivers, then the codon-frequency effect may be spurious or secondary. The paper states (Discussion) that 'multiple mechanisms likely contribute,' but does not quantify their relative contributions or test whether they are independent. A multivariate model (e.g., logistic or linear regression) predicting RAAS from codon frequency, modification status, and motif presence would clarify which mechanisms dominate and whether codon frequency remains significant after controlling for modifications.
- **The claim that 360 proteins have alternatively translated proteoforms as their most abundant form is not adequately supported by the evidence presented.** The paper identifies ~9,000 unique SAAP–BP pairs and reports that ~10% have RAAS > 1 (Fig. 2b), but the statement 'alternatively translated proteoforms are the most abundant protein products for 360 proteins' (Results, Quantification section) appears to conflate two different quantities: the number of unique substitution sites with RAAS > 1 and the number of proteins in which the substituted form is the dominant proteoform. If a protein has multiple substitution sites, only some of which have RAAS > 1, the protein-level abundance of the substituted form depends on which sites are included in the calculation. The paper computes 'protein RAAS' as the median RAAS across all SAAP in a protein (Methods, Protein set enrichment analysis), but does not clearly state how many proteins have median RAAS > 1, nor does it account for the possibility that different substitution sites in the same protein may be on different proteoforms (i.e., not all substitutions may occur on the same molecule). Figure 2h shows rank-sorted proteins with RAAS > 0.1, not RAAS > 1, and the text does not clearly distinguish between the two thresholds. Clarification of the protein-level calculation and a recount of proteins with median RAAS > 1 would resolve this.
- The paper does not test whether the identified substitutions are functionally relevant or whether they alter protein activity; the claim that they 'diversify mammalian proteomes' is supported only by abundance and conservation, not by functional assays.
- The ANOVA in Fig. 4f shows that substitution type explains the most variance in RAAS, but the model does not include codon frequency, RNA modifications, or sequence motifs as predictors, so it is unclear whether substitution type is a proxy for these mechanistic variables or an independent driver.
- The prediction model (Fig. 5i) achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.44 between predicted and observed RAAS, which is modest; the paper does not discuss whether this is sufficient to claim that protein features 'successfully predict' RAAS or whether unmeasured variables dominate.
- The conservation analysis (Fig. 5l–m) identifies only 55 identical SAAP sequences between human and mouse out of thousands, and the authors acknowledge this likely underestimates true conservation; however, the correlation of RAAS values for these 55 sites is presented as strong evidence of conservation, which may not generalize to the full set of substitutions.
- The paper excludes SAAP from immunoglobulins and trypsin (Methods, Sequence confirmation) without quantifying how many sites were removed or justifying why these proteins are less likely to harbor genuine alternate translation events.

## Questions
- In Fig. 3d, does the inverse correlation between RAAS and degradation rate hold within individual proteins, or only across the population of proteins? If the latter, could the correlation be driven by protein-level confounds (e.g., highly abundant proteins tend to be more stable and also more likely to have detectable substitutions)?
- For the 360 proteins reported to have RAAS > 1, what is the median RAAS value and what fraction have median RAAS > 0.5 or > 0.1? How sensitive is this count to the choice of threshold?
- Can the authors provide a multivariate regression model predicting RAAS from codon frequency, RNA modification status, and sequence motif presence, with standardized coefficients, to quantify the independent contribution of each mechanism?
- What is the false-negative rate of the SAAP identification pipeline? The paper reports that ~40% of SAAP discovered by dependent peptide search were validated by standard database search, but does not estimate how many true SAAP were missed by the stringent filtering criteria.
