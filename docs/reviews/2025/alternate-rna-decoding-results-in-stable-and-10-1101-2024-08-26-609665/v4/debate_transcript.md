## Advocate — round 1

The panel's own strengths sections concede the core of this paper: reviewers across clarity, methodology, novelty, reproducibility, and rigor independently praise the same six-pronged validation strategy (ionization efficiency, detectability, multi-digest reproducibility, shared-peptide triangulation, retention-time prediction, degradation rates) as 'difficult to dismiss on technical grounds' and 'substantially reducing false positive risk.' That convergence across five independent specialist reviews is itself strong evidence this is a rigorous, publishable contribution, not a house built on sand. The rigor reviewer explicitly calls the disorder/conservation/gnomAD associations 'consistent with a functional role and argues against these being random noise.' The novelty reviewer calls the finding 'novel and significant relative to prior work in microorganisms' and says the work is 'publishable as is with appropriate framing of uncertainty.'

Critically, the manuscript already does what good science should: it self-limits its strongest claims. It explicitly separates the 8,801-site exploratory set from the 2,000-site high-confidence (positional probability >0.9) set, and further reports 590 substitutions surviving the most stringent filtering with RAAS>0.1 (Discussion). It states plainly that for sites with ambiguous localization, 'unknown combinatorial modifications cannot be rigorously disproved.' This is not overclaiming dressed as certainty—it's a tiered evidentiary structure, exactly what reviewers should want.

The statistical-reporting gaps (missing effect sizes, unspecified correction family sizes, model validation details) are real but are revision-fixable: they require the authors to report numbers already computed, not new experiments. None of the reviewers argue the underlying analyses are wrong—only incompletely disclosed. That is a manuscript revision issue, not a rejection issue.

**Key points:**
- Five independent reviewers converge on praising the same multi-pronged validation strategy as substantially reducing false-positive risk
- The manuscript already tiers its claims by confidence (8,801 exploratory vs. 2,000 high-confidence vs. 590 ultra-stringent sites), demonstrating appropriate epistemic humility rather than overclaiming
- Novelty reviewer explicitly states the work is 'publishable as is with appropriate framing of uncertainty'
- Statistical disclosure gaps (effect sizes, correction thresholds, model CI reporting) are revision-fixable reporting omissions, not evidence the analyses are flawed
- Cross-species conservation and disorder/conservation associations, while limited in scope, provide genuine orthogonal support against the null hypothesis of pure noise

## Skeptic — round 1

The advocate is right that the validation pipeline for peptide identity is strong—I concede that point. But acceptance turns on whether the paper's headline mechanistic and specificity claims survive scrutiny, and here three independent reviewers converge on problems that are not mere 'missing disclosure.' The data_analysis reviewer calculates that Fig. 4b,c and Extended Data Fig. 5's tissue/substitution-type comparisons involve roughly 1,000 implicit tests (255 substitution types × ~4 tissues), yet the paper reports uncorrected-looking thresholds (q≤0.01) and touts individual results like 'G→S in pancreas' (p<10⁻¹⁵) as tissue-specific. If proper family-wise correction changes which comparisons survive, the tissue- and cancer-specificity claims that are marketed in the Abstract ('exhibit strong tissue-type and cancer specificity') could evaporate — this is a substantive analytical gap, not a reporting formality, since the authors may not have actually applied appropriate correction.  Second, and more fundamental: the methodology, reproducibility, and rigor reviewers independently identify that the causal claim 'protein stability contributes to substitution abundance' is confounded by detection/selection bias — only stabilized SAAP are abundant enough to be detected at all, so the correlation in Fig. 3d could be an artifact of the measurement threshold rather than evidence that substitution causes stabilization. The authors' own Discussion admits 'signal to noise in the degradation rates is smaller' without resolving this. That three specialists flag the same confound independently, without the advocate's rebuttal round addressing it at all, suggests it's a live wound, not a settled point. This is fixable only by substantially softening the causal language in the Abstract/Discussion — the current framing overclaims what correlational SILAC data in four cell types can support.

**Key points:**
- Conceded: the multi-pronged peptide-identity validation pipeline is genuinely strong
- Unaddressed: ~1,000 implicit statistical comparisons in Fig 4b,c/Ext Fig 5 with unclear family-wise correction threaten the tissue/cancer-specificity claims central to the Abstract — FIXABLE only if correction is actually redone, not just disclosed
- Unaddressed: methodology, reproducibility, and rigor reviewers independently flag that the stability→abundance causal claim (Fig 3d) is confounded by detection/selection bias, since only stabilized SAAP would be observable — this undermines a headline mechanistic claim
- The advocate's round 1 argument did not engage with either of these two specific, convergent objections, only with the general validation strategy

## Advocate — round 2

Two concessions from the skeptic actually help my case: the peptide-identity pipeline is 'genuinely strong,' and even the two new objections are more modest than framed. On multiple comparisons: the skeptic claims the paper 'reports uncorrected-looking thresholds,' but the Methods (‘Substitution enrichment in tissue types’) state explicitly that 'p-values were adjusted with a Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction to get q-values' before applying the q≤0.01 threshold used for claims like G→S in pancreas. Correction was applied; what's missing is disclosure of the family size, which is a reporting fix, not a redo of the analysis — exactly the reproducibility reviewer's complaint ('does not disclose the family size'), not a claim the correction is absent or wrong.

On stability/detection confounding: the authors don't rest this claim on Fig. 3d alone. Fig. 3e-f specifically compares degradation rates of *shared* peptides — which are detected regardless of SAAP abundance — against BP and SAAP across RAAS bins, showing shared-peptide turnover tracks SAAP turnover only when RAAS is high. This design partially neutralizes the selection-bias critique, since shared-peptide detectability doesn't depend on the SAAP crossing a detection threshold. Moreover, the Abstract already hedges appropriately: stability is listed as one of four 'mechanisms contributing to substitution abundance,' not the sole causal driver — precisely the cautious framing reviewers should want.

Both objections identify real gaps in disclosure and could sharpen the causal language further, but neither shows the underlying findings are wrong — they show where revision should tighten reporting and phrasing, not where the paper should be rejected.

## Skeptic — round 2

The advocate's rebuttal narrows rather than resolves both objections. On multiple comparisons: the BH correction the advocate cites is described only in the 'Substitution enrichment in tissue types' Methods section — it does not cover Fig. 4b,c or Fig. 5c, where the Methods ('RAAS dotplots') state p-values come from raw two-sided t-tests with dot-size scaled to -log10(p) 'up to a cutoff,' with no correction mentioned. Yet these are the figures generating the headline claims about substitution-type and functional-domain specificity that the data_analysis reviewer flagged as tested ~hundreds of times without disclosed correction. So even granting the advocate's point about the tissue-enrichment analysis specifically, the broader dotplot-based claims remain uncorrected on the record — this is a real analytical gap, not just a disclosure omission.

On stability/detection confounding: the advocate concedes the shared-peptide analysis only 'partially neutralizes' the selection-bias critique — which is itself an admission the concern stands. Critically, the shared-peptide comparison in Fig. 3e-f is still restricted to the cohort of SAAP-BP pairs that were already detected and passed the paper's stringent filters; the underlying selection (only stable-enough substitutions get identified as SAAP in the first place) operates upstream of which peptide type is compared, so shared-peptide detectability doesn't escape it. Three independent reviewers (methodology, reproducibility, rigor) converged on this same confound; the advocate's answer softens but doesn't dissolve it.

Both issues are FIXABLE with revision (redo corrections properly, soften causal language to match correlational evidence), but neither is resolved as currently written, so 'publish as is' is not warranted.
