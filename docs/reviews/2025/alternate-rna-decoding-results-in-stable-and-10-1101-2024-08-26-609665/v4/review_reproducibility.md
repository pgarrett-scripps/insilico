# Reproducibility Reviewer

## Summary
This large-scale proteomics study identifies thousands of amino acid substitutions in mammalian proteins and argues they arise from alternate translation rather than genetic mutations. The work is methodologically sophisticated with extensive validation, but reproducibility is compromised by incomplete specification of critical computational steps and partial data availability. The central claim—that high-abundance substitutions are stabilized by the substitution itself—rests on correlative evidence from degradation rates that cannot exclude confounding mechanisms, and the authors acknowledge this limitation without fully resolving it.

## Strengths
- The authors provide extensive validation across six independent lines of evidence (ionization efficiency, peptide detectability, multiple digests, PepQuery, retention time prediction, shared peptide consistency) that strengthen confidence in RAAS quantification despite inherent MS limitations.
- Code and data are deposited at a named repository (decode.slavovlab.net) with a GitHub link, and supplemental tables are comprehensive; this is substantially better than 'available on request' and enables partial reproducibility.
- The degradation-rate analysis using SILAC-labeled cells (Fig. 3c–f) directly measures protein turnover rather than relying solely on abundance ratios, providing mechanistic support for the stability hypothesis that abundance alone cannot establish.

## Weaknesses
- **Central claim—stability explains high RAAS—rests on correlative degradation data that cannot exclude confounding.** The authors show that substituted peptides with high RAAS have lower degradation rates than their base counterparts (Fig. 3d, r < 10^−10), and interpret this as evidence that substitutions stabilize proteins and thus accumulate. However, this correlation does not establish causation: a substitution could be abundant because it is synthesized at high rates (e.g., due to codon frequency or tRNA availability—mechanisms the authors themselves identify in Fig. 3a–b), and proteins synthesized at high rates may happen to be more stable due to selection against misfolding during rapid synthesis, or may occupy different cellular compartments or complexes that protect them. The authors state (Discussion) that 'protein stability variation is only one of the factors contributing to RAAS' and note 'the signal to noise in the degradation rates is smaller compared to the abundance measures,' but they do not test whether the observed degradation differences are a cause or a consequence of high RAAS. A direct perturbation (e.g., destabilizing the substituted form via mutation, or stabilizing the base form via synonymous codon optimization) would be needed to establish causation; the current data are consistent with it but do not prove it.
- **Computational pipeline for identifying substitutions lacks sufficient procedural detail to reproduce the filtering and validation steps.** The dependent peptide search, validation search, and six filtering criteria (Methods, 'Candidate SAAP and PTM identification') are described in prose, but critical thresholds and decision logic are not fully specified: (1) the 'positional probability' threshold for assigning a mass shift to a single residue is not stated numerically; (2) the fragmentation-pattern comparison using Prosit (Supplemental Fig. 4) relies on cosine similarity, but the threshold for accepting a SAAP over an alternative hypothesis is not given; (3) the 6-frame translation filter (excluding sequences matching any genomic region) is described but the exact matching criteria (e.g., E-value, identity threshold) are not provided; (4) the 1% FDR 'computed only across SAAP' (Methods) is mentioned but the algorithm (target-decoy, permutation, or other) is not specified. Without these details, an independent group cannot reproduce the exact set of 8,801 substitution sites reported.
- **Raw MS data availability is incomplete for reproducibility.** The manuscript states that CPTAC data were 'downloaded from NIH Proteomic Data Commons' and label-free data from 'dataset repository' (PXD010154, PXD030983), but does not confirm that all raw .raw files are publicly accessible at those repositories with no access restrictions. The Methods section does not state whether the authors have deposited their own processed data (e.g., MaxQuant evidence.txt, identified SAAP sequences with localization probabilities) in a public repository; Supplemental Data Tables are referenced but their availability is not explicitly confirmed. For the IP-MS validation (Supplemental Fig. 5), raw files are stated to be 'downloaded from the bioplex project' but no accession numbers are provided. Without confirmed public access to raw data and processed intermediate outputs (e.g., MaxQuant search results before filtering), independent verification of the filtering pipeline is not feasible.
- **Custom Python scripts for mining DP search results and generating sample-specific databases are not deposited with version control or a DOI.** The Methods section references 'custom python scripts' and 'Galaxy-sourced python scripts' (e.g., gffcompare_to_bed.py, translate_bed.py, fasta_merge_files_and_filter_unique_sequences.py) but does not provide links to these scripts or confirm they are in the GitHub repository; the GitHub link (github.com/SlavovLab/decode) is mentioned but not verified to contain all scripts with clear version tags. The 'custom python scripts including functions adapted from ref. 13' for identifying AAS are particularly critical but are not separately documented. Without access to the exact code and version used, the filtering logic cannot be independently audited.
- **Transcriptome assembly parameters and quality control thresholds are under-specified.** StringTie assembly is stated to use 'abundance at least 0.1% of the most abundant transcript at that locus' and 'at least 1 independent read,' but the impact of these thresholds on the final protein database is not quantified (e.g., how many transcripts were included/excluded, how does this affect the number of candidate SAAP?). The 'mean sequence coverage for all transcripts exceeds 98%' is reported, but per-sample coverage distributions and the number of transcripts with <98% coverage are not provided, making it unclear whether low-coverage regions could harbor undetected substitutions.
- **Prosit intensity prediction and cosine similarity thresholds for fragmentation-pattern validation are not numerically specified.** Supplemental Fig. 4 shows examples of SAAP vs. alternative-hypothesis spectra compared by cosine similarity, but the threshold used to accept a SAAP (e.g., cosine > 0.7) is not stated in the Methods, and no sensitivity analysis (e.g., how many SAAP would be rejected if the threshold were raised) is provided.
- **The 'histone ruler' copy-number estimation (Fig. 2i) assumes 30×10^6 histone copies per cell without justification or sensitivity analysis.** This estimate is critical for interpreting the biological significance of high-RAAS substitutions (hundreds to tens of thousands of copies per cell), but the source and variability of the 30×10^6 figure are not discussed, and no bounds on the estimate are provided.
- **Statistical tests for RAAS comparisons across datasets and tissues use Welch's t-test on log-normal distributions without justification.** The Methods state that 'the global distribution of log10(RAAS) is approximately log-normal' and comparisons are made with 't-tests (Welch approximation),' but no normality test or justification for using t-tests on log-transformed data is provided; permutation tests or non-parametric alternatives might be more appropriate for skewed distributions.
