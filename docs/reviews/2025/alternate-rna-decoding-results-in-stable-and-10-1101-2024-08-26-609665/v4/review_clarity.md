# Clarity & Presentation Reviewer

## Summary
This manuscript is clearly written overall and its central claims are well-supported by the evidence presented. The authors identify thousands of amino acid substitutions in mammalian proteomes via mass spectrometry, quantify their abundance relative to canonical peptides, and demonstrate that protein stability contributes to their accumulation. The presentation is generally transparent about methods and limitations. One significant load-bearing weakness concerns the interpretation of high RAAS values as evidence that alternatively translated proteoforms are the dominant form — alternative explanations involving undetected genetic variants or combinatorial modifications are not fully excluded — but the authors acknowledge this caveat and provide multiple lines of supporting evidence. The manuscript would benefit from clearer upfront statement of the central claim and slightly tighter organization in places.

## Strengths
- The authors explicitly acknowledge their stringent filtering strategy and its trade-offs (false negatives vs. false positives), and they transparently report both the high-confidence subset (2,000 sites with >0.9 localization probability) and the broader set (8,801 sites), allowing readers to calibrate confidence appropriately.
- Multiple independent validation approaches are clearly described and their results reported: database search validation, PepQuery peptide-centric search, IP-MS experiments, multiple protease digestion, and degradation rate analysis all converge on the same conclusions, making the evidence difficult to dismiss on technical grounds alone.
- The quantification method (RAAS as the ratio of substituted to base peptide abundance) is well-motivated by the steady-state protein model, and the authors test its internal consistency through six complementary analyses (ionization efficiency, detectability, shared peptide abundance, retention time prediction, multiple digests, and correlation patterns across patients).

## Weaknesses
- The central claim that alternatively translated proteoforms are 'the most abundant protein products for 360 proteins' (Abstract and Results) rests on the interpretation of RAAS > 1 as evidence that the substituted form dominates. However, the authors cannot fully exclude the possibility that some high-RAAS observations result from undetected genetic polymorphisms (despite filtering against gnomAD, they acknowledge 'polymorphisms cannot account for the substitutions'), combinatorial modifications on the same peptide adding up to the observed mass shift, or systematic biases in peptide ionization or detectability that differ between substituted and base peptides in ways not captured by their models. The authors address each concern individually (Extended Data Fig. 2d-i shows ionization and detectability are similar), but the cumulative case that high RAAS reflects true proteoform dominance rather than a measurement artifact or confound is circumstantial. What would definitively rule out the hypothesis that most high-RAAS observations reflect undetected genetic variants or combinatorial modifications rather than alternate translation?
- The manuscript does not state its central contribution explicitly in the Introduction or early Results. The reader must infer from the Abstract and scattered statements that the paper's main claim is that (1) alternate translation produces thousands of abundant proteins, and (2) protein stability explains why some substitutions accumulate despite low synthesis rates. This should be stated as a clear hypothesis before the results section, not left implicit.
- Figure 1 is dense and difficult to parse without extensive caption reading; the workflow diagram would benefit from clearer labeling of decision points (e.g., which filters remove which candidates) and the relationship between the dependent peptide search and validation search is not immediately clear from the figure alone.
- The term 'alternate translation' is used throughout but is never formally defined in the main text; readers must infer from context that it means 'translation deviating from the genetic code' but the exact mechanisms (wobble pairing, tRNA mischarging, codon recoding via RNA modifications) are discussed only later and not unified under a single clear definition.
- The claim that RAAS values are 'positively associated with intrinsically disordered regions' (Abstract) is supported by Fig. 5h but the effect size and statistical significance are not stated in the main text, only in the figure legend.
- The paper identifies 60,803 spectra corresponding to 8,801 unique substitution sites but then focuses analysis on 2,000 high-confidence sites; the relationship between these numbers and how they relate to the 360 proteins with RAAS > 1 is not clearly explained in the Results section.
- The conservation analysis (Fig. 5l-m) identifies only 55 identical SAAP sequences between human and mouse despite analyzing thousands of sites; the authors acknowledge this likely underestimates true conservation but do not quantify how much of the apparent lack of conservation is due to technical factors (peptide detectability, sequence homology) versus true biological divergence.

## Questions
- Can the authors provide a quantitative estimate of how many of the high-RAAS substitutions would be expected if the observed mass shifts resulted from undetected genetic variants at frequencies below the gnomAD detection threshold, or from combinatorial modifications?
- In Fig. 2e, the shared peptide abundance for RAAS > 1 is claimed to be 'closer to SAAP abundance than BP abundance,' but no quantitative measure of 'closeness' (e.g., fold-difference, correlation coefficient) is provided; what is the actual magnitude of this effect?
- The authors state that 'for about half of all spectra matched to SAAP, the measured mass shift indicating a substitution is localized with high confidence to a single position' but do not specify whether the high-RAAS substitutions are enriched in this high-confidence subset or distributed across both high- and low-confidence groups.
