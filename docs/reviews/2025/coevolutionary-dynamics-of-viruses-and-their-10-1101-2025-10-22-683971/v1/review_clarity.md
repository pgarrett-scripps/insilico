# Clarity & Presentation Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript is clearly written overall and the mathematical framework is well-presented, with good use of figures to illustrate dynamics. The central claims are stated explicitly and the methods section is detailed enough to follow. However, there are several presentation issues that create friction: inconsistent notation between dimensional and nondimensional forms, some figures lack sufficient detail in legends, and the connection between abstract model parameters and concrete biological mechanisms could be clearer in places. These are fixable issues that do not undermine the work but slow comprehension.

## Strengths
- The paper clearly states its central contribution upfront (coevolutionary chase dynamics in virus-DIP systems) and maintains a coherent narrative from motivation through results to implications.
- Figures 3 and 4 effectively communicate the core dynamics through multiple complementary views (population time series, phenotype trajectories, and spatial density heatmaps), with good use of color and layout.
- The nondimensionalization is mathematically sound and well-explained, with Table 2 providing explicit derivations that aid reproducibility.

## Weaknesses
- The load-bearing claim is that the model 'captures strong-mutation regimes' (line 49) where continuous mutation allows coevolution of both virus and DIP. However, the distinction from classical adaptive dynamics is asserted rather than demonstrated: the manuscript never shows that a discrete-mutation model would fail to produce chase dynamics, or that the diffusion-based approach is necessary rather than convenient. The claim rests on the assumption that 'mutations occur frequently and generate broad polymorphism' (lines 55–56), but no evidence is provided that the baseline parameters (μ = 10⁻³, η = 10⁻³) actually satisfy this regime in real virus-DIP systems. Without a quantitative comparison to experimental mutation rates or a sensitivity analysis showing that discrete mutations fail to produce the observed outcomes, the necessity of the strong-mutation framework remains unsubstantiated.
- The second load-bearing claim is that the model reproduces 'transient resistance' observed in DePolo et al. (1987) time-shift experiments (lines 31–38, Fig 6). The model does produce cyclical resistance patterns, but the quantitative match is acknowledged as poor: 'modeled resistance changes are on a linear scale, not the orders-of-magnitude changes experimentally observed' (lines 444–445). The authors attribute this to 2D phenotype space being too constrained (lines 448–449), but this is a post-hoc explanation, not a validation. The figure caption for Fig 6c states the pattern 'matched' the experimental data, yet the text admits the match is qualitative only. This overstates the evidence: a cyclical pattern is not the same as the specific transient resistance signature, and the orders-of-magnitude discrepancy is substantial enough that a reader cannot determine whether the model captures the mechanism or merely produces superficially similar oscillations.
- The third load-bearing claim is that 'evolutionary conflict' (high α) can trap viruses at the fitness optimum and prevent escape (Fig 7b). The model shows that when α > 0.5, phenotypic distance remains constant regardless of κ. However, the biological interpretation conflates two distinct mechanisms: (1) the virus cannot escape because the fitness cost is prohibitive, and (2) the virus does not escape because the DIP population is too weak to drive selection. The model does not isolate these—when α is high, both the virus and DIP populations are suppressed (Fig 7a), so the lack of escape could reflect DIP extinction rather than evolutionary constraint. A comparison of virus-DIP phenotypic distance in the 'trapped' regime to a control where DIPs are absent would clarify whether the virus is genuinely constrained by fitness costs or simply not under selective pressure.

## Questions
- Lines 162–166 state that newly generated DIPs 'inherit the exact phenotype of its parent virus' because major phenotypic changes would render them non-viable; what evidence supports that viable DIPs must remain phenotypically similar to their parent, and how sensitive are the results to relaxing this assumption?
- The zero-sum resource transfer (Eq. 4) is justified as a 'neutral baseline' (line 122), but the authors note DIPs could be more or less efficient than viruses (lines 123–125); does the choice of zero-sum versus positive/negative-sum substantially alter the regimes identified in Fig. 5, or is it truly neutral?
- Figure 5 shows mutation rate μ has 'negligible effect' (line 329), but the baseline μ = 10⁻³ is not justified against experimental RNA virus mutation rates; is this parameter biologically plausible, and would results change under higher mutation rates typical of RNA viruses?
- The aggregation penalty β (Eq. 10) is motivated by quasispecies cohesion (lines 144–151), but this is an assumption about viral population structure, not a derived consequence; how does the model behave if β = 0, and does the choice of β value affect the qualitative regimes?
