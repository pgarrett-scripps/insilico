## Concessions
- **literature** — The manuscript claims the model 'reproduces' or 'matches' the DePolo et al. (1987) transient resistance pattern, but the text itself admits the modeled resistance changes are linear-scale while experimental data show orders-of-magnitude shifts.
  - Proposed change: Revise the Abstract, Introduction, and the Fig 6 caption to replace 'reproduces'/'matched' with 'qualitatively reproduces the cyclical/transient structure of resistance but underestimates its magnitude.' Add a quantitative metric to the Results text (e.g., ratio of modeled peak-to-baseline resistance fold-change vs. the experimentally reported fold-change) so readers can assess the size of the discrepancy rather than inferring it from prose.
- **methodology** — The paper never demonstrates that continuous phenotype-diffusion under strong mutation is necessary to produce von Magnus oscillations and chase dynamics, rather than merely one sufficient mechanism (no comparison to a discrete-mutation or fixed-trait predator-prey null model).
  - Proposed change: Add a supplementary simulation of a simplified null variant (e.g., μ reduced toward zero with discrete stochastic trait substitutions, or a fixed-phenotype virus/DIP predator-prey ODE lacking the diffusion term) run at matched parameters, and report whether oscillatory/chase behavior is attenuated or absent. Temper the framing in the Introduction and Discussion from 'requires' to 'we show this framework is sufficient to generate...; a discrete-substitution comparison (Supplementary Fig SX) shows diminished/absent chase dynamics under the same parameters.'
- **data_analysis** — The LHS sensitivity analysis (n=10,000) reports regime proportions without confidence intervals, stated random seeds, or a convergence check, and the extinction/chase classification thresholds are not justified or sensitivity-tested.
  - Proposed change: Report bootstrap confidence intervals on the stacked proportions in Fig 5a-f, state the random seed(s) used for LHS sampling in Methods, add a convergence plot showing regime proportions at n=2,000/5,000/10,000 samples, and include a supplementary sensitivity check varying the chase threshold (centroid-distance variation cutoff) and extinction threshold by an order of magnitude to show the classification is not an artifact of the specific cutoffs chosen.
- **literature** — The manuscript does not clearly delineate which model components are novel versus directly adapted from Alfaro et al. [39].
  - Proposed change: Add an explicit paragraph (or table) in the Methods section stating: the diffusion term, centering (α) and aggregation (β) fitness terms are adapted from Alfaro et al. [39]; the Gaussian convolution interference terms (Eqs 1-3), the de novo DIP generation term (η), and the zero-sum resource-transfer identity (Eq 4) are novel additions introduced in this work.
- **clarity** — The evolutionary-conflict analysis (Fig 7) conflates two distinct mechanisms for lack of viral escape at high α: prohibitive fitness cost versus insufficient DIP-driven selection pressure due to DIP suppression.
  - Proposed change: Add a control simulation in which the DIP population is held at a fixed nonzero density independent of α (decoupling DIP abundance from the centering penalty), and report the resulting virus-DIP phenotypic distance in a new supplementary figure to isolate whether high-α 'trapping' reflects a genuine fitness constraint rather than an artifact of DIP extinction.
- **literature** — Several citation-hygiene issues reduce verifiability: the DePolo et al. (1987) reference lacks a journal name, the Giachetti & Holland (1988) finding is cited without direct quotation, and the Pelz et al. (2021) experimental system is not specified in-text.
  - Proposed change: Correct the DePolo et al. reference to include the journal name (Journal of Virology), add a direct paraphrase of the Giachetti & Holland (1988) finding (polymerase mutations reducing DIP-template affinity while preserving genome replication) in the sentence citing ref 32, and specify in the sentence citing Pelz et al. that their system was semi-continuous bioreactor propagation of influenza A virus and its DIPs.
- **novelty** — The zero-sum resource-transfer assumption and the equal-carrying-capacity-contribution assumption for virus and DIP are untested against alternatives, leaving their effect on the Fig 5 regime boundaries unknown.
  - Proposed change: Add a supplementary LHS sweep varying a resource-transfer efficiency multiplier on Eq (2) (spanning negative-sum to positive-sum regimes) and a per-particle carrying-capacity weight favoring DIPs, then report whether the four-regime boundaries in Fig 5 shift materially, in a new supplementary figure and a paragraph in the Discussion.

## Disagreements
- **rigor** — The claim that high α traps the virus at the fitness optimum is dismissed as tautological because α is defined as a penalty for deviation from the origin, so 'of course' high α prevents escape.
  - Response: The threshold behavior in Fig 7b is not a restatement of the model's definition but a nontrivial, quantitative joint function of α and κ: the manuscript shows that escape only occurs once κ exceeds a specific α-dependent threshold, and that above a critical α (~0.5) no finite κ tested induces escape. That the *location* of this threshold and its interaction with interference strength is not fixed by the definition of α alone but emerges from simulation is exactly the predictive content we claim—an empirically testable relationship between escape-cost and interference-strength, not a tautology.
  - Manuscript reference: 'Below this α threshold, however, a sufficiently high interference cost (κ) forces the virus to abandon the optimum and engage in a coevolutionary chase (Fig 7b)... In the “trapped” regime (high α), increasing κ effectively suppresses the viral population. In the “escape” regime (low α), the virus population is less affected by κ because it can simply evolve away from the interference with minimal loss of fitness.'
- **literature** — The historical foundation citing von Magnus and early oscillatory-dynamics papers (refs 1-10) 'cannot be verified through modern indexing,' implying the claims may be inaccurate or unverifiable.
  - Response: These are pre-1955 publications predating PubMed and arXiv indexing entirely; their absence from modern search indices is expected for that era and is not evidence against their accuracy. The specific claims attributed to them (progressive enrichment of defective particles, drop in infectious titer) are independently corroborated by the subsequent review literature we also cite, which directly discusses and re-derives the von Magnus phenomenon.
  - Manuscript reference: 'In a landmark series of experiments, Preben von Magnus showed that undiluted passage cultures of influenza virus led to the accumulation of non-infectious “incomplete” particles that interfered with standard virus replication [1–3]. This progressive enrichment of defective particles and corresponding drop in infectious virus output came to be known as the von Magnus effect [4].' The claim is further corroborated by refs 7 (Roux & Simon, Adv Virus Res 1991) and 8 (Bangham & Kirkwood, Trends Microbiol 1993), both independent reviews of the same phenomenon.
- **novelty** — Mutation rate (μ) having negligible effect on outcomes (Fig 5f) is presented as an unexamined oddity, suggesting either too narrow a parameter range or unexplained model insensitivity.
  - Response: This point is already explicitly addressed in the Results text, which attributes the weak effect to the chosen parameter bounds and notes that other ranges could shift the balance—this is not an undiscussed gap but a stated caveat on the scope of the sensitivity analysis.
  - Manuscript reference: 'Mutation rate (µ, Fig 5f) has comparatively weaker effects: chase maintains a consistent prevalence of ∼25%, while coexistence dominates (∼70%)... The relatively low frequency of chase here reflects the chosen parameter bounds; other ranges could yield a different balance.'
- **novelty** — The sensitivity analysis is characterized as yielding 'largely intuitive results' (e.g., intermediate interference favoring chase, low decay rates favoring chase) that are mechanistically obvious and not novel.
  - Response: Several of the reported effects are not obvious a priori. The washout rate (γ) sweep shows a distinctly nonmonotonic relationship—chase and extinction both elevated at very low γ, coexistence dominant at intermediate γ, and extinction rising again at high γ—a pattern that would not be predicted from simple intuition about decay strengthening or weakening DIP pressure alone. Similarly, the de novo generation rate (η) sweep shows that continuous DIP regeneration near the virus can suppress rather than promote chase, counter to a naive expectation that more DIPs favor more interference-driven escape pressure.
  - Manuscript reference: 'The washout rate (γ, Fig 5e) exhibits the most complex effects. At low values, chase is highly prevalent (nearly 50%) alongside elevated extinction rates... As γ increases, extinction decreases and coexistence grows more common... However, at the highest values, strong washout suppresses both populations and again increases extinction. This reveals that γ tunes the relative balance of viral escape and DIP persistence in a strongly nonlinear manner.'

## Load-bearing critiques
- If the panel requires a strict quantitative (order-of-magnitude) match between the model's predicted resistance dynamics and the DePolo et al. time-shift data as a precondition for accepting the paper's central mechanistic claims, this cannot be delivered within the current revision cycle: closing that gap would require developing and validating a higher-dimensional 'corkscrew trajectory' extension, which is beyond the scope of a revision and is explicitly flagged in the Discussion as future work rather than a deliverable now.
