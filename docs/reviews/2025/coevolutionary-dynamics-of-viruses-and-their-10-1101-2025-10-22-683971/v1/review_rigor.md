# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
This is a well-executed theoretical study that makes a solid contribution to understanding virus-DIP coevolution through a phenotype-space model. The core findings—that interference strength and decay rates determine whether populations exhibit chase dynamics, coexistence, or extinction—are clearly demonstrated and novel. However, the abstract and several key claims overstate what the model actually establishes. The model is abstract and two-dimensional, the connection to experimental data is qualitative rather than quantitative, and critical biological assumptions (isotropic mutation, equal resource costs, inherited phenotypes in de novo DIPs) are not validated. The work merits publication with revision to temper overclaiming and clarify the scope of what the model predicts.

## Strengths
- The authors transparently acknowledge limitations (two-dimensional phenotype space, deterministic framework, Gaussian kernel choice) and test robustness by showing similar dynamics under alternative kernels and higher dimensions, strengthening confidence in the core mechanism.
- The sensitivity analysis (Latin Hypercube Sampling, n=10,000) systematically maps parameter space to four regimes and identifies which parameters most strongly promote chase dynamics, providing actionable predictions for therapeutic design.
- The model successfully reproduces two experimentally observed phenomena—von Magnus oscillations and transient cyclical resistance patterns from time-shift experiments—grounding the abstract framework in real biology.

## Weaknesses
- **Load-bearing claim 1: Chase Red Queen dynamics.** The abstract states the model 'reveals…coevolutionary chases in which viruses temporarily escape and new DIPs emerge to follow, as observed in serial-passage evolution studies.' The evidence is Figs 3d–f and 4, which show sustained oscillations in phenotypic distance and population size under one set of initial conditions (virus and DIP phenotypically separated). However, the same model with different initial conditions (Fig 2d–f, or DIPs emerging de novo in Fig 3a–c) produces convergence to the origin with no sustained chase. The claim that chase dynamics are 'observed in serial-passage evolution studies' is supported only qualitatively by Fig 6c, which reproduces the *pattern* of transient resistance from DePolo et al. (1987) but not the *magnitude*—the authors themselves note the model produces linear-scale changes while experiments show orders-of-magnitude shifts (lines 443–447). The alternative explanation is that chase dynamics are a transient artifact of the specific initial condition (phenotypic separation) rather than a robust attractor of the system. The sensitivity analysis (Fig 5) shows chase occurs in only ~20% of parameter space at intermediate κ, and the authors acknowledge (line 338) that 'coexistence and extinction represent more robust attractors.' This undermines the framing that chase is a central predicted outcome. The claim should be reworded to specify the initial conditions required and the parameter ranges where chase is favored, or downgraded to 'can exhibit' rather than 'reveals.'
- **Load-bearing claim 2: Evolutionary conflict and viral escape thresholds.** The abstract claims the model 'predicts thresholds where viral escape is either constrained by intrinsic fitness penalties or enabled through phenotypic separation from DIPs.' Fig 7 shows that high α (fitness penalty) prevents escape and low α permits it, but this is a direct consequence of the model's design: the α term is defined as a penalty for deviation from the origin (line 139), so of course high α prevents escape. This is not a prediction; it is a tautology built into the model structure. The biological claim—that escape mutations incur fitness costs—is well-established (cited: refs 27, 32, 51) and the model formalizes it rather than predicting it. What the model does predict is the *quantitative relationship* between α and escape distance (Fig 7b), but the authors do not validate whether the α values they use match real biological systems. They note (lines 431–441) that applying the model to a specific virus requires 'translating its abstract phenotype space into one defined by concrete, measurable biological traits,' but provide no worked example or parameter-fitting procedure. The claim that the model 'predicts thresholds' should be qualified: it shows that *if* escape mutations incur costs proportional to α, *then* high α prevents escape—a conditional prediction, not a discovery.
- **Load-bearing claim 3: Implications for DIP-based therapeutics.** The abstract states findings have 'implications for designing DIP-based therapeutics that better resist viral escape.' The sensitivity analysis (Fig 5, lines 476–485) identifies parameter ranges favoring viral extinction or stable coexistence, but does not test whether these regimes are achievable in practice. The authors recommend 'high interference and high costs to evolutionary escape' (line 478), but acknowledge (lines 484–485) that 'TIP failure is most likely to occur through low costs to escape (e.g., targeting non-conserved viral functions in high-mutation environments).' This is a restatement of the problem, not a solution. The model does not predict *how* to engineer a TIP to achieve high α (fitness cost to escape) or high κ (interference strength) in a real virus; it only shows that if you achieve them, viral control improves. The therapeutic implication is therefore speculative. The claim should be downgraded to 'suggests design principles' or paired with explicit acknowledgment that the model does not address how to implement them.
- The model assumes de novo DIPs inherit the exact phenotype of their parent virus (lines 161–166), justified by the requirement for 'significant functional similarity.' This is not validated: real defective genomes arise from deletions and mutations, which could produce phenotypic shifts. If DIPs frequently emerge with phenotypes offset from the virus, the coevolutionary dynamics could differ substantially. This assumption should be tested by relaxing it (e.g., allowing small random phenotypic shifts in newly generated DIPs) and comparing outcomes.
- The interference kernel (Eq. 3) is a Gaussian with a single scale parameter σ, but the biological basis for this choice is stated only as an analogy to spatial ecology (line 98–100). The authors test alternative kernels (Fig S5) and find 'qualitatively similar chase dynamics,' but do not quantify how much the parameter estimates or regime boundaries change. For a model whose predictions depend on κ and σ, robustness to kernel choice should be reported numerically, not visually.
- The model assumes virus and DIP populations contribute equally to the carrying capacity K (line 135), though DIPs have shorter genomes and may be less resource-intensive. The authors acknowledge this (lines 135–136) but do not test sensitivity to unequal contributions. If DIPs are more efficient, the logistic factor L could favor DIP growth, potentially shifting the regime boundaries shown in Fig 5.
- The claim that the model 'captures strong-mutation regimes' (abstract, line 49) is not formally validated: the authors do not show that the diffusion approximation is valid for their parameter choices, nor do they compare predictions to a stochastic individual-based model across the parameter ranges explored.
- The abstract states the model shows 'how both population dynamics and trait evolution shape outcomes,' but the two are not decoupled: the oscillations in Fig 4a are driven by the phenotypic chase in Fig 4b. The claim should specify that population dynamics and trait evolution are coupled, not independent drivers.
- Figure 6c reproduces the DePolo et al. (1987) resistance pattern qualitatively, but the caption does not state whether the model was fit to that data or whether the match is post-hoc. If post-hoc, this is a consistency check, not a validation; if fit, the fitting procedure should be described.

## Questions
- What happens to the regime boundaries (Fig 5) if DIPs are modeled as requiring fewer resources per particle than viruses (e.g., logistic factor weighted by genome length)?
- Can you provide a worked example showing how to map the abstract phenotype space (x₁, x₂) to a real biological system (e.g., VSV replicase binding affinity and packaging signal strength) and estimate α, κ, and σ from experimental data?
- How sensitive are the chase dynamics to the assumption that newly generated DIPs inherit the exact phenotype of their parent virus? What if DIPs emerge with phenotypic shifts drawn from a distribution?
- The model neglects stochasticity; how do results change if you compare the deterministic predictions to a stochastic individual-based model at the parameter values used in Figs 3–4?
