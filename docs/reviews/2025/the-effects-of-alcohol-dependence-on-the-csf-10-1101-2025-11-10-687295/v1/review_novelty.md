# Novelty & Contribution Reviewer

## Summary
The core contribution — a CSF proteomic comparison of alcohol-dependent vs. alcohol-exposed non-dependent mice in the CIE-2BC model — is real and, on the searches I could run, not preempted. I found no prior work reporting CSF proteomics in this model or in any alcohol-dependence mouse model distinguishing dependent from non-dependent animals; the closest hits were human CSF biomarker studies (Azuar et al. 2021, cited) and mechanistic BBB/neuroinflammation work (Wei et al. 2021; Togre et al. 2024, cited). The novelty is therefore genuine but modest in kind: a candidate-marker discovery list from a small, qualitative study. The main novelty-dimension problem is framing — the "dichotomous" claim is presented as a finding of this paper when the dichotomy concept is already established in the same model by the authors' own companion transcriptomic work (Hashimoto et al. 2025, cited), and the CSF data here extend rather than establish it.

## Weaknesses
- The central novelty claim — "alcohol exposure and alcohol dependence are dichotomous" — is a conceptual claim that outruns what this paper's evidence establishes, and it is partly preempted by the authors' own cited companion work. The dichotomy (dependence-specific neuroimmune activation vs. homeostatic response to moderate drinking) is already reported in the same CIE-2BC model at the astrocyte transcriptome level in Hashimoto et al. 2025, which the manuscript cites. The genuinely new delta here is the CSF proteomic evidence for that dichotomy, not the dichotomy itself. As framed ("This work provides novel protein-level evidence that..."), the claim is defensible, but the paper should state explicitly that it extends a dichotomy already established in this model rather than presenting the dichotomy as its own discovery. This is a SOFT positioning issue, not a HARD preemption — the CSF proteome comparison itself appears to be new.
- The magnitude of the contribution is overstated relative to the evidence base for the "dichotomous" framing. The dichotomy rests on a qualitative detection-based analysis (present/absent at 1% FDR) in n=4–5 mice, with the authors' own power analysis conceding only 38% power for moderate effect sizes. A claim of qualitative dichotomy — that dependence produces a categorically different proteome rather than a quantitative escalation — is a strong inference from a design that cannot distinguish "not detected" from "below detection limit" and cannot resolve moderate effect sizes. The authors acknowledge this in the Limitations, which is to their credit, but the abstract and conclusions still assert the dichotomy more firmly than the evidence supports. The framing should be scaled to "preliminary evidence consistent with a qualitative shift," which is what the data actually support. (The statistical adequacy of this is rigor's call; I flag it only as a magnitude/positioning issue.)
- Differentiation from the nearest competing work is thin. The manuscript cites human CSF studies in AUD (Azuar et al. 2021) and the mechanistic BBB literature but never names a nearest-neighbor CSF proteomics study and explains the delta over it. Given that the individual markers (GFAP, MMP2, CHI3L1, UCHL1) are all established biomarkers in other contexts, the paper's contribution is specifically the co-detection in CSF of dependent mice in this model — that should be stated as the differentiation, and a head-to-head against the closest human CSF proteomics work offered where feasible.
