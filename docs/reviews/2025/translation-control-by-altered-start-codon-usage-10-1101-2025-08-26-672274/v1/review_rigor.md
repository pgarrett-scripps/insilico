# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript presents a well-executed study of translational control via start codon usage in Listeria monocytogenes, with solid experimental work linking non-canonical start codons to SigB regulation and a large-scale genomic analysis. However, the central claims about start codon selection as a regulatory mechanism and its evolutionary significance rest on correlational evidence from genome-wide analysis that cannot establish causation or selection pressure, and the mechanistic story—that altered translation rates fine-tune virulence and stress response—is supported only for rsbW, not for the 39 genes claimed to show flexible usage under selection. The work is sound within its scope but overclaims the generality and evolutionary drivers of the phenomenon.

## Strengths
- The experimental design isolating rsbW mutations through in vitro evolution under lethal acid stress is rigorous, and the translational reporter assays (Fig. 4B–C) directly demonstrate that ATG > ATA > ATT in translation efficiency, establishing a clear mechanism for one gene.

## Weaknesses
- **Load-bearing claim 1: Start codon flexibility is 'under selection for stress response and virulence genes' (title, abstract, Results section heading).** The evidence is a genome-wide survey showing that 39 conserved genes (1.8% of 2,180) display variable start codons across 60,690 genomes, with 13 of these involved in virulence/stress. However, this is purely correlational: the authors observe that start codon variants cluster by phylogenetic clade (e.g., argB uses ATG in lineage I, GTG in lineage II) but provide no evidence that this clustering reflects selection rather than drift, founder effects, or neutral divergence during lineage splitting. The alternative—that start codon variants arose by chance and were fixed neutrally—is never excluded. The authors note that rsbW non-canonical start codons are 'rare (2 found in 60,690 genomes)' yet claim this supports selection; rarity could equally indicate these are deleterious in most contexts and only beneficial under extreme stress. No phylogenetic comparative method, no dN/dS analysis, no test of whether start codon changes correlate with known selective pressures (e.g., temperature, food vs. clinical niches) is offered. The claim that these variants 'likely contribute to an overall genomic shift that provides an incremental fitness advantage' (line 426) is speculative and unsupported by the data presented.

## Questions
- For the 39 genes with flexible start codons, what is the phylogenetic distribution—do variants cluster by clade, or are they scattered, and does that pattern distinguish selection from drift?
- The authors show ATG > ATA > ATT for rsbW but test only plcB and lftR (Fig. 5C) for canonical start codon differences; why not test the other 36 genes claimed to show flexible usage, or at least a representative sample, to establish whether translation efficiency differences are general?
- The GadR R154Q mutation in MQ140025 is noted as arising 'much earlier' in the phylogeny (line 357) and associated with a population expansion event; is this a confound for the acid resistance phenotype, and were ARDs tested for GadR status?
- Figure 3A shows additional mutations in ARD1, ARD3, and ARD12 beyond rsbW; the authors dismiss these as non-phenotypic, but were these strains re-sequenced to confirm no other rsbW-independent changes arose during the acid challenge?
