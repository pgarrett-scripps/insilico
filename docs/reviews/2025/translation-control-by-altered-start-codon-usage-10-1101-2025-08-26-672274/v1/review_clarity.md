# Clarity & Presentation Reviewer

## Summary
The manuscript is scientifically sound and addresses an interesting question about translational control in L. monocytogenes, but presentation clarity is compromised by inconsistent terminology, undefined abbreviations at first use, and structural issues that obscure the central contribution. The core findings—that non-canonical start codons reduce rsbW translation and that start codon usage varies across stress/virulence genes—are supported, but a reader must work to extract the narrative thread from dense methods and results sections.

## Strengths
- The manuscript clearly defines the experimental design for in vitro evolution (Figure 1A–B) and provides quantitative survival data with appropriate replication (three independent experiments with technical duplicates).
- Start codon usage analysis across 60,690 genomes is well-documented with transparent methodology (tblastn pipeline, >95% presence threshold, >5% flexibility criterion defined in text).
- Translational reporter assays (Figure 4B–C) directly test the mechanism proposed and show consistent dose-response (ATG > ATA > ATT) across fluorescence and Western blot readouts.

## Weaknesses
- The central claim—that altered start codons serve as a regulatory mechanism for fine-tuning SigB activity during adaptation—rests on the assumption that reduced RsbW translation increases SigB activity by perturbing stoichiometry (Figure 4D). However, the manuscript does not directly measure RsbW protein levels or RsbW:SigB ratios in the ARD strains, only infer them from reporter assays in a different genetic background (MQ140025 with pIMK3 plasmid). An alternative explanation is that the non-canonical start codons create a truncated or misfolded RsbW that is non-functional regardless of translation rate; the nonsense mutations in ARD3 and ARD5 (rsbW R23*, Y37*) produce the same SigB hyperactivity phenotype, suggesting loss-of-function rather than stoichiometric tuning. To distinguish these, the authors would need to measure endogenous RsbW protein in ARD strains or show that a synonymous mutation reducing translation rate (without changing the start codon) produces the same effect.
- The claim that start codon flexibility in 39 conserved genes represents adaptive selection for niche-specific fitness (lines 395–427) is supported only by phylogenetic association (different codons in different clades) and modest in vitro reporter differences (Figure 5C shows small reduction for GTG vs ATG in plcB and lftR). The manuscript does not demonstrate that these expression differences confer a fitness advantage in any relevant environment, nor does it exclude neutral drift or codon usage bias as alternative explanations for the observed patterns. The statement 'they likely contribute to an overall genomic shift that provides an incremental fitness advantage' (line 426) is speculative and unsupported by the data presented.
- Abbreviations and terminology are inconsistent and sometimes undefined at first use: 'GSR' appears in the abstract (line 42) without definition; 'ARD' is used in Figure 1 caption before being defined in text (line 156); 'CC' (clonal complex) and 'ST' (sequence type) are used in the introduction without explicit definition; 'PMSC' (premature stop codon) is introduced at line 208 but the acronym is never expanded in the text. 'SigB-dependent' genes are referenced (e.g., line 186) before the SigB regulon is described.
- The narrative structure obscures the central question: the introduction motivates SigB regulation and lists seven genetic targets (line 119) but does not explicitly state that the study will investigate whether translational control of these targets is a regulatory mechanism. The results section presents acid adaptation, then ARD characterization, then genome sequencing, then start codon analysis—a logical experimental sequence but not a logical argument sequence. The reader must infer that the rsbW mutations prompted the genome-wide start codon analysis; this connection is stated only retrospectively at line 248 ('The strong influence of rsbW start codon selection on SigB activity prompted us to investigate...').
- Figure 3 is difficult to parse: the circular genome map uses color coding (purple, cyan labels) that is explained only in the caption, and the distinction between mutations in MQ140025 vs. ARDs is not immediately clear from the figure itself. The legend states 'Purple labels denote mutations detected in ARD1, ARD3, and ARD5' but does not explain why these three strains are grouped separately or what the biological significance of their unique mutations is.
- The quantitative claim that 'flexible usage of start codons associated with genetic clades in 39 conserved genes' (abstract, line 47) is not precisely defined: does this mean all 39 genes show clade-specific variation, or only some? Table S1 is referenced but not provided in the manuscript, making it impossible to verify the claim or assess the distribution of start codon changes.
- Methods section does not specify the IPTG concentration used to induce pIMK3 reporters until line 516 (1 mM), creating ambiguity about whether induction conditions were consistent across all reporter experiments.
- The phylogenetic analysis of ST1 strains (Figure S1) is presented as supporting evidence for the evolutionary trajectory of MQ140025 but is not integrated into the main narrative; its relevance to the start codon hypothesis is unclear.

## Questions
- Figure 4D proposes that reduced RsbW translation perturbs RsbW:SigB stoichiometry, but how do you exclude the possibility that non-canonical start codons produce a non-functional RsbW protein (as suggested by the nonsense mutations in ARD3/ARD5 producing identical phenotypes)?
- For the 39 genes with flexible start codon usage, what is the distribution of clade-specific variation—do all 39 show the pattern, or is it a subset, and how many strains per clade were analyzed?
- Table S1 is cited as containing the complete start codon dataset but is not provided; can this be included or made accessible?
- Why were only plcB, lftR, and mntC selected for translational reporter validation among the 13 virulence/stress genes with flexible start codons?
