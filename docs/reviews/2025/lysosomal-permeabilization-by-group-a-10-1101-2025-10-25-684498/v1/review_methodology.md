# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The central finding — that cytosolic fractions from GAS-infected macrophages stimulate IL-1β secretion in naïve cells — is supported by appropriate controls (buffer, membrane fraction, uninfected cytosol) and is reproducible across independent preparations. However, the two load-bearing mechanistic claims rest on designs that do not exclude important alternatives: the attribution of the IL-1β response to M1 protein released into the cytosol confounds "M1 absent from the bacterium" with "M1 absent from the cytosol," and the histone-release claim does not rule out contamination from GAS-killed cells whose nuclei are leaky to digitonin. Both are fixable with targeted controls, but as presented the conclusions outrun the evidence.

## Strengths
- The digitonin-based cytosolic isolation is validated with organellar markers (LAMP-2, Lamin A/C), and the absence of nuclear matrix proteins in the proteomics is a good internal check.
- The use of isogenic mutant strains (ΔSLO, ΔM1) alongside WT and a heterologous species (GBS) provides a clean genetic framework for dissecting the response.
- The IL-1β transfer assay includes appropriate vehicle and membrane-fraction controls, and the ΔM1 result is a genuine attempt to identify the active component.

## Weaknesses
- The histone-release claim does not exclude dead-cell contamination of the cytosolic fraction (HARD). GAS infection — and SLO in particular, per the authors' own prior work — causes macrophage apoptosis and pyroptosis. Dead or dying cells have permeabilized plasma membranes, and their nuclei may be partially degraded or leaky. When the mixed population is lysed with digitonin, contents of dead cells (including histones) would enter the "cytosolic" fraction. The absence of Lamin A/C does not rule this out: Lamin A/C is ~70 kD and would not leak from a nucleus whose small pores admit 10–23 kD histones. The authors report no viability or LDH data at harvest, and no correlation between histone signal and cell death. The GBS control shows specificity to GAS but not to live-cell permeabilization — GBS also kills macrophages. What would settle it: report viability at harvest and show that the histone signal tracks with the live-cell permeabilization readout (e.g., cathepsin B release) rather than with the dead-cell fraction; or isolate cytosol from cells where death is blocked (e.g., a pancaspase inhibitor) and show histones persist.
- The M1 attribution confounds "M1 released into cytosol" with "M1 present on the bacterium" (HARD). The IL-1β comparison is WT cytosol vs ΔM1 cytosol. The ΔM1 strain lacks M1 on its surface entirely, which changes its phagocytosis, survival, and secreted-protein repertoire — none of which are measured. The reduced IL-1β from ΔM1 cytosol could reflect lower intracellular bacterial load or altered bacterial protein release, not the specific absence of cytosolic M1. The proteomics finding (M1 in WT but not ΔSLO cytosol) is the evidence for *release*, but the ΔSLO strain still expresses M1, so this comparison does support release — the gap is that the IL-1β experiment uses a different strain (ΔM1) whose phenotype is not characterized. What would settle it: report intracellular bacterial counts for WT and ΔM1 at harvest, and/or spike recombinant M1 into ΔM1 cytosol and show the response is restored.
- The nucleosome claim is weakened by the absence of DNA (SOFT). The authors argue that histones must be released as nucleosomes because they used a 30 kD MWCO and found all histone subunits. But individual histones are 10–23 kD and would pass the filter as free proteins. The DNA quantification shows no increase in cytosolic DNA in infected cells; the authors invoke GAS DNase to explain this, but do not test it (no DNase inhibitor, no measurement of DNA degradation). The data are equally consistent with free histone release, which would not require nucleosome-sized complexes. What would settle it: size-fractionate the cytosol (e.g., native gel or SEC) and show histones co-migrate with DNA, or add a DNase inhibitor during infection and show DNA then appears in the cytosol.
- The methods state cytosolic fractions were exposed to cells for 24 hrs, but the Fig. 4 legend says 2 hours — this timing difference matters for whether the response reflects processing of pre-formed pro-IL-1β (inflammasome) or de novo synthesis (TLR-driven), and should be reconciled.
- The proteomics annotation of "streptolysin O precursor" in ΔSLO-infected cytosol is a red flag — either the annotation is wrong (Ifs/Nga from the same operon, as the authors speculate) or the strain is not clean; this should be resolved by checking the specific peptides identified before relying on the annotation.
- The CA-074 methyl ester experiment has no positive control showing the inhibitor actually blocked cathepsin B in this system; without it, the negative result is uninterpretable.
- No dose-response is shown for the IL-1β transfer assay — a single 20 µg dose cannot distinguish a specific protein effect from a threshold/contaminant effect.
- The cathepsin B Western blot negative result sits at the 30 kD MWCO boundary and cannot support the conclusion that cathepsin B is absent from the fractions; the proteomics identification is the better evidence and should be the cited one.
- A cytosol-from-heat-killed-GAS-infected-cells control would isolate "bacterial proteins present but no permeabilization" from "permeabilization occurred," strengthening the claim that the IL-1β response is specifically due to lysosomal leakage.
