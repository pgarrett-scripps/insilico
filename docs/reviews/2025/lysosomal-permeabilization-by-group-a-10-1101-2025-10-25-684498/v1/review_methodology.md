# Methodology Reviewer

## Summary
The design is largely sound for the central claims — the cytosol isolation protocol is validated (LAMP-2/LAMP-1/Lamin A/C absence, GAPDH presence), the M1 attribution is supported by the ΔM1 control, and the histones-in-cytosol finding is corroborated by Western blot and by the GBS negative control. The main design gap is that the IL-1β readout is used as a proxy for 'cytosolic release of phagosomal proteins' without a direct measurement that the released proteins are the active species, and the heat-killed control does not fully isolate the variable of interest.

## Strengths
- The cytosol isolation protocol is validated with multiple organellar markers (LAMP-2, LAMP-1, Lamin A/C) and a cytosolic marker (GAPDH), and the proteomics cross-reference against nuclear matrix GO terms supports purity.
- The ΔM1 isogenic mutant provides a clean genetic control that isolates M1 as the active species in the IL-1β response, and the GBS infection provides a useful negative control for the histone finding.
- The use of both WT and ΔSLO strains, plus heat-killed bacteria, gives a graded set of conditions that partially separates SLO-mediated from CD63/LAMP-3-mediated permeabilization.

## Weaknesses
- HARD: The IL-1β readout is used as a proxy for 'cytosolic release of phagosomal proteins', but the design does not directly measure that the released proteins are the active species. The cytosolic fractions contain a complex mixture of host and bacterial proteins, and the IL-1β response could be driven by any component — including the bacterial proteins themselves (SLO, SpeB, M1) or host danger signals — rather than by the specific lysosomal contents the paper claims to have captured. The ΔM1 control shows M1 is necessary, but it does not show that the lysosomal proteins are sufficient or even contributing; the CA-074 experiment addresses cathepsin B but not the other lysosomal enzymes identified in the proteomics.
- HARD: The heat-killed (HK) bacteria control does not fully isolate the variable of interest. HK bacteria are described as 'incapable of secreting bacterial proteins, including SLO', but heat-killing also destroys surface proteins (including M1, which the paper later shows is the key activator) and alters the phagocytosis and trafficking of the bacteria. The comparison between live and HK bacteria therefore conflates 'live vs dead' with 'SLO present vs absent', and the conclusion that 'infection with live GAS caused an IL-1β response regardless of SLO expression' is not fully supported by this control.
- SOFT: The claim that histones are released into the cytosol 'in the absence of DNA' rests on a Qubit dsDNA measurement that is not calibrated to the detection limit of the assay for the sample matrix. The authors note 'a low amount of DNA was detectable' but do not report the assay's limit of detection or whether the Qubit could detect the amount of DNA that would accompany the histone levels observed by Western blot. The absence of a positive control (e.g., spiked DNA in the same buffer) in the reported data makes it unclear whether the 'no significant difference' is a true negative or a floor effect.
- SOFT: The conclusion that 'GAS infection causes histones to be present in the cytosol' is supported by Western blot and proteomics, but the design does not distinguish between histones released from the nucleus (via nuclear envelope damage) and histones that were already cytosolic or released from other compartments. The authors acknowledge this ('the origin and mechanism of histone release in GAS infection remain to be determined'), which is honest, but the claim as stated in the abstract ('releases proteins into the macrophage cytosol') implies a specific mechanism that the design does not establish.
- SOFT: The DSLO mutant is described as causing 'phagolysosomal permeabilization' via CD63/LAMP-3, but the design does not include a control that isolates this pathway — e.g., a CD63/LAMP-3 knockdown or a mutant that lacks both SLO and the CD63/LAMP-3 mechanism. Without such a control, the claim that DSLO-mediated permeabilization is the cause of the observed protein release in DSLO-infected cells is inferred rather than demonstrated.
- SOFT: The proteomics analysis identified proteins 'exclusively in DSLO-infected cells' including complement proteins and immunoglobulins, but the design does not control for the possibility that these are serum-derived contaminants from the opsonization step (the bacteria are opsonized with pooled normal human serum before infection). The authors note this for immunoglobulins but not for the complement proteins.

## Questions
- What is the limit of detection of the Qubit dsDNA assay in the cytosolic fraction matrix, and would it detect the amount of DNA that would accompany the histone levels observed by Western blot?
- Could the authors include a CD63/LAMP-3 knockdown or a double mutant (ΔSLO + CD63/LAMP-3 inhibition) to directly test whether CD63/LAMP-3-mediated permeabilization is responsible for the protein release in DSLO-infected cells?
- For the heat-killed control, could the authors measure M1 protein surface levels on HK vs live bacteria to confirm that the comparison isolates SLO activity rather than M1 loss?
- Could the authors test whether the IL-1β response to WT-infected cytosol is reduced when the lysosomal enzyme fraction (excluding M1 and SLO) is depleted, to directly test whether lysosomal contents contribute to the response?
