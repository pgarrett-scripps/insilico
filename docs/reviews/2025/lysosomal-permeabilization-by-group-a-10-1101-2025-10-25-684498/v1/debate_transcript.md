# Debate Transcript

## Advocate — round 1

I’ll make the case for acceptance, addressing the hardest objections head-on rather than dismissing them. The three most serious concerns are the IL-1β carry-over confound (data_analysis, methodology, rigor), the O’Neill citation error (literature), and the dead-cell contamination alternative for histones (methodology, rigor). Each is real, and each is fixable in revision rather than fatal to the conclusions.

**1. IL-1β carry-over.** The worry is that transferred pro-IL-1β, not new synthesis, causes the readout. This is a serious alternative. But a reviewer who reads the methods closely will see that cytosol is isolated in ice-cold digitonin buffer with HALT protease inhibitors (100 µM PMSF, pepstatin, 0.5 mM EDTA) and 0.1 µg/mL penicillin, then filtered and concentrated by MWCO — conditions that suppress proteolysis during isolation. The ELISAs measure accumulated IL-1β after 2–24 h in culture, a window in which carry-over pro-IL-1β would need to survive both the isolation and the recipient-cell environment without being degraded nor activating the naïve cell. The authors should add a control (cycloheximide in the recipient cells) to definitively exclude it, but the design has built-in safeguards. The key positive claim — that WT cytosol produces more IL-1β than UI cytosol — survives the carry-over objection because the difference cannot be explained by pro-IL-1β present in both: UI and WT donors are identically primed (PMA), so they contain comparable pro-IL-1β levels; the excess signal from WT cytosol reflects either more or better-processed IL-1β in the WT fraction. Even if some of that processing occurs in the recipient well, the difference still requires a GAS-specific activator present in the WT fraction. The M1-carry-over problem is thus a quantitation question, not a knockout of the claim.

**2. O’Neill citation.** This is a clear error — O’Neill 2016 argues the opposite of what it is cited for. The authors must correct this. But the error is in the introduction, not in the experimental design or conclusions. A corrected version would cite O’Neill as a competing model (GAS does escape the phagosome) and either engage with the discrepancy or note that the work here does not resolve it. Deleting the citation from that sentence is trivial. The mistake is careless but not structural.

**3. Histone release from dead cells.** The absence of Lamin A/C in the cytosol does not exclude leakage from dead cells whose nuclei are selectively permeable to small histones. This is the strongest objection in the batch. The GBS control weakens it somewhat — GBS also kills macrophages but does not produce the histone signal — but specificity to GAS does not prove active release from living cells. The authors should report viability at harvest and correlate histone signal with a live-cell permeabilization marker. But even if the histones come entirely from dead cells, the result is still novel and interesting: GAS infection uniquely renders histones extractable into a cytosolic fraction, which GBS does not. The mechanism (active release vs. dead-cell leakage) is a secondary question that the body text already hedges appropriately (“mechanism… remain to be determined”). The abstract’s “released into the cytosol” overstates it and should be softened to “present in the cytosol.”

**Strengths that go beyond ‘fixable’:** The M1 delivery mechanism is the paper’s first genuine contribution: Valderrama et al. 2017 showed recombinant M1 activates NLRP3 but left open how it reaches the cytosol during infection. This paper closes that gap by detecting M1 in the cytosol of WT-infected (and not ΔSLO-infected) cells via proteomics and then showing that ΔM1-infected cytosol elicits significantly less IL-1β. The combination — a detection claim and a functional readout using an isogenic mutant — is stronger than either alone. The proteomics itself is a useful resource (deposited, with search parameters given in unusual detail), and the histone finding, while mechanistically unresolved, is a surprising and well-controlled observation unlikely to be noise. The paper is worth publishing after revision; the corrections needed are specific and bounded.

**Key points:**
- The IL-1β carry-over confound is serious but mitigated by protease inhibitors during isolation and identical priming of donor cells; the key difference (WT vs UI cytosol) cannot be explained by pro-IL-1β present in both.
- The O’Neill citation error is careless but easily fixed (delete or re-cite as a competing view); it does not affect the experimental design or conclusions.
- The dead-cell alternative for histones is plausible but does not invalidate the observation — GAS uniquely renders histones extractable vs. GBS; the abstract should soften 'released' to 'present.'
- The genuine strength is the M1 delivery mechanism: proteomics detection + functional ΔM1 mutant confirmation, closing a gap left by Valderrama et al. 2017.
- All three hard objections are bounded and fixable in revision; the paper makes two novel contributions (M1 cytosolic delivery; histone presence) that survive the identified confounds.

## Skeptic — round 1

The advocate concedes the O'Neill citation and the dead-cell alternative, but their reframing of both papers over the real gaps. Two load-bearing problems remain unaddressed.

**1. The M1 claim is the paper's headline, and it is the least tested claim.** The advocate calls M1 delivery "the first genuine contribution," yet the functional test (Fig. 4A) compares WT cytosol to ΔM1 cytosol — a strain that lacks M1 on its surface entirely, altering phagocytosis, survival, and secreted repertoire. The reduced IL-1β could reflect lower bacterial load or weaker permeabilization, not specific loss of cytosolic M1. The proteomics detection (M1 in WT, not ΔSLO) is a single presence/absence call, unverified by targeted MS or Western blot. The advocate's own "combination of detection + functional readout" is exactly where the confound bites: the detection uses ΔSLO, the function uses ΔM1, and neither strain's phenotype (bacterial load, SLO levels) is characterized. This is FATAL to the specific attribution as written, fixable only with add-back or load controls.

**2. The advocate's carry-over rebuttal concedes the point.** They argue UI and WT donors are "identically primed (PMA), so they contain comparable pro-IL-1β." But GAS infection itself primes donor cells — WT-infected donors plausibly contain MORE pro-IL-1β than uninfected donors. That is precisely the confound, not a mitigation. The WT-vs-UI difference is fully explained by donor pro-IL-1β carried over and cleaved in the recipient well.

**3. The DSLO-elicits-IL-1β result contradicts Harder 2009 and Richter 2021, who found SLO required for NLRP3 activation.** The advocate is silent on this. The "regardless of SLO expression" framing rests on an underpowered null (n=3) the authors themselves note "always trended lower."

The paper's two novel claims — M1 delivery and DSLO activity — are precisely the ones the panel left materially untested.

**Key points:**
- M1 attribution confounds 'M1 absent from bacterium' with 'M1 absent from cytosol' — detection uses ΔSLO, function uses ΔM1, neither strain's load/SLO phenotype characterized; FATAL as written, fixable with add-back or load controls
- Advocate's carry-over rebuttal concedes the point: GAS infection itself primes donors, so WT donors plausibly have MORE pro-IL-1β than UI donors — the WT-vs-UI difference is fully explained by carry-over
- DSLO-elicits-IL-1β contradicts Harder 2009 and Richter 2021 (SLO required for NLRP3); rests on underpowered n=3 null the authors admit 'always trended lower' — unaddressed by advocate
- The two novel claims (M1 delivery, DSLO activity) are exactly the ones the panel left materially untested
