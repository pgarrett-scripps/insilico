# Related-Work & Citations Reviewer

## Summary
The citation record contains one clear HARD error — a paper that argues the opposite of what it is cited to support — and a few soft omissions that round out the picture. The manuscript's central contribution (proteomic profiling of cytosol from GAS-infected macrophages and the demonstration that M1 protein is delivered to the cytosol in an SLO-dependent manner) is novel and the authors' own prior work is credited appropriately. But the attribution error undermines a key framing claim about whether GAS does or does not escape into the macrophage cytosol, and the discussion of histone release lacks a reference to the direct prior work on GAS-mediated nuclear envelope damage.

## Strengths
- The authors cite their own foundational work (ref 16, Nishioka et al. 2024) transparently and do not inflate its scope, nor do they over-cite themselves elsewhere.
- The key mechanistic precursor — Valderrama et al. 2017 showing M1 activates NLRP3 — is correctly attributed, and the manuscript honestly identifies that the cytosolic delivery mechanism was previously unknown, setting up their contribution.

## Weaknesses
- HARD — O'Neill et al. 2016 (ref 17) is cited in support of a claim it directly contradicts. The manuscript states: "Following lysosomal permeabilization, GAS remains in lysosomes, where it neither replicates nor escapes into the cytosol, enabling persistence in a cell that normally functions to kill them (7,12,16,17)." O'Neill et al. 2016 (mBio, "Cytosolic Replication of Group A Streptococcus in Human Macrophages") presents evidence that GAS *does* escape the phagosome and replicate in the cytosol of human macrophages. That paper's central claim and the statement it is cited to support are opposites. This is a HARD attribution error. It matters for the paper's novelty: if the reader believes GAS can reach the cytosol (as O'Neill argues), then finding GAS proteins in the cytosol is less surprising and the SLO-pore mechanism is not the only route. The authors must either correct the citation, or if they dispute O'Neill's findings, state that disagreement explicitly and provide evidence. Resolution needed: cite O'Neill as a competing view, not as support for the claim that GAS stays in lysosomes; or remove the reference from that sentence and engage with it elsewhere as prior work that conflicts with their model.
- SOFT — The claim that histones are present in the cytosol "in the absence of DNA" is not well-situated against prior work on GAS-mediated nuclear damage. The manuscript notes that GAS induces NETs and that extracellular histones are inflammatory (refs 45–46, 49–50), but does not cite work showing that GAS or its toxins (SLO in particular) can directly damage the nuclear envelope, potentially releasing histones without DNA (e.g., via gasdermin D pores or SLO itself). The discussion speculates about this mechanism but does not anchor it in the relevant literature on SLO-mediated nuclear envelope permeabilization or GAS-induced pyroptosis pathways that involve nuclear rupture. This is a SOFT completeness issue — the finding is still interesting, but situating it against known mechanisms of nuclear damage in GAS infection would strengthen the claim of novelty.

## Questions
- None beyond the resolutions specified above — the HARD issue (O'Neill citation) and the two SOFT verification requests (refs 23, 7) cover what needs fixing.
