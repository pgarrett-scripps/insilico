# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
The central claims — that GAS permeabilization releases lysosomal and bacterial proteins into the macrophage cytosol, that M1 protein released this way drives the IL-1β response in WT-infected cells, and that histones appear in the cytosol of GAS-infected cells — are largely supported by the data, and the body text is appropriately hedged in most places. The weaknesses are concentrated in the abstract, which states several claims more strongly than the Results license, and in one negative control that is missing. These are fixable with requalification and a single control experiment; the work itself is sound and worth publishing after revision.

## Strengths
- The ΔM1 comparison (Fig. 4A) is a well-designed test that isolates M1's contribution to the IL-1β response rather than relying on correlation.
- The cytosolic isolation protocol is validated by the absence of organellar markers (LAMP-2, Lamin A/C), giving confidence that the proteomic findings are not contamination artifacts.
- The body text consistently hedges the histone and SpeB claims ("suggesting," "hypothesize," "may explain"), showing appropriate restraint where the mechanism is not directly tested.

## Weaknesses
- Load-bearing claim 1 — M1 protein released into the cytosol drives the IL-1β response. The evidence (M1 detected in WT but not ΔSLO cytosol; ΔM1 cytosol elicits significantly less IL-1β than WT cytosol) supports the claim in the body. However, the abstract states that M1 "corresponded with the IL-1β response," which is over-strong: Fig. 2B shows WT and ΔSLO infections elicit IL-1β responses that are *not significantly different*, and ΔSLO cytosol (which lacks M1) also elicits a strong response (Fig. 2C). M1 therefore does not correspond with the IL-1β response in general — only with the specific WT-vs-ΔM1 cytosol difference. Similarly, the abstract's "indicating SLO-mediated release of M1 protein" is stronger than the body's own "likely due to the different-sized pores" (line ~365). The alternative — that ΔSLO bacteria express less M1 or that the smaller CD63/LAMP-3 pores retain M1 for a different reason — is not excluded. Requalify the abstract: "consistent with SLO-mediated release" and "accounts for the majority of the response to WT-infected cytosol," not a general correspondence.
- Load-bearing claim 2 — histones are released into the cytosol during GAS infection. The Western blot (Fig. 5A) and proteomics support that histones are present in the >30 kD cytosolic fraction of infected but not uninfected cells. But the claim that they are "released into the cytosol" (abstract) carries a mechanistic implication the data do not exclude. GAS causes SLO-mediated macrophage apoptosis (ref 26); in a dead or dying cell, the nuclear membrane is compromised, and histones could leak into the digitonin-released "cytosol" fraction. The absence of Lamin A/C argues against wholesale nuclear contamination but does not distinguish active release from intact nuclei against release from cells that died during the 1-hour infection — a dead cell's nucleus could release abundant histones while other nuclear proteins remain below detection. The body's hedging ("hypothesize," "mechanism... remain to be determined") is appropriate; the abstract's "released into the cytosol during GAS infection" is not. Either requalify to "histones are present in the cytosol" or add a cell-viability/cell-death control (e.g., LDH or propidium iodide staining at the 1-hour time point) to show the histones come from live cells.
- Fig. 4B: the CA-074 negative result has no positive control; without evidence that CA-074 actually inhibits cathepsin B in this system, the conclusion "cathepsin B does not contribute" is uninterpretable — show the inhibitor blocks a known cathepsin B-dependent readout, or drop the claim.
- Abstract says "all histone proteins"; the body says "almost all" (line ~354) — align the wording.
- The IL-1β assay applies donor-cell cytosol to recipient cells; if WT-infected donor cells contain more pro-IL-1β (primed by infection) than uninfected donors, the apparent response could be partly donor-derived pro-IL-1β processed in the recipient — this is a design confound for the data_analysis reviewer to weigh.
- "Strong" IL-1β response (line ~310): the WT-cytosol response is roughly 2–4× the uninfected control (Figs. 1C, 2C) — "strong" overstates a modest effect size.
- The exosome interpretation ("GAS infection significantly alters exosome/extracellular vesicles," line ~360) is speculative; exosome annotations are broad and overlap with cytosolic proteins — requalify.
- Cathepsin B is "not detectable by Western blot" (Supp. Fig. 3) yet identified by proteomics; the explanation ("close to the molecular weight cut-off") is questionable since mature cathepsin B is ~30 kD, at the cutoff — clarify the discrepancy.

## Questions
- What is the cell viability at the 1-hour infection time point, and does the cytosolic histone signal track with the fraction of dead cells?
- Is there a positive control for CA-074 activity in this system (e.g., inhibition of a known cathepsin B-dependent IL-1β stimulus)?
- Does the ΔM1 strain express WT levels of SLO and other secreted virulence factors, so the reduced IL-1β response is attributable to M1 rather than a pleiotropic effect of the mutation?
