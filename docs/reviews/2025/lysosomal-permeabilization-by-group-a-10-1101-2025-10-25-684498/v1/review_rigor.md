# Rigor & Overclaiming Reviewer

## Summary
The paper's central claims — that GAS infection releases lysosomal and bacterial proteins into the cytosol and that M1 protein drives the IL-1β response — are supported by the data, but several load-bearing claims outrun their evidence. The title's 'releases proteins into the macrophage cytosol' is fine, but the abstract's 'all histone proteins' and the claim that histones are released 'in the absence of DNA' are over-strong given the data. The M1 mechanism claim is the strongest part, though the DSLO IL-1β response is left mechanistically unexplained despite being a headline finding.

## Strengths
- The M1 protein claim is well-supported: the ΔM1 mutant specifically reduces the IL-1β response in cytosolic fractions, providing a direct test of the mechanism.
- The use of both WT and ΔSLO strains, plus heat-killed and GBS controls, gives a reasonable comparison set for isolating SLO-specific effects.
- The proteomic analysis is careful to exclude organellar contamination (LAMP-2, Lamin A/C, nuclear matrix proteins), strengthening the claim that detected proteins are genuinely cytosolic.

## Weaknesses
- HARD: The abstract claims 'all histone proteins' are released into the cytosol, but the data show only 'almost all' histone subunits (Fig. 3D, Supp. Table 2), and the presence of individual histones does not establish that full nucleosomes are released — the 30kD MWCO only rules out free monomers, not partial complexes. The claim that histones are present 'in the absence of DNA' (Fig. 5C) is also over-strong: the DNA quantification shows no significant difference, but the assay cannot rule out low-level DNA below detection, and the mechanism of histone release is explicitly unknown.
- HARD: The DSLO-infected cytosol elicits a strong IL-1β response (Fig. 2C), but the paper does not identify what drives it. The authors suggest SpeB, but this is not tested — no SpeB mutant or inhibitor experiment is shown. This is a load-bearing finding because it is the basis for the claim that GAS-induced permeabilization causes IL-1β 'regardless of whether permeabilization is mediated by SLO or by other cellular mechanisms.'
- SOFT: The claim that 'M1 protein released from WT GAS-permeabilized phagosomes accounts for the majority of the inflammasome activation' (Fig. 4A) is supported for the cytosolic fraction, but the paper does not show that M1 is the dominant driver in intact infected cells — the live-infection IL-1β response (Fig. 2B) is not dissected by strain in the same way.
- SOFT: The claim that 'GAS infection causes histones to be present in the cytosol in the absence of DNA' (Fig. 5C) is presented as a conclusion, but the mechanism is unknown and the DNA assay may not be sensitive enough to detect low-level DNA release. The authors acknowledge this, but the abstract states it more definitively than the data support.

## Questions
- What drives the IL-1β response in ΔSLO-infected cytosol? Is SpeB the mediator, and can this be tested with a SpeB-deficient mutant or inhibitor?
- Can the histone release be confirmed with a nucleosome-specific assay (e.g., detecting H3-H4 tetramers or H2A-H2B dimers) rather than individual subunits?
- Is the DNA quantification sensitive enough to rule out low-level DNA release below the detection limit of the Qubit assay?
